                          <h2>Key findings</h2>

<ul>
	<li>Bitsight has recently observed a 15-year-old modular spambot called Tofsee being distributed by PrivateLoader (ruzki), a notorious malware distribution service we also closely monitor.&nbsp;</li>
	<li>Bitsight has noticed Tofsee engaging in web traffic proxying, with a small percentage of it being email spam related traffic, and also performing cryptocurrency mining.</li>
	<li>Bitsight's partial visibility over its botnet of infected machines suggests that its spread worldwide, with a significant percentage of infections in India.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Old bot, new tricks (not really)</h2>

<p>In January 2023, <a href="https://www.bitsight.com/blog/tracking-privateloader-malware-distribution-service">PrivateLoader</a>, a malware loader from a pay-per-install malware distribution service called “<a href="https://blog.sekoia.io/privateloader-the-loader-of-the-prevalent-ruzki-ppi-service/#h-ruzki-a-popular-pay-per-install-service">ruzki</a>”, started to <a href="https://urlhaus.abuse.ch/url/2513471/">distribute Tofsee</a> (a.k.a. Gheg), a modular spambot. Spambots are typically utilized by cybercriminals to spread malware and phishing emails, and this&nbsp;particular one has been <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20090428005953/http://www.marshal8e6.com/trace/i/Gheg,spambot.897~.asp">in operation since at least 2008</a>. Due to its modular architecture, Tofsee is capable of performing a wide range of tasks once it receives instructions to do so (as it did in the past), such as <a href="https://www.f5.com/company/blog/f5-distributed-cloud-services-stands-up-to-l7-ddos-attacks">denial of service attacks</a> and <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/tofsee-spam/">click fraud</a>. The samples are packed but can be easily <a href="https://www.unpac.me/results/f70671d9-2ffe-4d3f-b992-86bb7aa3c3a5/#/">unpacked</a>. Unpacking denotes the last stage in which the main functionality of the malicious software is exposed. Threat Actors make use of packers when distributing their malware as they remain an effective way to evade detection.</p>

<p>As <a href="https://cert.pl/en/posts/2016/09/tofsee-en/">revealed by CERT.pl</a>, the malware downloads two types of resources (updates) from its command-and-control (C2) server: configurations, and <a href="https://cert.pl/en/posts/2017/10/a-deeper-look-at-tofsee-modules/">plugins</a> to extend its functionality. After trying to decrypt the packet capture from a sandbox run of the sample to understand what resources have recently been fetched, we were getting high entropy data, signaling that something on the protocol may have changed. One of the first guesses was that the hardcoded 7-byte-lowercase-only-letters encryption key “<strong>abcdefg</strong>” might have changed.</p>

<p>To understand if that was the case, we tried to search for the key on the binary, but couldn’t find it. Going deeper, statically analyzing the sample using a disassembler, right on the main function, one of the first functions called (Fig. 1) looks like a string decryption function and is called 67 times throughout the code. After implementing it in python and testing one of the calls to it, a plaintext string is indeed returned.</p>

<h2>Proxying web traffic</h2>

<p>Regarding the proxy plugin, we extracted a configuration payload (Code 1) with 6 IPs located in Russia. Looking at the same packet capture previously mentioned, after trying to decrypt the TCP streams related to those IPs, we were again getting high entropy data. Looking at the proxy plugin DLL, there is yet another 7-byte-lowercase-only-letters string, “prcbsrv”. After decrypting the packets with it, the streams revealed HTTP(S) and SOCKS(4/5) requests sent from those IPs to the bot, which leads us to believe those are addresses of backconnect servers. A backconnect proxy server is a server that utilizes a pool of proxies (in this case, the Tofsee botnet) to perform requests on behalf of the user.</p>

<p>While looking through the traffic, we spotted an interesting pattern. Around <strong>3%</strong> of the requests were HTTP POST with the URI ending in “<strong>.php</strong>” and, in many cases, starting with “<strong>/wp-</strong>”, to random websites that appear legitimate. Each request’s payload starts with the string “<strong>ce=</strong>” followed by a base64-encoded spam template (similar to Code 2). These indicators lead us to believe that these (apparently) legitimate websites have been likely compromised to be used to distribute spam.</p>

<h2>Wrap-up</h2>

<p>Tofsee remains a persistent threat to organizations worldwide, with its primary focus recently being the proxying of web traffic and cryptocurrency mining. However, its modular design also allows for it to be used for a variety of other malicious activities, including spam campaigns and distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks, as seen in the past. As such, it is crucial for organizations to remain vigilant in their cybersecurity efforts and take steps to mitigate the risk of Tofsee infection. Bitsight will continue to monitor the threat landscape closely and provide updates on new developments related to Tofsee and other emerging threats.</p>
