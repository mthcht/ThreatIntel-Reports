                            <h1 class="header__title title-h2 mb-24 mt-md-48 mt-lg-64 mt-xl-64">Godfather: A banking Trojan that is impossible to refuse</h1><p class="header__text mb-xs-48 mb-64 mb-lg-96 mb-xl-96">Group-IB discovers banking Trojan targeting users of more than 400 apps in 16 countries</p><div class="post-info__date mb-24 mb-lg-32 mb-xl-48"><div class="body2">December 21, 2022 · <span id="reading-time"></span> min to read · Malware Analysis</div></div><div class="post-info__image mb-24 mb-lg-32 mb-xl-48"><picture><source srcset="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/img-1440-16.jpg.webp" type="image/webp"><img src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/img-1440-16.jpg" alt="" class="webpexpress-processed"></picture></div><div class="post-info__tags"><div class="post-info__tag body2">Banking</div><div class="post-info__tag body2">Godfather</div><div class="post-info__tag body2">Trojan</div></div>                        </div>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="blog-post-content" id="blog-post-content">
                    <div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>The <strong>Android banking Trojan Godfather is currently being utilized by cybercriminals to attack users of popular financial services across the globe</strong>. Godfather is designed to allow threat actors to harvest login credentials for banking applications and other financial services, and drain the accounts. To date, its victims include users of over 400 international targets, including banking applications, cryptocurrency wallets, and crypto exchanges.</p>
<p>Few people realize that hiding under Godfather’s hood is an old banking Trojan called Anubis, whose functionality has become outdated due to Android updates and the efforts of malware detection and prevention providers. In this blog post, <strong>Group-IB’s <a href="/products/threat-intelligence/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Threat Intelligence</a> team describes in detail who Godfather attacks</strong>, how it does it, and what this banking Trojan inherited from its predecessor.</p>
<p>Group-IB first detected <strong>Godfather, a mobile banking Trojan</strong> that steals the banking and cryptocurrency exchange credentials of users, in June 2021. Almost a year later, in March 2022, <a href="https://twitter.com/ThreatFabric/status/1505932079401480198)" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">researchers at Threat Fabric</a> were the first to mention the banking Trojan publicly. A few months later, in June, the Trojan stopped being circulated. One of the reasons, Group-IB analysts believe, why Godfather was taken out of use was for developers to update the Trojan further. Sure enough, Godfather reappeared in September 2022, now with slightly modified WebSocket functionality.</p>
<h2>Key Findings</h2>
<ol>
<li>Group-IB’s Threat Intelligence detected <strong>more than 400 international financial companies targeted by the Godfather Android banking Trojan</strong> between June 2021 and October 2022.</li>
<li>Half of the targeted financial companies were banks. Cryptocurrency wallets and exchanges were also targeted.</li>
<li>Godfather’s targets include <strong>49 US-based companies</strong>, <strong>31 Turkish-based companies</strong>, and <strong>30 Spanish-based companies</strong>. Financial services providers in <strong>Canada, France, Germany, UK, Italy, and Poland</strong> were also among the most affected.</li>
<li>Godfather’s predecessor is another banking Trojan named Anubis.</li>
<li>Godfather’s developers used Anubis source code as a basis and modernized it for newer versions of Android, adding relevant features and removing others such as file encryption.</li>
<li>Godfather overlays web fakes on infected devices that appear when a user interacts with a decoy notification or tries to open one of the legitimate applications targeted by Godfather.</li>
<li>Any data, such as usernames and passwords, entered on the web fakes are harvested by the threat actors. Godfather can also exfiltrate SMS and push notifications to bypass two-factor authentication.</li>
<li>According to Telegram channels analyzed by Group-IB, Godfather is being distributed via the Malware-as-a-Service model.</li>
<li>Based on Godfather&#8217;s network infrastructure, this banking Trojan is distributed through decoy applications hosted on Google Play.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Godfather’s international targets</h2>
<p>To date, <strong>215 international banks, 94 cryptocurrency wallets and 110 crypto exchange platforms</strong> have fallen victim to Godfather, as of October 2022. Most of the targeted companies are located in the United States, Turkey, Spain, Canada, Germany, France, and the UK. Interestingly, Godfather spares users in post-Soviet countries. If the potential victim’s system preferences include one of the languages in that region, the Trojan shuts down. This could suggest that Godfather’s developers are Russian speakers.</p>
<div id="attachment_20947" style="width: 1610px" class="wp-caption alignnone"><img aria-describedby="caption-attachment-20947" decoding="async" class="size-full wp-image-20947" src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/1-1.webp" alt="Who and where Godfather targets" width="1600" height="892" /><p id="caption-attachment-20947" class="wp-caption-text">Figure 1: Who and where Godfather targets</p></div>
<p><strong>A signature technique found in the functionality of any Android banking Trojan is the use of web fakes</strong> (HTML pages created by the threat actors that are shown over legitimate applications), and Godfather has these in abundance. The fake pages that Godfather can overlay on infected devices appear after users click on decoy notifications or open legitimate apps targeted by Godfather. All data entered into the fake web pages (such as usernames and passwords) is exfiltrated to command-and-control (C&amp;C) servers. Group-IB researchers were able to analyze some of the web fakes affecting Turkish banks, likely produced by a threat actor explicitly targeting Turkish companies, and these are provided below.</p>
</div>

<p><strong>Gone but not forgotten: Anubis, we recognize you!</strong></p>
<p><strong>The basis of Godfather is a version of the banking Trojan called Anubis</strong>, whose source code was <a href="https://www.threatfabric.com/blogs/anubis_2_malware_and_afterlife" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">leaked</a> as early as 2019. As new versions of Android were released, and malware detection and prevention providers got up to speed, many Anubis features stopped working and were thrown into the dustbin of history. <strong>But why create a new Trojan if one has been developed already?</strong></p>
<p><strong>The developers of Godfather used Anubis source code as a basis</strong> and modernized it for newer versions of Android, adding relevant features and removing others such as file encryption.</p>
<p>We found that both Trojans, Anubis and Godfather, have the same code base, but the C&amp;C communication protocol and capabilities, together with their implementation, were modified in Godfather. The latter can therefore be considered an Anubis fork.</p>

<h2>Godfather malware responsibilities</h2>
<p><picture><source srcset="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/9-min.jpg.webp" type="image/webp"><img decoding="async" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-20955 webpexpress-processed" src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/9-min.jpg" alt="" width="1200" height="935"></picture></p>
<p>The analyzed Godfather samples, one of which was uploaded to <a href="https://blog.virustotal.com/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">VirusTotal</a> in September 2022, emulate <a href="https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Google Protect</a>. After a user launches the malware, it emulates the legitimate Google application. An animation shows Google Protect “activity”, but the “scanner” does not actually do anything and instead Godfather’s criminal roots become apparent. After being launched, the malware achieves persistence on the infected device, creates a pinned notification, and hides its icon from the list of installed applications.</p>
<div id="attachment_20956" style="width: 258px" class="wp-caption alignnone"><img aria-describedby="caption-attachment-20956" decoding="async" class="size-full wp-image-20956" src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/10.gif" alt="Google Protect animation" width="248" height="409" /><p id="caption-attachment-20956" class="wp-caption-text">Figure 7: Google Protect animation</p></div>
<p>Initialization also involves launching a service for requesting access to AccessibilityService, which is an Android feature used by developers to adapt their applications to users with disabilities. Recently, AccessibilityService has been actively used to carry out ATS (Automated Transfer System) attacks. Access to AccessibilityService is also requested when the “Scan” button is pressed. It is worth noting that users cannot launch the “scanning” function without granting the Trojan access to AccessibilityService.</p>
<p>Of course, no scanning takes place. The scanning animation is displayed for 30 seconds, after which a message appears saying that no malicious applications were found. With access to AccessibilityService, Godfather issues itself the necessary permissions and starts communicating with the C&amp;C server.</p>
<p><strong>The user assumes that there are no Trojans on the device and launches their banking application</strong>, entering their login details and not realizing that their data just got into the hands of threat actors.</p>
<p>The user eventually discovers that the money from their account is gone. They might try withdrawing the permissions or deleting the application, but the settings will keep collapsing and the device will keep returning to the home screen.</p>
<p><strong>How has the mafia boss managed to pull off such a heist? Let’s look into this.</strong></p>
<h3>Technical findings</h3>
<p>When launched for the first time, Godfather does the following:</p>
<ul>
<li>Checks system language and context</li>
<li>Initializes SharedPreferences parameters</li>
<li>Launches a service to request access to AccessibilityService</li>
<li>Launches a service to communicate with the C&amp;C server</li>
<li>Hides its icon in the menu</li>
</ul>
<p>Let’s now look at some of the points above in more detail.</p>
<h3>Checking system language and context</h3>
<p><strong>Godfather checks the system language, and the Trojan shuts down if the language is one of the following</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>RU (Russia)</li>
<li>AZ (Azerbaijan)</li>
<li>AM (Armenia)</li>
<li>BY (Belarus)</li>
<li>KZ (Kazakhstan)</li>
<li>KG (Kyrgyzstan)</li>
<li>MD (Moldova)</li>
<li>UZ (Uzbekistan)</li>
<li>TJ (Tajikistan)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>It also checks for the device context in order to determine whether the Trojan was launched in an emulator. If so, Godfather stops functioning.</strong></p>
<h3>Initialization of SharedPreferences parameters</h3>
<p>Godfather uses <a href="https://developer.android.com/reference/android/content/SharedPreferences" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">SharedPreferences</a> to store necessary settings such as the C&amp;C server, the status of required permissions, the list of targets, and log data. The configuration file is called <strong>config.xml</strong>. Parameters for storing VNC settings (settings_port, settings_password) are saved to the default settings file. This stage also involves generating a unique identifier (the relevant field in the traffic is called <strong>key</strong>) which is used for identifying infected devices — this is essentially a bot ID. The generation algorithm involves choosing 15 random characters from the Latin alphabet and numbers from 0 to 9:<strong> ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ1234567890</strong>.</p>
<h3>A service for requesting access to AccessibilityService</h3>
<p><strong>The service checks whether the malware has been granted access to AccessibilityService</strong>. If it has not, the service requests access and creates a pop-up notification with the following text: <em>“Enable accessibility for protection to take effect ‘Google Protect”</em>. This will happen every eight seconds until the user grants the malicious application access to AccessibilityService.</p>
<p>In addition to being launched upon initialization, the service is launched when the victim device is restarted, turned on, or unlocked. You can find more information about what access to AccessibilityService is used for in the <em>“AccessibilityService”</em> section of this article.</p>
<h3>A service for communicating with the C&amp;C server</h3>
<p><strong>This service creates a task that is executed every 70 seconds</strong>. The service is launched when the device is turned on, restarted, or unlocked. Before obtaining the C&amp;C server and communicating with it, <strong>the Trojan checks for the necessary permissions</strong> (writing to external storage, reading contacts, reading the device status, and making calls). If the Trojan does not have these permissions, it does the following:</p>
<ul>
<li>Requests the necessary permissions</li>
<li>Exfiltrates contacts</li>
<li>Exfiltrates the list of installed applications</li>
</ul>
<p>In addition,<strong> the Trojan checks for administrator privileges</strong>. If they are not present, the malware requests them.</p>
<p>Next, the Trojan communicates with the C&amp;C server to receive commands and turn on its modules. Network requests, as well as commands that can be executed as a result of these requests, are described in the <em>“Network communication”</em> section of this article.</p>
<h3>Trojan Godfather network communication</h3>
<div id="attachment_20957" style="width: 1610px" class="wp-caption alignnone"><img aria-describedby="caption-attachment-20957" decoding="async" class="size-full wp-image-20957" src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/11-1.webp" alt="Communication between Godfather and C&amp;C addresses" width="1600" height="892" /><p id="caption-attachment-20957" class="wp-caption-text">Figure 8: Communication between Godfather and C&amp;C addresses</p></div>
<p><strong>The figure above shows Godfather’s communication with a C&amp;C address</strong>, which can be broken down into three parts:</p>
<ol>
<li>Receiving an up-to-date C&amp;C address</li>
<li>Receiving general commands (<em>RatCommandRequest</em>)</li>
<li>Receiving commands for launching malware modules (<em>InjectCommandRequest</em>)</li>
</ol>
<p>It is worth noting that <em>RatCommandRequest</em> and <em>InjectCommandRequest</em> commands are requested simultaneously.</p>
<p>Godfather does not have an up-to-date list of C&amp;C addresses. Instead, it receives them from Telegram channel descriptions by executing an HTTP request (the <em>GetC2Request</em> in the figure above). An example of a Telegram channel with an encrypted C&amp;C address is shown below. The up-to-date C&amp;C address is encrypted using <em>Blowfish (ECB mode)</em>, where the key is the string <em>ABC</em>. The same algorithm is used to decrypt received commands, which the application requests from the C&amp;C server immediately after receiving the C&amp;C address. As mentioned earlier in the article, there are two modules for receiving commands: regular commands and commands for enabling and disabling the Trojan modules (<em>RatCommandRequest</em> and <em>InjectCommandRequest</em> in the figure above).</p>
<div id="attachment_20958" style="width: 398px" class="wp-caption alignnone"><img aria-describedby="caption-attachment-20958" decoding="async" class="size-full wp-image-20958" src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/12-1.webp" alt="Example of Telegram channel with encrypted C&amp;C address" width="388" height="532" /><p id="caption-attachment-20958" class="wp-caption-text">Figure 9: Example of Telegram channel with encrypted C&amp;C address</p></div>
<h3>RatCommandRequest</h3>
<p>This request is used for receiving commands and a list of victim applications. Request parameters contain the following information about the device:</p>
<ul>
<li>Name of the network operator</li>
<li>Phone status (locked or unlocked)</li>
<li>Whether AccessibilityService permissions have been granted</li>
<li>Whether the Trojan’s service for handling SMS messages has been installed</li>
<li>Whether the necessary permissions have been granted (writing to external storage, reading contacts, reading the device status, and making calls)</li>
<li>The default user-agent for the device</li>
<li>Whether the device is being charged (the new version does not have this feature)</li>
<li>The country code of the current network operator</li>
<li>Bot ID (the key parameter)</li>
<li>Whether the screen is currently being recorded</li>
<li>List of installed applications</li>
<li>Android version</li>
<li>Device model</li>
</ul>
<p>Example of a request body:</p>

<h3>Proxy module</h3>
<p>On infected devices, <strong>Godfather can launch a backconnect proxy server</strong>, for which port 34500 is used (located in the body of the Trojan). <strong>The Trojan opens and configures this port for handling the SOCKS5 protocol</strong>. Parameters (host, user, pass, port) necessary for implementing a proxy are received together with a command for launching a proxy (<em>startsocks5</em>).</p>
<h3>Godfather Trojan VNC module</h3>
<p>Godfather implements VNC functionality using two native libraries, which are stored in the Trojan’s resources in encrypted form. The encryption algorithm is <em>AES (ECB mode)</em>, where the key is the string <em>GWy8tfjp4mXpu58fCRpWCLxqHV8YmeHR</em>. The libraries are stored in the Trojan’s resources with the following naming convention: <em>lib.%ARCHITECTURE%.godfat.so, lib.%ARCHITECTURE%.vncserver.so</em>. It is worth noting that in the new version of the Trojan these libraries are stored in unencrypted form and have the following names: <em>libspotify.so, libjson.so</em>.</p>
<p><em>lib.%ARCHITECTURE%.vncserver.so</em> is an open-source library used for implementing VNC connections. The code of this library is <a href="https://github.com/LibVNC/libvncserver" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">available at GitHub</a>.</p>
<p>The library <em>lib.%ARCHITECTURE%.godfat.so</em> is a JNI (Java Native Interface) wrapper that is required for the abovementioned Unix library to function correctly.</p>
<p>Transferring control via VNC occurs in two stages:</p>
<ol>
<li>The C&amp;C server sends a command to start the VNC module.</li>
<li>The C&amp;C server sends an address to which the infected device will connect and transfer VNC control (establish a reverse VNC connection)</li>
</ol>
<p>When the command to launch a VNC server is received, no parameters from the server are processed. Below are static parameters that are used when the server is launched:</p>
<ul>
<li>port: the value of the SharedPreferences<em> settings_port</em> parameter (by default the value is 5900)</li>
<li>user: the value of the system settings <em>bluetooth_name</em> parameter</li>
<li>pass: the value of the SharedPreferences <em>settings_password</em> parameter (by default the value is 123).</li>
</ul>
<p>When the command for establishing a reverse VNC connection is processed, the “host” parameter to which the reverse connection will be established is transferred. The port for the connection is contained in the Trojan and has the value 5500 (the default port for the reverse VNC connection).</p>

<h3>ScreenCapture module</h3>
<p>This module is used for recording the screen of an infected device and then exfiltrating the recording to the C&amp;C server. The module makes it possible to steal data entered by the user in legitimate applications as well as any information contained in an application that Godfather targets. This module is launched in one of two cases:</p>
<ul>
<li>A relevant command has been received from the server</li>
<li>An application from the target list has been launched (the malware should receive this list in advance)</li>
</ul>
<p>It is worth mentioning that some of the package names from the list were intentionally altered (the character 1 was added to the package names) to prevent the module from being launched. It is possible that the threat actors have not yet decided what they will do with the data they receive or they simply lost interest in these applications during the testing stage. The screen is recorded using standard Android classes:</p>
<ul>
<li>android.media.projection.MediaProjectionManager</li>
<li>android.media.projection.MediaProjection</li>
</ul>
<p>The screen recording is saved to a file whose path is created according to the following template:</p>
<p><strong>%DEFAULT_DIRECTORY_MOVIES%/%KEY%_%CURRENT_TIME_MS%.mp4</strong></p>
<p>The screen is recorded for two minutes. The resulting file is sent to the C&amp;C server. The URL for sending the file is created according to the following template:</p>
<p><strong>%DECRYPTED_C2%/mp4_recorder.php</strong></p>
<p>Below is the format of the request body:</p>
</div>

<h3>WebSocket module</h3>
<p>This module was added to the updated September 2022 version of the banking Trojan. The module makes it possible to use a persistent WebSocket connection to control an infected device. At the moment, <strong>Godfather processes three types of messages</strong> from the server:</p>
<ul>
<li>Perform the action “Back”</li>
<li>Execute clicks</li>
<li>Enter text into fields</li>
</ul>
<p>The address of the server to which the connection will be established must first be received from the C&amp;C server as an option in the request <em>InjectCommandRequest</em>.</p>
<h3>Web-fake module</h3>
<p>Like Anubis, <strong>Godfather has a module for downloading and displaying web fakes</strong>. The method of exfiltrating data from a web fake to the C&amp;C server in Godfather is the same as in Anubis.</p>
<p>Web fakes can be downloaded in two cases: if the user has followed the decoy push notification or if an application in the target list has been opened.</p>
<p>In the case of a fake push notification, a notification icon will be downloaded in addition to the web fake. The icon is located at a URL in the following format:</p>
<p><em>%DECRYPTED_C2%/icon/%PACKAGE_NAME%.png</em></p>
<p><em>%PACKAGE_NAME%</em> is the package name for the application that the fake push notification mimics. The URL for downloading web fakes is formed according to the following template:</p>
<p><em>%DECRYPTED_C2%/itor/fafa.php?f=%PACKAGE_NAME%&amp;p=%KEY%|%LOCALE%</em></p>
<p><em>%KEY%</em> is the key sent as a parameter in the requests mentioned above. <em>%LOCALE%</em> is the system language. The user-agent used when the request is executed is:</p>
<p><em>Mozilla/5.0 (Linux; Android 9; SM-J730F Build/PPR12.180610.011; wv) AppleWebKit/537.36 (KHTML, like Gecko) Version/4.0 Chrome/88.0.4324.181 Mobile Safari/537.36</em></p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Sometimes the sequel really can be better than the original. <strong>The case of Godfather highlights how quickly Trojan developers can adapt their tools and stay one step ahead of their Android counterparts.</strong> Additionally, it shows how easily available source code, such as that of Anubis, can be modernized and relaunched, especially under the Malware-as-a-Service model.</p>
<p><strong>The lack of direct communication between the threat actor and victim also makes Godfather an effective malware type</strong>. Of course, Godfather shuts down on an infected device if it detects that the user is from Russia or a CIS country, but this model allows the malware to be spread across the world, given that all that is required is the creation of a web fake impersonating a bank or an e-wallet provider in a particular country.</p>
<p><strong>By imitating Google Protect, Godfather can easily go undetected on infected devices.</strong> Unwitting users believe they are being protected by an Android service, but in fact, the malicious actors gain access to their banking and financial portal accounts. While Group-IB does not have definitive data on the amount of money stolen by operators of Godfather, the methods harnessed by malicious actors are cause for concern.</p>
<h2>Recommendations on how to protect against Godfather</h2>
<p>The security of mobile applications and operating systems is improving rapidly. However, it is too early to write Android banking Trojans off completely. In our experience, <strong>banking Trojans are still highly active and threat actors widely distribute modified Trojans whose source code is publicly available</strong>. A good example of this trend is Godfather, which is damaging to not only end users of banking applications but also the entire banking sector itself.</p>

</ol>
