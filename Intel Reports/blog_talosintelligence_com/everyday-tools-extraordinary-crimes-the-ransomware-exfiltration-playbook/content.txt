    <h1 class="text-center">Everyday tools, extraordinary crimes: the ransomware exfiltration playbook</h1>

    <div class="text-center m-1">
        <div class="post-author">
            <span>By </span>
                <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/author/maria/">Maria Jose Erquiaga</a>, 
                <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/author/darin/">Darin Smith</a>
        </div>

        <br/>
        <time class="post-datetime" datetime="March 19, 2026 06:00">
            Thursday, March 19, 2026 06:00
        </time>

        <div class="m-3">
                        <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/category/the-deep-dive-with-ntdr/" class="category primary-category">
                            The Deep Dive with NTDR
                        </a>
                            </div>
    </div>

    <section class="post-content-wrapper mt-5">
        <div class="post-content" id="post-content">
            <ul><li>Data exfiltration activity increasingly&nbsp;leverages&nbsp;legitimate native utilities, commonly deployed third-party tools, and cloud service clients, reducing the effectiveness of static indicators of compromise (IOCs) and tool-based blocking strategies.&nbsp;</li><li>The <a href="https://github.com/Cisco-Talos/Xfiletrator" rel="noreferrer">Exfiltration Framework</a> systematically normalizes&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;and forensic characteristics of these tools, enabling cross-environment comparison independent of operating system, deployment model, or infrastructure domain.&nbsp;</li><li>By&nbsp;modeling&nbsp;execution context, parent-child process relationships, network communication patterns, artifact persistence, and destination characteristics, the framework exposes detection-relevant signals that&nbsp;remain&nbsp;stable even when tools are renamed,&nbsp;relocated, or&nbsp;operated&nbsp;within trusted infrastructure.&nbsp;</li><li>The analysis&nbsp;demonstrates&nbsp;that reliable detection requires correlation across endpoint, network, and cloud telemetry, with emphasis on&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;baselining, contextual anomalies, and cumulative transfer analysis rather than protocol-level or allow-list–based controls.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="background">Background</h2><p>As defenders have improved their ability to detect malicious code, attackers have adapted by reducing their reliance on bespoke implants.&nbsp;As a result,&nbsp;<a href="https://attack.mitre.org/tactics/TA0010/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>data</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://socprime.com/blog/what-is-data-exfiltration-mitre-attack/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>exfiltration</u></a>&nbsp;is no longer primarily driven by custom malware or specialized tooling.&nbsp;Instead, many modern exfiltration operations&nbsp;leverage&nbsp;legitimate, widely deployed&nbsp;<a href="https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/cybersecurity-101/cyberattacks/living-off-the-land-attack/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>utilities already present</u></a>&nbsp;in enterprise environments, along with benign cloud storage locations as the destination of the exfiltration connections.&nbsp;</p><p>This shift significantly complicates detection. Tools and services used for routine business operations can be repurposed to transfer stolen data outside the network without triggering traditional security controls. In many real-world incidents, exfiltration does not rely on novel protocols, custom command-and-control&nbsp;(C2)&nbsp;infrastructure, or overtly malicious binaries. Instead, attackers abuse trusted, allow-listed tools such as cloud command-line interfaces, file synchronization utilities, managed file transfer platforms, and legitimate file storage services. In these scenarios, the distinction between legitimate use and malicious abuse is subtle, contextual, and difficult to&nbsp;identify.&nbsp;</p><p>This research originated from a fundamental question:&nbsp;If attackers&nbsp;don’t&nbsp;require malicious software or infrastructure to exfiltrate data, what signals can defenders rely on to detect their&nbsp;behavior?&nbsp;</p><h2 id="goal">Goal&nbsp;</h2><p>The Exfiltration Framework was developed to explore this question by systematically&nbsp;analyzing&nbsp;how legitimate tools are abused for data exfiltration. While many existing frameworks&nbsp;catalog&nbsp;the misuse of legitimate tools by platform or technology domain, the Exfiltration Framework takes a cross-platform perspective and categorizes tools by exfiltration tactic rather than environment or implementation.&nbsp;</p><p>The goal of this project is&nbsp;not to&nbsp;catalog&nbsp;attack techniques or&nbsp;enumerate&nbsp;tools, but to understand&nbsp;how benign utilities are abused for data exfiltration and which telemetry defenders can realistically rely on for detection. By focusing on observable&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;rather than tool presence, this research aims to support detection strategies that&nbsp;remain&nbsp;effective even when attackers&nbsp;operate&nbsp;entirely within trusted software and&nbsp;permitted infrastructure. A secondary&nbsp;objective&nbsp;is to&nbsp;identify&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;patterns that recur across tools and can be applied more generically to detect exfiltration activity.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="the-exfiltration-framework">The Exfiltration Framework&nbsp;</h2><p>The <a href="https://github.com/Cisco-Talos/Xfiletrator" rel="noreferrer">Exfiltration Framework</a>&nbsp;is a defensive project designed to systematically document how legitimate tools are abused for data exfiltration. Early in its development, the goal was to provide a comparative overview of exfiltration-capable tools, similar in spirit to matrix-style projects that summarize capabilities at&nbsp;a high level. While useful for classification, this approach proved insufficient for capturing the&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;and forensic details&nbsp;required&nbsp;for detection and investigation.&nbsp;</p><p>As a result, the framework evolved toward a structured, feature-oriented model inspired by projects such as LOLBAS, where tool capabilities,&nbsp;behaviors, and artifacts are documented in a consistent and extensible format. This design allows exfiltration-relevant characteristics to be organized clearly and compared across tools without oversimplifying their&nbsp;behavior.&nbsp;</p><p>The framework is intentionally scoped to legitimate, widely available tools commonly present in enterprise environments. It does not&nbsp;attempt&nbsp;to&nbsp;catalog&nbsp;all&nbsp;possible exfiltration&nbsp;mechanisms, nor does it&nbsp;analyze&nbsp;custom malware, exploit-based techniques, or novel&nbsp;C2&nbsp;protocols. Instead, it concentrates on utilities routinely used for legitimate purposes that can naturally blend into normal activity, making their abuse particularly difficult to detect.</p><h3 id="design-goals-and-data-model">Design&nbsp;goals and&nbsp;data&nbsp;model&nbsp;</h3><p>The Exfiltration Framework is designed to capture&nbsp;<strong>behavioral</strong>&nbsp;<strong>and forensic characteristics</strong>&nbsp;that are directly useful for detection, investigation, and comparative analysis. Rather than documenting tool functionality or attack procedures, each tool is represented using a&nbsp;<strong>structured, normalized schema</strong>&nbsp;that emphasizes how exfiltration manifests operationally across endpoint, network, and cloud telemetry.&nbsp;</p><p>This normalization allows defenders to compare tools with&nbsp;very different&nbsp;implementations based on shared&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;characteristics, and to reason about exfiltration activity independently of the specific utility involved.&nbsp;</p><p>To reflect real-world enterprise environments, tools are grouped into three categories:&nbsp;</p><ul><li>Built-in operating system tools, available by default on endpoints&nbsp;</li><li>Commonly deployed endpoint tools, installed for operational or administrative purposes&nbsp;</li><li>Cloud-native tools, designed to interact with cloud services and storage platforms&nbsp;</li></ul><p>This categorization illustrates how exfiltration can occur across multiple layers of the environment, from endpoints to cloud infrastructure, while highlighting differing detection trade-offs.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="core-framework-fields-and-rationale">Core&nbsp;framework&nbsp;fields and&nbsp;rationale&nbsp;</h3><p>The Exfiltration Framework is designed around the premise that detecting data exfiltration&nbsp;via legitimate tools requires understanding&nbsp;how those tools behave when misused,&nbsp;rather than relying on static identifiers or tool presence alone. Each field in the framework schema was selected to capture signals that defenders can realistically&nbsp;observe&nbsp;and correlate across endpoint, network, and cloud telemetry.&nbsp;</p><p>Rather than&nbsp;attempting&nbsp;to model every&nbsp;possible abuse&nbsp;pattern, the framework focuses on a small set of fields that consistently influence detection outcomes across tools and environments.</p><ul><li><strong>Tool identity and classification</strong>&nbsp;<br>Basic metadata such as tool name, category, and supported platforms provides essential context without implying malicious intent. Classification by deployment model&nbsp;—&nbsp;built-in operating system tools, commonly deployed endpoint tools, and cloud-native tools&nbsp;—&nbsp;helps frame expected&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;and informs detection trade-offs.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>This distinction is important because tool legitimacy strongly influences both attacker&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;and defensive visibility. Built-in tools often&nbsp;benefit&nbsp;from implicit trust and extensive allow-listing, while cloud-native tools&nbsp;operate&nbsp;against shared infrastructure with limited network-level discrimination. Capturing this context allows defenders to reason about&nbsp;<em>expected</em>&nbsp;versus&nbsp;<em>anomalous</em>&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;for a given class of tool.&nbsp;</li><li><strong>Execution characteristics</strong>&nbsp;<br>Execution-related fields capture how a tool is typically invoked when abused, including execution mode (interactive, background, headless), command-line usage, and parent-child process relationships. These attributes are&nbsp;frequently&nbsp;more stable indicators of misuse than the presence of a specific binary, particularly in scenarios involving masquerading or living-off-the-land techniques.&nbsp;<br>Execution context often provides early signals of abuse, such as tools launched by unexpected parent processes, executed from atypical directories, or run in unattended modes inconsistent with normal usage. By explicitly&nbsp;modeling&nbsp;these characteristics, the framework enables detection approaches that&nbsp;remain&nbsp;effective even when tools are renamed or&nbsp;relocated.&nbsp;</li><li><strong>Network behavior</strong>&nbsp;<br>Network-focused fields describe how tools communicate during exfiltration, including protocol usage, destination types, authentication models, and connection patterns. Rather than relying on static indicators such as IP addresses or domains, the framework emphasizes&nbsp;behaviors&nbsp;that affect detection strategy, such as long-lived outbound connections, cloud API interactions, or peer-to-peer synchronization.&nbsp;<br>This abstraction is critical because many legitimate tools produce network traffic that appears benign in isolation. By capturing destination categories and communication patterns instead of specific endpoints, the framework supports detection approaches that focus on contextual anomalies, such as unexpected destinations, unusual transfer volumes, or deviations from baseline&nbsp;behavior.&nbsp;</li><li><strong>Forensic artifacts</strong>&nbsp;<br>Forensic artifact fields document traces that may persist on disk or in system state, including configuration files, logs, cached credentials, scheduled tasks, or registry changes. These artifacts are particularly valuable for retrospective detection, incident response, and timeline reconstruction.&nbsp;<br>Importantly, the framework treats forensic artifacts as&nbsp;variable rather than guaranteed. Some tools leave extensive footprints, while others&nbsp;operate&nbsp;with minimal persistence or rely on in-memory execution. Explicitly&nbsp;modeling&nbsp;this variability helps defenders understand where forensic blind spots may exist and which tools require stronger reliance on real-time telemetry.&nbsp;</li><li><strong>Detection</strong>&nbsp;<strong>focus</strong>&nbsp;<strong>areas</strong>&nbsp;<br>Instead of defining specific detection rules, the framework highlights a set of&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;patterns&nbsp;observed&nbsp;when legitimate tools are used for exfiltration. These include transfers to network destinations that are unusual for that tool or environment,&nbsp;command-line arguments specific to a particular tool being provided to a differently named process and data volume inconsistencies. These focus areas are intentionally abstract, allowing defenders to adapt them to different environments, logging capabilities, and threat models.&nbsp;</li></ul><p>This design choice reflects the reality that effective detection logic is highly&nbsp;environment-specific. By emphasizing&nbsp;<em>what to look for</em>&nbsp;rather than&nbsp;<em>how to detect it</em>, the framework supports reuse across organizations and avoids coupling the research to a specific detection platform or rule format.</p><h3 id="example-normalized-tool-representation">Example: Normalized Tool Representation&nbsp;</h3><p>To illustrate how these fields are applied, the following excerpt shows one&nbsp;particular entry&nbsp;in the framework, a normalized representation of the actionable items gleaned from research into the&nbsp;MOVEit&nbsp;Transfer tool.</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2026/03/moveit-yml.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="1750" height="3810" srcset="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w600/2026/03/moveit-yml.png 600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w1000/2026/03/moveit-yml.png 1000w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w1600/2026/03/moveit-yml.png 1600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2026/03/moveit-yml.png 1750w" sizes="(min-width: 720px) 720px"></figure><p>This normalized representation enables tools with&nbsp;very different&nbsp;implementations to be compared based on shared&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;characteristics, supporting consistent analysis across endpoint, network, and cloud contexts. The standardized format also&nbsp;facilitates&nbsp;reuse beyond manual analysis, including integration into automated or AI-assisted detection workflows.</p><h2 id="examples-of-tools-covered-in-the-framework">Examples of&nbsp;tools&nbsp;covered in the&nbsp;framework&nbsp;</h2><p>The framework currently&nbsp;analyzes&nbsp;a curated set of legitimate tools&nbsp;observed&nbsp;in real-world exfiltration scenarios and commonly found in enterprise environments.&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Built-in operating system tools</strong>&nbsp;</p><ul><li>PowerShell&nbsp;</li><li>robocopy&nbsp;</li><li>xcopy&nbsp;</li><li>bitsadmin&nbsp;</li><li>curl&nbsp;</li><li>wget&nbsp;</li></ul><p><strong>Third-party endpoint tools</strong>&nbsp;</p><ul><li>rclone&nbsp;</li><li>Syncthing&nbsp;</li><li>restic&nbsp;</li><li>GoodSync&nbsp;</li><li>MOVEit&nbsp;</li><li>PSCP&nbsp;</li></ul><p><strong>Cloud tools</strong>&nbsp;</p><ul><li>AWS CLI&nbsp;</li><li>AzCopy&nbsp;</li><li>Google Cloud CLI (gcloud)&nbsp;</li><li>S3 Browser&nbsp;</li></ul><p>This list is not intended to be exhaustive, but&nbsp;represents&nbsp;tools selected based on documented abuse in the wild, prevalence in enterprise environments, and relevance to defensive detection efforts.</p><h2 id="key-research-observations">Key&nbsp;research observations&nbsp;</h2><p>Analysis of these tools revealed several recurring patterns with direct implications for detection. Although individual utilities differ in implementation, their abuse for data exfiltration often converges in ways that undermine tool-centric detection approaches. The observations below highlight how attackers&nbsp;leverage&nbsp;legitimate functionality, existing trust relationships, and normal operational patterns to obscure exfiltration activity.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="similarity-of-network-traffic">Similarity of&nbsp;network&nbsp;traffic&nbsp;</h3><p>Across a wide range of tools&nbsp;analyzed&nbsp;in this research, outbound network traffic generated during data exfiltration often converges on common, legitimate patterns. Whether the utility is a native command-line tool, a third-party endpoint application, or a cloud client, data transfer typically occurs over standard application-layer protocols such as HTTPS.&nbsp;Typically&nbsp;the network traffic uses expected destination ports and encrypted payloads as well. This convergence reflects attackers’ preference for tools and services that are already&nbsp;permitted&nbsp;and widely used within enterprise environments.&nbsp;</p><p>In practice, exfiltration performed via cloud command-line interfaces, storage clients, or synchronization tools&nbsp;frequently&nbsp;targets trusted cloud platforms or externally hosted infrastructure.&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Public</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://cert.gov.ua/article/6279600" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>reporting</u></a>&nbsp;shows that attackers commonly&nbsp;leverage&nbsp;legitimate cloud services for data theft, resulting in outbound traffic that is difficult to distinguish from authorized business activity at the network layer.&nbsp;</p><p>For example, a cloud storage client uploading data to an external bucket and a synchronization utility transferring files to a remote peer may both generate long-lived HTTPS sessions with steady outbound throughput. From a network perspective, these transfers are characterized by encrypted traffic over standard ports, sustained outbound connections, and destinations associated with legitimate cloud storage providers. This combination closely resembles routine backup or synchronization activity and has been&nbsp;observed&nbsp;in multiple ransomware and extortion investigations involving tools&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>such as</u>&nbsp;<u>rclone</u></a>&nbsp;and cloud storage clients. As a result, network flow log-based detection approaches based on protocol identification, destination allow-listing, or port filtering provide limited visibility into exfiltration activity.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>Layer 7 metadata such as Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) flags or certificate data may&nbsp;provide&nbsp;additional detection opportunities in some cases, but even&nbsp;there&nbsp;standard attributes were the norm. This convergence highlights the need to correlate network telemetry with execution context, data volume&nbsp;relative&nbsp;to baselines, and destination characteristics&nbsp;—&nbsp;such as ownership or prior association with the organization&nbsp;—&nbsp;rather than relying on network traffic alone.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="variability-of-forensic-artifacts">Variability of&nbsp;forensic&nbsp;artifacts&nbsp;</h3><p>While network-level&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;often converges across exfiltration tools, the forensic artifacts left on the endpoint can vary significantly depending on the utility and execution method used. Some tools generate a rich and persistent footprint, including configuration files, local state databases, cached credentials, scheduled tasks, or detailed logs. When present, these artifacts provide valuable context for incident response, supporting threat hunting, timeline reconstruction, and attribution, as&nbsp;observed&nbsp;in real-world abuse of tools such as&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>rclone</u></a>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<a href="https://cert.gov.ua/article/6279600" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Syncthing</u></a>. While tool renaming is common and sometimes difficult to detect in process telemetry, masqueraded tools may still generate many of the same forensic artifacts, leading to&nbsp;additional&nbsp;opportunities for identification.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>Other tools&nbsp;operate&nbsp;with a much lighter or more transient footprint. Command-line utilities executed with inline arguments, temporary configurations, or fileless techniques may leave little evidence beyond short-lived process execution, command-line telemetry, and ephemeral network connections.&nbsp;<a href="https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/vice-society-ransomware-powershell/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Public reporting</u></a>&nbsp;shows that PowerShell-based exfiltration can rely&nbsp;almost entirely&nbsp;on execution context and in-memory&nbsp;behavior, leaving few durable artifacts on disk. In these cases,&nbsp;<a href="https://github.com/SigmaHQ/sigma/blob/master/rules/windows/process_creation/proc_creation_win_susp_data_exfiltration_via_cli.yml" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>forensic visibility</u></a>&nbsp;depends heavily on the availability and quality of endpoint logging, including process creation, command-line auditing, and script execution telemetry.&nbsp;</p><p>This variability reinforces a key finding of the research:&nbsp;There is no uniform forensic signature for exfiltration using legitimate tools. Effective detection therefore requires correlating endpoint telemetry with network and cloud data, rather than&nbsp;assuming that&nbsp;exfiltration activity will consistently leave persistent artifacts.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="cloud-native-tools-blend-into-normal-operations">Cloud-native&nbsp;tools&nbsp;blend into&nbsp;normal&nbsp;operations&nbsp;</h3><p>Cloud-native tools present a significant detection challenge because they&nbsp;operate&nbsp;within services that are already central to enterprise workflows. Authentication flows, API interactions, and data transfer patterns&nbsp;observed&nbsp;during exfiltration often closely resemble legitimate activity such as backups, deployments, or routine synchronization.&nbsp;<a href="https://cert.gov.ua/article/6279600" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Public</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/unc5537-snowflake-data-theft-extortion" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>incident reporting</u></a>&nbsp;shows that attackers&nbsp;frequently&nbsp;abuse officially supported cloud clients to move data to attacker-controlled storage while&nbsp;maintaining&nbsp;the appearance of normal cloud usage.&nbsp;</p><p>In these scenarios, traditional IOCs,&nbsp;such as domains or IP addresses,&nbsp;provide limited value. Cloud platforms rely on large, shared infrastructure, resulting in highly generic service endpoints used by both legitimate users and attackers. As a result,&nbsp;<a href="https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/unc5537-snowflake-data-theft-extortion" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>detecting or blocking exfiltration</u></a>&nbsp;based on network indicators alone is often impractical and risks significant operational disruption.&nbsp;</p><p>Compounding this challenge, many&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;network detections explicitly allow-list major cloud providers to reduce noise from expected business activity. While operationally necessary, this practice further limits visibility into cloud-based exfiltration, enabling attackers to bypass both legacy IOC-based detections and higher-level&nbsp;behavioral&nbsp;controls by&nbsp;operating&nbsp;entirely within trusted cloud services. This is where cloud-native security products with visibility into which tenants, subscriptions or individual storage buckets are owned by the organization, as well as whether the identity&nbsp;initiating&nbsp;the file transfer typically interacts with that cloud resource, can&nbsp;assist&nbsp;with detection.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="masquerading-as-a-common-technique">Masquerading as a&nbsp;common&nbsp;technique&nbsp;</h3><p>Masquerading is&nbsp;frequently&nbsp;used to reduce the visibility of data exfiltration by exploiting assumptions about trusted binaries and execution contexts. Rather than introducing unfamiliar tools, attackers often rename legitimate utilities or execute them from locations typically associated with benign software, allowing exfiltration activity to blend into normal endpoint operations and undermining detections based solely on binary names or file paths.&nbsp;</p><p>A&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>well-documented example</u></a>&nbsp;involves&nbsp;rclone, a legitimate cloud synchronization tool repeatedly abused in ransomware and data theft operations.&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Incident response reporting</u></a>&nbsp;shows&nbsp;rclone&nbsp;binaries being renamed and staged in trusted locations to evade scrutiny while enabling large-scale data transfers to attacker-controlled cloud storage under the appearance of routine administrative activity.&nbsp;</p><p>These cases&nbsp;demonstrate&nbsp;that filename- or path-based trust assumptions are insufficient for detecting exfiltration activity. Effective detection requires correlating execution context, parent process relationships, command-line usage, and network&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;to&nbsp;identify&nbsp;misuse of otherwise legitimate tools, particularly when they are deliberately presented as benign components of the operating environment.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="exfiltration-via-small-incremental-data-transfers">Exfiltration via&nbsp;small,&nbsp;incremental&nbsp;data&nbsp;transfers&nbsp;</h3><p>A recurring pattern across multiple exfiltration tools is the use of&nbsp;small, incremental data transfers&nbsp;instead of large, single exfiltration events. This technique, which MITRE&nbsp;tracks as&nbsp;<a href="https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1030/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>T1030</u></a>, relies on splitting data into smaller units and transmitting it over extended periods. By doing this, attackers can remain below volume-based detection thresholds and reduce the likelihood of drawing attention.&nbsp;<a href="https://reliaquest.com/blog/exfiltration-tools/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>This approach</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>has been</u>&nbsp;<u>observed</u></a>&nbsp;in real-world data theft and ransomware operations involving legitimate transfer and synchronization tools.&nbsp;</p><p>This&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;is not protocol- or tool-specific. File transfer utilities, synchronization tools, cloud clients, and scripting environments can all be configured to transfer data gradually, often closely resembling routine background activity.&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Public</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://cert.gov.ua/article/6279600" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>reporting</u></a>&nbsp;on the abuse of tools like&nbsp;rclone&nbsp;and&nbsp;Syncthing&nbsp;shows how repeated low-volume transfers can collectively result in significant data loss while&nbsp;remaining&nbsp;difficult to distinguish from legitimate use.&nbsp;</p><p>Because the timing, size, and frequency of these transfers often align with expected operational patterns&nbsp;—&nbsp;such as business hours or periodic jobs&nbsp;—&nbsp;detection typically requires longitudinal analysis rather than single-event alerts. Without baselining normal usage,&nbsp;<a href="https://reliaquest.com/blog/exfiltration-tools/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>low-and-slow</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://github.com/SigmaHQ/sigma/blob/master/rules/windows/process_creation/proc_creation_win_susp_data_exfiltration_via_cli.yml" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>exfiltration</u></a>&nbsp;can persist unnoticed.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="stealth-is-often-a-function-of-policy-not-tooling">Stealth is&nbsp;often a&nbsp;function of&nbsp;policy,&nbsp;not&nbsp;tooling&nbsp;</h3><p>Stealth in exfiltration scenarios often results from organizational policy rather than technical sophistication. Tools that are explicitly&nbsp;permitted&nbsp;or widely deployed&nbsp;frequently&nbsp;operate&nbsp;under relaxed monitoring, creating low-friction paths for data exfiltration.&nbsp;</p><p>In living-off-the-land scenarios, attackers deliberately abuse trusted utilities to&nbsp;benefit&nbsp;from existing allow-listing and policy exemptions, rather than advanced evasion techniques.&nbsp;<a href="https://redcanary.com/blog/threat-detection/rclone-mega-extortion/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>Public</u></a>&nbsp;<a href="https://reliaquest.com/blog/exfiltration-tools/" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>reporting</u></a>&nbsp;shows that legitimate cloud and synchronization tools are often misused precisely because their activity is expected and rarely scrutinized. In some ransomware incidents, attackers do not&nbsp;attempt&nbsp;to minimize volume or hide&nbsp;behavior&nbsp;at all, instead exfiltrating&nbsp;large amounts&nbsp;of data directly using trusted tools and infrastructure, relying on policy trust and limited inspection to avoid detection.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="observation-summary">Observation&nbsp;summary</h3><p>The following table summarizes several of the trends&nbsp;observed&nbsp;during the longitudinal analysis detailed above into categories:</p>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2><p>As data exfiltration increasingly relies on legitimate, trusted tools rather than custom malware, defenders must rethink how they approach detection. This research shows that meaningful visibility does not come from&nbsp;identifying&nbsp;tools in isolation, but from understanding the specific&nbsp;behaviors&nbsp;those tools exhibit when misused. By&nbsp;analyzing&nbsp;and normalizing execution patterns, network characteristics, and forensic artifacts across a wide range of benign utilities, the Exfiltration Framework provides a practical foundation for&nbsp;behavior-driven detection grounded in real telemetry. Ultimately, improving exfiltration detection requires not only broader visibility, but a deeper understanding of how trusted tools can be repurposed&nbsp;—&nbsp;and how those&nbsp;behaviors&nbsp;can be&nbsp;observed, contextualized, and detected in practice.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="contributing-to-the-exfiltration-framework">Contributing to the Exfiltration Framework&nbsp;</h2><p>The Exfiltration Framework is intended to evolve with the threat landscape. Contributions from defenders, researchers, and incident responders are welcome, particularly when grounded in real-world observations of how legitimate tools are abused for data exfiltration.</p><p>Whether it is documenting&nbsp;additional&nbsp;tools, refining existing entries, or sharing detection-relevant insights, community input helps keep the framework&nbsp;accurate&nbsp;and&nbsp;practically useful. Details on how to contribute are available in the <a href="https://github.com/Cisco-Talos/Xfiletrator" rel="noreferrer">project repository</a>.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="key-takeaways">Key takeaways&nbsp;</h2><ul><li>Legitimate tools are&nbsp;frequently&nbsp;abused for data exfiltration, making tool presence alone an unreliable detection signal.&nbsp;</li><li>Detection difficulty increases with tool legitimacy and native or cloud integration.&nbsp;</li><li>Behavioral&nbsp;signals&nbsp;—&nbsp;such as execution context, timing, data volume, authentication, and destination&nbsp;—&nbsp;are more reliable than static indicators when evaluated together.&nbsp;</li><li>Masquerading and low-and-slow transfer techniques exploit trust assumptions and volume-based detection thresholds.&nbsp;</li><li>Effective detection requires correlating endpoint, network, and cloud telemetry and baselining expected tool&nbsp;behavior.&nbsp;</li></ul><h2 id="acknowledgements">Acknowledgements&nbsp;</h2><p>We would like to thank the following researchers who have contributed to this project, via research into use of tools for exfiltration, review of the paper and framework, or general guidance and input:&nbsp;</p><ul><li>Dariia&nbsp;Deshunina&nbsp;</li><li>Jan&nbsp;Kotrady&nbsp;</li><li>Josh MacKenzie&nbsp;</li><li>Melvin Wiens&nbsp;</li><li>Nasreddine&nbsp;Bencherchali&nbsp;</li><li>Nick Randolph&nbsp;</li><li>Onur Erdogan&nbsp;</li><li>Radka&nbsp;Viskova&nbsp;</li><li>Ray McCormick&nbsp;</li><li>Robert Harris</li></ul>
