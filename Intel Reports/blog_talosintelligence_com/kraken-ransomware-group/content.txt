    <h1 class="text-center">Unleashing the Kraken ransomware group</h1>

    <div class="text-center m-1">
        <div class="post-author">
            <span>By </span>
                <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/author/chetan/">Chetan Raghuprasad</a>, 
                <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/author/michael/">Michael Szeliga</a>
        </div>

        <br/>
        <time class="post-datetime" datetime="November 13, 2025 06:00">
            Thursday, November 13, 2025 06:00
        </time>

        <div class="m-3">
                            <a href="/category/threat-spotlight/" class="category primary-category">
                                Threat Spotlight

    <section class="post-content-wrapper mt-5">
        <div class="post-content">
            <ul><li>In August 2025, Cisco Talos&nbsp;observed&nbsp;big-game hunting and double extortion attacks carried out by Kraken, a Russian-speaking group that has&nbsp;emerged&nbsp;from the remnants of the&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;ransomware cartel.</li><li>Talos observed&nbsp;in one intrusion&nbsp;that the Kraken&nbsp;actor&nbsp;exploited&nbsp;Server Message&nbsp;Block&nbsp;(SMB)&nbsp;vulnerabilities for&nbsp;initial&nbsp;access, then&nbsp;used&nbsp;tools like&nbsp;Cloudflared&nbsp;for persistence&nbsp;and&nbsp;SSH Filesystem (SSHFS)&nbsp;for data exfiltration before encryption.&nbsp;</li><li>Kraken is a cross-platform ransomware with distinct encryptors for Windows, Linux, and VMware&nbsp;ESXi,&nbsp;targeting a&nbsp;wide range of enterprise environments.&nbsp;</li><li>Kraken ransomware benchmarks a&nbsp;victim&nbsp;machine before starting the encryption&nbsp;process,&nbsp;a&nbsp;feature rarely seen in ransomware.&nbsp;</li><li>Talos&nbsp;also&nbsp;observed&nbsp;the&nbsp;announcement of a new underground forum, “The Last Haven Board,” on Kraken’s data leak blog, aimed at creating an anonymous and secure communication channel&nbsp;for&nbsp;the cybercrime underground.&nbsp;</li></ul><h2 id="who-is-kraken">Who is Kraken?&nbsp;</h2><p>The Kraken ransomware group, which&nbsp;emerged&nbsp;in February 2025, employs a double&nbsp;extortion technique and&nbsp;appears&nbsp;to be opportunistic, as it has&nbsp;not concentrated&nbsp;on any specific&nbsp;business verticals. According to Kraken’s leak site, victims span various&nbsp;geographies, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Denmark, Panama, and Kuwait.&nbsp;</p><p>Like other operators in the double extortion space, Kraken also&nbsp;operates&nbsp;a data leak site to&nbsp;disclose&nbsp;the stolen data of victims who do not meet their ransom demands.&nbsp;</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/data-src-image-fb028643-659a-4c1e-a2fe-4318b621e0d2.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="465" height="232"><figcaption><i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure 1. Kraken data leak blog.</em></i></figcaption></figure><p>Kraken encrypts the victim’s environment, uses the .zpsc&nbsp;file extension for the encrypted files, and drops&nbsp;a&nbsp;ransom note titled “readme_you_ws_hacked.txt.” In the ransom note, the actor threatens the victims by&nbsp;stating&nbsp;that they have stolen and encrypted their confidential data. They&nbsp;instruct the victim to contact them using an onion URL to prevent&nbsp;posting to&nbsp;their leak site.&nbsp;</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/data-src-image-ae56c5ac-0a33-49b0-9c9a-9b6e5d7a9e84.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="624" height="171" srcset="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w600/2025/11/data-src-image-ae56c5ac-0a33-49b0-9c9a-9b6e5d7a9e84.png 600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/data-src-image-ae56c5ac-0a33-49b0-9c9a-9b6e5d7a9e84.png 624w"><figcaption><i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure 2. Kraken ransom</em></i> <i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">note.</em></i></figcaption></figure><p>&nbsp;Talos observed in one of the instances that the actor demanded a ransom of&nbsp;around&nbsp;1&nbsp;million&nbsp;USD&nbsp;to be paid in Bitcoin to the actor’s wallet address.&nbsp;Kraken&nbsp;assures victims that after the successful payment, they will decrypt the&nbsp;environment and guarantee the non-disclosure of stolen data.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="ties-to-hellokitty">Ties to&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;</h2><p>Kraken, a Russian-speaking gang, is suspected to have&nbsp;emerged&nbsp;from the ashes of the&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;ransomware cartel or to have been&nbsp;established&nbsp;by some of its former members, according to&nbsp;<a href="https://www.cyjax.com/resources/blog/kraken-onto-the-extortion-scene-new-kraken-dls-emerges#:~:text=Should%20this%20fail%20to%20motivate,PII%2C%20or%20other%20sensitive%20documents.&amp;text=Context,on%20the%20extortion%20group's%20website." rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>external reports</u></a>. The title of the Kraken data leak site explicitly mentions the&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;ransomware group name. Additionally, Talos has&nbsp;observed&nbsp;that Kraken and&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;use the same ransom note filename,&nbsp;indicating&nbsp;a&nbsp;possible link&nbsp;between the two groups.&nbsp;</p><p>In September 2025, the Kraken group announced a new underground forum called “The Last Haven Board” in their data leak blog. According to its description, Last Haven’s primary&nbsp;objective&nbsp;is to create an anonymous and secure environment for communication within the cybercrime underground. Talos&nbsp;observed&nbsp;that the Last Haven forum administrator announced support and collaboration from the&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;team and&nbsp;WeaCorp, an exploit buyer organization, suggesting the possible involvement of&nbsp;HelloKitty&nbsp;operators with the Kraken group.&nbsp;</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/data-src-image-64d331e3-d412-42f5-83ee-1b4e3549d448.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="573" height="464"><figcaption><i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure</em></i> <i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">3. Last Haven underground forum announcement on Kraken data leak blog.</em></i></figcaption></figure><h2 id="infection-chain">Infection chain</h2><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/Kraken_infection.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="2000" height="929" srcset="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w600/2025/11/Kraken_infection.jpg 600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w1000/2025/11/Kraken_infection.jpg 1000w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w1600/2025/11/Kraken_infection.jpg 1600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w2400/2025/11/Kraken_infection.jpg 2400w" sizes="(min-width: 720px) 720px"><figcaption><span style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure&nbsp;4. Kraken infection chain.&nbsp;</span></figcaption></figure><p>In August 2025, Cisco Talos Incident Response (Talos IR)&nbsp;observed&nbsp;in one instance that the Kraken ransomware actor gained&nbsp;initial access to the victim's machine by exploiting an&nbsp;existing vulnerability&nbsp;in the SMB service on servers exposed to the internet.&nbsp;Once they&nbsp;established&nbsp;their foothold on the&nbsp;victim's&nbsp;machine, they extracted valid&nbsp;administrators’&nbsp;and other&nbsp;privileged accounts’&nbsp;credentials. Subsequently, they re-entered the victim environment through a Remote Desktop connection using the exfiltrated privileged account credentials.&nbsp;</p><p>After re-entering the&nbsp;victim&nbsp;machine, the attacker&nbsp;established&nbsp;a persistent connection by installing the&nbsp;Cloudflared&nbsp;tool and configuring a reverse tunnel on the&nbsp;victim's&nbsp;machine. Additionally, the attacker installed the SSHFS tool on the victim machine,&nbsp;utilizing&nbsp;it to navigate the victim's environment and exfiltrate sensitive data. The attacker then deployed the Kraken ransomware binary and moved laterally to other machines connected to the infected machine through&nbsp;Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP)&nbsp;connections, using the stolen privileged user accounts to deploy the ransomware binaries. Through this persistent remote connection, the attacker executed commands to run the ransomware on multiple systems within the victim's environment.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="kraken-ransomware-analysis">Kraken ransomware analysis&nbsp;&nbsp;</h2><p>Kraken ransomware is a sophisticated&nbsp;ransomware family with&nbsp;variants&nbsp;that&nbsp;target&nbsp;Windows, Linux, and&nbsp;ESXi&nbsp;systems. This ransomware offers extensive command-line options, providing operational flexibility for the&nbsp;actors&nbsp;who&nbsp;utilize&nbsp;Kraken ransomware in their attacks. It has the&nbsp;capability for either full or partial encryption of targeted files, along with features that allow for the encryption of specific files, including SQL databases and network shares.&nbsp;</p><p>To encrypt targeted files,&nbsp;Kraken ransomware employs RSA encryption algorithms with a key length of 4096 bits and ChaCha20 symmetric encryption. Additionally, the ransomware features encryption benchmarking capabilities to assess how quickly it can&nbsp;operate&nbsp;on the victim's machine without causing system overload, ensuring maximum damage in minimal time while evading detection through resource exhaustion.&nbsp;</p><p>Talos observed that the attacker executed the commands on Windows and&nbsp;ESXi&nbsp;environments to run the encryptor program.&nbsp;The&nbsp;Kraken encryptor is engineered with various command line&nbsp;arguments&nbsp;that the&nbsp;attacker&nbsp;could&nbsp;leverage&nbsp;depending on&nbsp;the&nbsp;victim’s environment.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="commands-for-windows-machine">Commands for&nbsp;Windows&nbsp;machine:&nbsp;</h3>

<h2 id="kraken-windows-encryptor">Kraken Windows encryptor&nbsp;&nbsp;</h2><p>The Windows version of Kraken ransomware is a 32-bit executable written in C++ and&nbsp;possibly&nbsp;obfuscated&nbsp;using&nbsp;a&nbsp;Golang-based&nbsp;packer. The ransomware exhibits&nbsp;features&nbsp;such as anti-reinfection checks, anti-analysis, and anti-recovery, and it encrypts the targeted files, appending&nbsp;the&nbsp;.zpsc&nbsp;file extension to the encrypted files.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="initial-execution-phase">Initial execution&nbsp;phase&nbsp;</h3><p>In the&nbsp;initial&nbsp;phase of execution,&nbsp;Kraken&nbsp;processes the command line parameters&nbsp;and performs&nbsp;the anti-reinfection checks on the victim machine to avoid double-encryption. The actor has employed&nbsp;anti-reinfection checks&nbsp;to effectively manage the&nbsp;decryption keys.</p><p>Kraken ransomware disables the WoW64 filesystem redirection on the victim machine by using the function <code>Wow64EnableWow64FsRedirection</code> with the argument “\0 (False)” to enable the 32-bit&nbsp;binary&nbsp;to access the 64-bit files on Windows machine.&nbsp;</p><p>WoW64 is a compatibility layer on a 64-bit Windows operating system that allows 32-bit applications to run seamlessly. The key feature of WoW64 is file system redirection, which ensures that when a 32-bit application attempts to access the “C:\Windows\System32” folder, WoW64 redirects it to “C:\Windows\SysWoW64”, allowing the 32-bit application to load the correct 32-bit version of system DLLs.&nbsp;</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/data-src-image-2de7e4c8-3f78-41b5-b37b-f988acc3aa09-1.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="700" height="147" srcset="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w600/2025/11/data-src-image-2de7e4c8-3f78-41b5-b37b-f988acc3aa09-1.png 600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/data-src-image-2de7e4c8-3f78-41b5-b37b-f988acc3aa09-1.png 700w"><figcaption><i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure</em></i> <i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">5. Function snippet disabling the WoW64 redirection.</em></i></figcaption></figure><p>Kraken ransomware, after disabling the WoW64 redirection,&nbsp;modifies&nbsp;its process token privilege, enabling the debugging rights.&nbsp;This privilege&nbsp;is essential for&nbsp;ransomware&nbsp;to access and&nbsp;encrypt&nbsp;files belonging to other&nbsp;processes.&nbsp;Further,&nbsp;the ransomware encrypts&nbsp;the&nbsp;local drives, network&nbsp;shares,&nbsp;and&nbsp;SQL&nbsp;database files and disables the&nbsp;backup services&nbsp;on the 64-bit&nbsp;Windows&nbsp;operating system.&nbsp;All&nbsp;these&nbsp;operations&nbsp;of the 32-bit ransomware binary&nbsp;would require&nbsp;access to the&nbsp;folder&nbsp;“C:\Windows\System32”.&nbsp;Disabling the redirection in Wow64 detection,&nbsp;complicate&nbsp;analysis, and&nbsp;prevent execution in sandbox environments.&nbsp;</p><p>The&nbsp;ransomware employs extensive control flow obfuscation with multiple conditional loops throughout the code, concealing the actual control flow paths and&nbsp;increasing complexity for static analysis and pattern matching for signature generation.&nbsp;</p><p>It also&nbsp;manipulates system exception handlers to prevent Windows error dialogs from appearing by&nbsp;executing <code>SetErrorMode</code> function with the value 0x8003&nbsp;which is a bitwise OR combination of three <p>It also&nbsp;deletes&nbsp;the recycle bin using the Windows function&nbsp;<code>SHEmptyRecycleBinA</code>.</p><h3 id="encryption-performance-testing-and-benchmarking">Encryption&nbsp;performance testing and benchmarking&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</h3><p>Kraken ransomware has the ability to conduct performance testing on the victim's machine before initiating the actual encryption.&nbsp;An actor&nbsp;can&nbsp;use this&nbsp;feature through command line options such as “-tests,” “-tempfile,” and “-tempsize” to assess the victim machine’s performance and&nbsp;optimize&nbsp;the ransomware encryption process.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>Kraken does this by first creating a temporary test file, using the path and filename specified via the "-tempfile" parameter. It then populates this file with random data, writing in 1MB chunks until the total size defined by the "-tempsize" parameter is reached. To time the core operation, the module records the start time with the clock_gettime&nbsp;function, performs the actual encryption on the test file, and then records the end time. Finally, it calculates the elapsed time and computes the encryption speed for the victim machine, expressed in MB/s, using the formula:&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>Speed&nbsp;= ((total bytes / elapsed time) * 1000) / 1048576.&nbsp;</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/image-1.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="1490" height="349" srcset="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w600/2025/11/image-1.png 600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w1000/2025/11/image-1.png 1000w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/image-1.png 1490w" sizes="(min-width: 720px) 720px"><figcaption><i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure</em></i> <i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">7. Function snippet performs calculation.</em></i></figcaption></figure><p>Based on the throughput results, the&nbsp;function&nbsp;validates&nbsp;if the attacker should choose full encryption mode or partial encryption mode with the&nbsp;maximum&nbsp;file size chunks to encrypt. After the performance testing process, it removes the test file&nbsp;using the function <code>unlink()</code> .&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><h3 id="parallel-encryption-operation">Parallel encryption operation&nbsp;&nbsp;</h3><p>The&nbsp;Kraken Windows encryptor has four encryption modules&nbsp;including SQL database, Network share, Local&nbsp;drive,&nbsp;and Hyper-V encryption. Based on the command-line flags provided by the attacker, the encryptor&nbsp;determines&nbsp;which encryption module to execute.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>The&nbsp;SQL&nbsp;database encryption&nbsp;module encrypts Microsoft SQL server databases.&nbsp;To target database files, the module accesses the Microsoft SQL Server registry keys on the victim machine, specifically querying&nbsp;“HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Microsoft SQL Server”&nbsp;and its&nbsp;“Instance Names\SQL”&nbsp;subkey to search for the "MSSQLSERVER" and "SQLEXPRESS" instances. Upon&nbsp;locating&nbsp;an instance, it retrieves the "SQLDataRoot" registry value to&nbsp;determine&nbsp;the path to the database files. The module then&nbsp;validates&nbsp;that these paths exist using the&nbsp;PathFileExistsWWindows&nbsp;API before&nbsp;proceeding&nbsp;to encrypt the database files.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>The network share encryption module&nbsp;enumerates&nbsp;and encrypts accessible network shares by using Windows&nbsp;WNet&nbsp;APIs to detect both mapped and unmapped network locations, specifying&nbsp;RESOURCETYPE_DISK&nbsp;and&nbsp;RESOURCETYPE_ANY. During enumeration, it iterates through the discovered network resources but explicitly skips the&nbsp;ADMIN$&nbsp;and&nbsp;IPC$&nbsp;shares. For each accessible network&nbsp;shares&nbsp;it finds, the module creates dedicated encryption worker threads to handle the encryption process.&nbsp;</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img src="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/image-2.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="1295" height="274" srcset="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w600/2025/11/image-2.png 600w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/size/w1000/2025/11/image-2.png 1000w, https://blog.talosintelligence.com/content/images/2025/11/image-2.png 1295w" sizes="(min-width: 720px) 720px"><figcaption><i><em class="italic" style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Figure 8. For each letter, it checks the drive type with the GetDriveTypeW&nbsp;function, targeting drives identified as&nbsp;DRIVE_REMOVABLE,&nbsp;DRIVE_FIXED, or&nbsp;DRIVE_REMOTE&nbsp;while excluding CD-ROM and network-only drives. After constructing the drive path (e.g., "X:\"), it creates a dedicated encryption&nbsp;worker&nbsp;thread for each validated drive path.&nbsp;</p><p>The Hyper-V virtual machine encryption module targets virtual machine files by executing a series of embedded PowerShell commands. First, it disables PowerShell restrictions on the victim machine to ensure its commands run. It then discovers the virtual machine files by listing all VMs and extracting their corresponding hard disk file paths. To unlock these files for encryption, the module forcefully stops all running virtual machines. After these prerequisite steps, it creates encryption worker threads to encrypt the located virtual machine files. The PowerShell commands executed by the module:&nbsp;</p>

<h3 id="encryption-types">Encryption types&nbsp;&nbsp;</h3><p>The ELF&nbsp;version of Kraken ransomware performs the multi-threaded&nbsp;encryption,&nbsp;supporting both “solid - Full encryption”&nbsp;and&nbsp;“setp&nbsp;- partial encryption”.&nbsp;It also&nbsp;employs the encryption performance benchmarking module that an attacker can&nbsp;leverage&nbsp;during the attack to calculate the encryption speed and decide if they want to perform full or partial encryption. The performance benchmarking algorithm is&nbsp;like&nbsp;the Windows version of Kraken ransomware&nbsp;described in the&nbsp;previous&nbsp;section.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>It performs the recursive directory traversal and encrypts the file based on the type of encryption mode specified in the command line&nbsp;parameter&nbsp;by the attacker and appends&nbsp;the&nbsp;.zpsc&nbsp;file extension to the encrypted files.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><h3 id="anti-analysis-and-detection-evasion">Anti-analysis and detection evasion&nbsp;&nbsp;</h3><p>The ELF&nbsp;version of Kraken ransomware&nbsp;employs control flow obfuscation with the complex loop structure to hinder the analysis&nbsp;and&nbsp;operates&nbsp;in daemon mode&nbsp;by forking into background process through&nbsp;<code>fork_as_daemon()</code> function and continues&nbsp;to run, performing&nbsp;the encryption in&nbsp;background.&nbsp;It also ignores the signal handlers SIGCHLD (child process termination) and SIGHUP&nbsp;(Terminal hangup).&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>The ransomware employs a multi-stage self-deletion and cleanup process to erase traces of its&nbsp;execution,&nbsp;leaving a minimal forensic artefact,&nbsp;after completing the encryption operation.&nbsp;Kraken&nbsp;creates a bash script “_bye_bye_.sh” in the same directory as the ransomware binary.&nbsp;It then builds the script with the commands to&nbsp;delete&nbsp;the log files, shell history, ransomware&nbsp;binary,&nbsp;and the script itself.&nbsp;</p>
