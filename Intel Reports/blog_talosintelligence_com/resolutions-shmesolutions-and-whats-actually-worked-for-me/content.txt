    <h1 class="text-center">Resolutions, shmesolutions (and what’s actually worked for me)</h1>

    <div class="text-center m-1">
        <div class="post-author">
            <span>By </span>
                <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/author/amy-ciminnisi/">Amy Ciminnisi</a>
        </div>

        <br/>
        <time class="post-datetime" datetime="January 8, 2026 14:00">
            Thursday, January 8, 2026 14:00
        </time>

        <div class="m-3">
                        <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/category/threat-source-newsletter/" class="category primary-category">
                            Threat Source newsletter
                        </a>

    <section class="post-content-wrapper mt-5">
        <div class="post-content" id="post-content">
            <p>Welcome to this week’s edition of the Threat Source newsletter.&nbsp;</p><p>I went to bed at 8:30 p.m. on New Year’s Eve, and I think that’s pretty indicative of&nbsp;how I&nbsp;approach&nbsp;the whole&nbsp;idea of&nbsp;New Year’s resolutions.&nbsp;</p><p>I love to count down to the new year with loved ones as&nbsp;much as the next person, but&nbsp;I have really conflicted feelings about traditional resolutions. On one hand,&nbsp;it’s&nbsp;great to have goals for the future and pick a day to start putting them into action. On the&nbsp;other, why wait until the New Year, and why pick goals that are often wildly unsustainable? It feels like it just promotes an “all or nothing”&nbsp;approach, and&nbsp;starts the year on a disappointing note if you stumble even a little.&nbsp;Life happens, and many resolutions&nbsp;don’t&nbsp;give enough grace.&nbsp;</p><p>Here are some resolutions&nbsp;I&nbsp;failed at this past year:&nbsp;</p><ul><li>Lift weights&nbsp;three days/week for&nbsp;a whole year: Close, but no cigar!&nbsp;</li><li>Journal&nbsp;at least one&nbsp;sentence every day:&nbsp;Yeah,&nbsp;I&nbsp;failed at this one&nbsp;pretty quickly.&nbsp;I’m&nbsp;not a journal person.&nbsp;</li><li>Knit at least three sweaters: I&nbsp;made a shirt, almost finished a vest, and&nbsp;spent a ton of&nbsp;money on&nbsp;yarn.</li></ul><p>I have done a lot of things&nbsp;I’m&nbsp;proud about this year, so then... what&nbsp;<em>has</em>&nbsp;worked?&nbsp;An&nbsp;intention that&nbsp;I’ve&nbsp;held throughout the year is turning “shoulds” into setting plans into motion right away. For example, “I should host a one-time book club to discuss my favorite book” becomes “I just posted&nbsp;in&nbsp;my neighborhood Facebook page to find people who are interested and pick a date.” Or “I should&nbsp;finish&nbsp;my certification” becomes “I just set a weekly three-hour calendar block, and I&nbsp;won’t&nbsp;move it unless&nbsp;there’s&nbsp;an emergency.”</p><p>That shift in mindset reminds me a lot of what works in cybersecurity. Our industry is full of ambitious, high-level goals: “Eliminate all vulnerabilities,” “achieve zero trust,” or “stop every threat.” These aspirations are important, but the reality is that security happens in small, consistent actions: patching systems as soon as updates are available, educating teams on the latest phishing techniques, reviewing logs regularly, or simply responding quickly to a new alert.</p><p>Just like with personal resolutions, there’s often pressure in security to be perfect, to never let anything slip through the cracks. Even the organizations that have amazing budget and headcount will face challenges and setbacks, and no environment is ever perfectly secure. What matters most is how we respond in the moment, learn from what’s happened, and keep moving forward.</p><p>So as we head into 2026, whether you’re setting personal goals or planning your organization’s security strategy, consider focusing less on flawless resolutions and more on building habits that adapt to change. Celebrate the small wins, reflect on what you’ve accomplished, and don’t be afraid to pivot when things don’t go as planned. Show up every day and take that next step.</p><h2 id="the-one-big-thing">The one&nbsp;big thing&nbsp;</h2><p>Earlier today, Cisco Talos&nbsp;<a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/uat-7290" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>disclosed</u>&nbsp;<u>a sophisticated threat actor</u></a>&nbsp;we&nbsp;track as&nbsp;UAT-7290,&nbsp;who has been active since&nbsp;at least&nbsp;2022.&nbsp;UAT-7290&nbsp;is tasked with&nbsp;gaining&nbsp;initial access as well&nbsp;as&nbsp;conducting espionage-focused intrusions against critical infrastructure entities in South Asia.&nbsp;UAT-7290's arsenal includes a malware family consisting of implants we call&nbsp;RushDrop,&nbsp;DriveSwitch,&nbsp;and&nbsp;SilentRaid. Our findings&nbsp;indicate&nbsp;that&nbsp;UAT-7290 conducts&nbsp;extensive&nbsp;technical&nbsp;reconnaissance&nbsp;of target organizations before carrying out intrusions.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="why-do-i-care">Why do I care?&nbsp;</h3><p>UAT-7290 targets telecom and network infrastructure, which, if compromised, can have cascading impacts on national security, business operations, and customer data. Their advanced tactics, use of publicly available exploits, and ability to&nbsp;establish&nbsp;persistent footholds make detection and remediation&nbsp;difficult.&nbsp;</p><h3 id="so-now-what">So now what?&nbsp;</h3><p>Review and apply the latest&nbsp;ClamAV&nbsp;and Snort signatures (see&nbsp;the&nbsp;<a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/uat-7290" rel="noreferrer noopener"><u>blog</u></a>) to detect and block UAT-7290’s malware and activity.
