<p><span style="font-weight: 400">Many of these exploits are new, with public disclosures and proofs of concepts dated as recently as September. While the X10-unix attacks vary in target operating system and architecture, each tries to do three things:</span></p>
<ol>
<li style="font-weight: 400"><span style="font-weight: 400">Install Monero cryptomining software.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400"><span style="font-weight: 400">Install the appropriate version of the X10-unix worm.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400"><span style="font-weight: 400"><span style="font-weight: 400">Open a backdoor listening on ports 30004 and 30006 and upload the victim’s IP address to a private Pastebin paste.</span></span>
<p><figure id="attachment_23216" class="thumbnail wp-caption aligncenter" style="width: 1024px"><img loading="lazy" class="size-large wp-image-23216" src="https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image7-1024x160.png" alt="" width="1024" height="160" srcset="https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image7-1024x160.png 1024w, https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image7-300x47.png 300w, https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image7-768x120.png 768w, https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image7.png 1138w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /><figcaption class="caption wp-caption-text">Victim IP address being uploaded to Pastebin</figcaption></figure></li>
</ol>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400">The backdoor is implemented by a UPX-packed binary “ss”, also written in Go, with variations compiled for Windows and multiple Linux architectures. This program first determines the hijacked computer’s external IP address from myexternalip.com, uploads that address as the name of a private Pastebin paste, then starts TCP listeners on ports 30004 and 30006.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400">In addition to the known vulnerabilities above, X10-unix attempts to connect to Android Debug Bridge connections on port 5555. </span></p>
<figure id="attachment_23217" class="thumbnail wp-caption aligncenter" style="width: 1024px"><img loading="lazy" class="size-large wp-image-23217" src="https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image1-1024x660.png" alt="" width="1024" height="660" srcset="https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image1-1024x660.png 1024w, https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image1-300x193.png 300w, https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image1-768x495.png 768w, https://blogs.juniper.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/image1.png 1276w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /><figcaption class="caption wp-caption-text">ADB infection code</figcaption></figure>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400">On successful connection, X10-unix runs a script that uploads a native binary (“blu”) and an Android APK (“weixin.apk”), both base64-encoded. Blu probes the device’s bluetooth hardware and installs the APK. The APK uploads the device’s IP address to Pastebin as above and then downloads and installs an ARM CPU port of X10-unix. A similar attempt is made to install payloads on existing reverse-shell backdoors listening on port 1337.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400">While it’s difficult to ascertain the breadth or effectiveness of this malware campaign, in part because Monero — unlike Bitcoin — does not have publicly traceable transactions, JTL can confirm over a hundred distinct hosts have been observed propagating the infection.</span></p>
