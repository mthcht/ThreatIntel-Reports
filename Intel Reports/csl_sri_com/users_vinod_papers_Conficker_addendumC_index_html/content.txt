<h2><br>
</h2>
<h2>Introduction</h2>
<p style="text-align: justify;">This addendum provides an evolving
snapshot of our understanding of
the latest Conficker variant, referred to as<span
 style="font-style: italic;"> Conficker C</span>.&nbsp;&nbsp; The
variant was brought to the attention of the Conficker Working Group
when one member reported that a compromised Conficker B honeypot was
updated with a new dynamically linked library (DLL). Although a network
trace for this infection is not available, we suspect that this DLL may
have propagated via Conficker's Internet rendezvous point mechanism (<a
 href="index.html#sec-global-impact">Global Network Impact</a>).&nbsp;&nbsp; The
infection
was found on the
morning of
Friday, 6 March 2009 (PST), and it was later reported that other
working
group
members had received other DLL reinfections throughout the
same day.&nbsp;&nbsp; Since that point, multiple members have reported
upgrades of previously infected machines to this latest variant via
HTTP-based Internet rendezvous points.&nbsp; We
believe this latest outbreak of Conficker variant C began first
spreading at roughly 6 p.m. PST, 4 March 2009 (5 March
UTC).&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
<br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;"><span style="font-style: italic;"><br>
</span>In this addendum report, we summarize the inner workings and
practical implications of this latest malicious software application
produced by the Conficker developers.&nbsp;&nbsp; In addition to the
dual layers of packing and encryption used to protect A and B from
reverse engineering, this latest variant also cloaks its newest code
segments, along with its latest functionality, under a significant
layer
of code obfuscation to further hinder
binary analysis.&nbsp;&nbsp; Nevertheless, with a careful mixture of
static and dynamic analysis, we attempt here to summarize the internal
logic of Conficker C.<span style="font-style: italic;"></span><br>
</p>
<br>
<div style="text-align: justify;">
<h2>Implications of Variant C<br>
</h2>
Variant C represents the third major revision of the Conficker malware
family, which first appeared on the Internet on 20 November
2008.&nbsp;&nbsp; C distinguishes itself as a significant revision to
Conficker B.&nbsp; In fact, we estimate that C&nbsp; leaves as little
as
15% of the original B code base untouched, as illustrated in Appendix
3,&nbsp; <a href="index.html#Appendix_3_Compartive_Assessment_from_">A
Comparative Assessment of Conficker B and C Process Images</a>.
&nbsp;&nbsp; Whereas the recently reported B++ variant represented a
more surgical derivative of B,&nbsp; C incorporates a major
restructuring of B's previous thread architecture and program logic,
including major functional additions such as a new peer-to-peer (P2P)
coordination channel,&nbsp; and a revision of the domain generation
algorithm (DGA). &nbsp; It is clear that the Conficker authors are well
informed and are tracking efforts to eliminate the previous Conficker
epidemics at the host and Internet governance level.&nbsp; In Conficker
C, they have now responded with many of their own countermeasures to
thwart these latest defenses.<br>
<br>
For example, C's latest revision of Conficker's now well-known Internet
rendezvous logic may represent a direct retort to the action of the
Conficker Cabal, which recently blocked all domain registrations
associated with the A and B strains.&nbsp;&nbsp; C now selects its
rendezvous points from a pool of over 50,000 randomly generated domain
name candidates each day.&nbsp;&nbsp; C further increases Conficker's
top-level domain (TLD) spread from five TLDs in Conficker A, to eight
TLDs in
B, to 110 TLDs that must now be involved in coordination efforts to
track and block C's potential DNS queries.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; With this
latest escalation in domain space manipulation, C not only represents a
significant challenge to those hoping to track its census, but
highlights some weaknesses in the long-term viability of
how&nbsp; Internet address and name space governance is conducted.<br>
&nbsp;
<br>
One interesting and minimally explored aspect of Conficker is its early
and sophisticated adoption of binary encryption, digital signatures,
and advanced hash algorithms to prevent third-party hijacking of the
infected population.&nbsp;&nbsp; At its core, the main purpose of
Conficker is to provide the authors with a secure binary updating
service that effectively allows them instant control of millions of PCs
worldwide.&nbsp;&nbsp; Through the use of these binary encryption
methods, Conficker's authors have taken care to ensure that other
groups cannot upload arbitrary binaries to their infected drone
population, and these protections cover all Conficker updating
services: Internet rendezvous point downloads, buffer overflow
re-exploitation, and the latest P2P control protocol.<br>
<br>
In evaluating this mechanism, we find that the Conficker authors have
devised a sophisticated encryption protocol that is generally robust to
direct attack.&nbsp; All three crypto-systems employed by Conficker's
authors (RC4, RSA, and MD-6) also have one underlying
commonality.&nbsp; They were all produced by Dr. Ron Rivest of
MIT.&nbsp; Furthermore, the use of MD-6 is a particularly unusual
algorithm selection, as it represents the latest encryption hash
algorithm produced to date.&nbsp; The discovery of MD-6 in Conficker B
is indeed highly unusual given Conficker's own development time
line.&nbsp; We date the creation of Conficker A to have occurred in
October 2008, roughly the same time frame that MD-6 had been publicly
released by Dr. Rivest (see http://groups.csail.mit.edu/cis/md6).
&nbsp; While A employed SHA-1, we can now confirm that MD-6 had been
integrated into Conficker B by late December 2008 (i.e., the authors
chose to incorporate a hash algorithm that had literally been made
publicly available only a few weeks earlier).<br>
<br>
Unfortunately for the Conficker authors, by mid-January, Dr. Rivest&#8217;s
group submitted a revised version of the MD-6 algorithm, as a buffer
overflow had been discovered in its implementation.&nbsp;&nbsp; This
revision was inserted quietly, followed later by a more visible public
announcement of the buffer overflow on 19 February 2009, with the
release
of the Fortify report
(http://blog.fortify.com/repo/Fortify-SHA-3-Report.pdf). We confirmed
that this buffer overflow was present in the Conficker B
implementations.&nbsp; However, we also confirmed that this buffer
overflow was not exploitable as a means to take control of Conficker
hosts.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Nevertheless, the Conficker developers were
obviously aware of these developments, as they have now repaired their
MD-6 implementation in Conficker C, using the identical
fix made by Dr. Rivest's group.&nbsp; Clearly the authors are aware of,
and
adept at understanding and incorporating, the latest cryptographic
advances, and are actively monitoring the latest developments in this
community.<br>
<br>
One major implication from the Conficker B and C variants, as well as
other
now recently emerging malware families, is the sophistication with
which they are able to terminate, disable, reconfigure, or blackhole
native operating system (OS) and third-party security services. &nbsp;
We provide an in-depth analysis of Conficker's <a
 href="index.html#SecurityProductDisablement">Security
Product Disablement</a> logic, to help illustrate the comprehensive
challenge that modern malware poses to security products, and to
Microsoft's anti-malware efforts.&nbsp;&nbsp; Conficker offers a nice
illustration of the degree to which security vendors are being actively
challenged to not&nbsp; just hunt for malicious logic, but to defend
their own availability, integrity, and the network connectivity vital
to providing them a continual flow of the latest malware threat
intelligence.<br>
<br>
Perhaps the most obvious frightening aspect of Conficker C is its
clear potential to do harm.&nbsp;&nbsp; Among the long history of
malware epidemics, very few can claim sustained
worldwide
infiltration of multiple millions of infected drones. &nbsp; Perhaps in
the best case, Conficker may be used as a sustained and profitable
platform for massive Internet fraud and theft.&nbsp; In the worst case,
Conficker could be turned into a powerful offensive weapon for
performing concerted information warfare attacks that could disrupt not
just
countries, but the Internet itself. <br>
&nbsp;<br>
Finally, we must also acknowledge the multiple skill sets that are
revealed within the evolving design and implementation of
Conficker.&nbsp; Those responsible for this outbreak have demonstrated
Internet-wide programming skills, advanced cryptographic skills, custom
dual-layer code packing and code obfuscation skills, and in-depth
knowledge of Windows internals and security products.&nbsp; They are
among the first to introduce the Internet rendezvous point scheme, and
have now integrated a sophisticated P2P protocol that does not require
an embedded peer list.&nbsp; They have continually seeded the Internet
with new MD5 variants, and have adapted their code base to address the
latest attempts to thwart Conficker.&nbsp;&nbsp; They have infiltrated
government sites, military networks, home PCs, critical infrastructure,
small networks, and universities, around the world.&nbsp; Perhaps an
even greater threat than what they have done so far, is what they
have learned and what they will build next.<br>
<br>
<br>
</div>
<h2>Conficker C Overview<br>
</h2>
<div style="text-align: justify;"><a href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/index2.html#fig-D-Overview">Figure
1</a> illustrates the
Conficker C program structure and logic.&nbsp;&nbsp; When initialized,
the DLL performs its setup logic, similar to that of A and
B, with extensions.&nbsp;&nbsp; At initialization, it checks for the
presence of three mutex
values on the target host to avoid
reinfection.&nbsp; If absent, these three mutexes are created: 1) the
mutex name "Global\&lt;string&gt;-7"; 2) the mutex name
"Global\&lt;string&gt;-99; and 3) a mutex named pseudo-randomly
generated based on the process ID.&nbsp; The &lt;string&gt; in the
first two mutex is unique per computer name; it is calculated based on
the crc32 hash of the computer name and XOR'ed with a constant.&nbsp; C
then installs several
in-memory patches to DLLs, and embeds other
mechanisms to thwart security applications that would otherwise detect
its presence.<br>
<br>
C modifies the host domain name service (DNS) APIs to block
various security-related network connections (<a href="index.html#dns-prevention">Domain
Lookup Prevention</a>), and installs a
pseudo-patch to repair the 445/TCP vulnerability, while maintaining a
backdoor for reinfection (<a href="index.html#sec-pseudo-path">Local
Host Patch Logic</a>). This pseudo patch protects the host
from buffer overflows by sources other than those
performed by the Conficker authors or their infected peers.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <br>
<br>
Like Conficker B,&nbsp; C incorporates
logic to defend itself from
security products that would otherwise attempt to detect and remove
it.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; C spawns a security <a
 href="index.html#service-disablement">product disablement thread</a>.&nbsp; This
thread disables critical host security services, such as Windows
defender, as well as Windows services that deliver security patches and
software updates.&nbsp; These changes effectively
prevent the victim host from receiving automated software
updates. The thread disables security update notifications and
deactivates safeboot mode as a future reboot option.&nbsp; This first
thread then spawns a new <a href="index.html#process-termination">security
process termination thread</a>,
which continually monitors for and kills processes whose names match a
blacklisted set of 23 security products, hot fixes, and security
diagnosis tools.&nbsp; <br>
</div>
<br>
<br>

<p style="text-align: justify;"><span style="font-style: italic;"></span><br>
Conficker C installs itself into the
user file system and configures
the registry appropriately to invoke its DLL at host
startup.&nbsp;&nbsp; It also inserts a variety of extraneous registry
keys that are subsequently unused, presumably to cloak its presence
(<a href="index.html#install-obfuscate">Obfuscating C's Installation and Its
Presence</a><span style="font-weight: bold;"></span>).&nbsp;&nbsp;
It copies itself into a randomly named DLL located in either the
System32 directory, program files directory, or the user's temporary
files
folder. &nbsp; It deletes all restore
points prior to its infection to thwart rollback.&nbsp; C then&nbsp;
performs a simple validation of its DLL size, and suicides if this
check fails.&nbsp; It sets the DLL's date to the same
date as the local&nbsp; <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">kernel32.dll,</span>
and sets NT File System (NTFS) file
permissions on its stored file image to
prevent write and delete privileges.&nbsp; Once installed, the DLL
spawns a remote thread,
which it attaches to the <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">netsvcs.exe</span>
or <span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">svchost.exe</span>
process, depending
on the OS version.</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">The core elements of Conficker C are
incorporated into two
threads:&nbsp; a P2P communication thread, and the domain
generation and Internet rendezvous point thread.&nbsp; The first thread
is embodied in a code segment that has undergone an additional layer of
code obfuscation, suggesting a desire by the Conficker authors to
hinder its analysis, and thereby providing an obvious point for in
depth
inspection.&nbsp;&nbsp; We describe the P2P protocol in
<a href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/index2.html#peer-to-peer">Peer to Peer Logic</a>.
&nbsp; The P2P protocol
includes an ability to coordinate with peers over TCP and UDP channels,
as well as download and run digitally signed Win32 binaries. &nbsp;
Incorporated with the P2P thread&nbsp; is anti-tracing logic
that will kill the Conficker C process when run under a debugger.&nbsp;
This logic was removed for this analysis.&nbsp; Conficker C also
incorporates an HTTP
date check function, which is discussed within <a href="index.html#peer-to-peer">Peer
to Peer Logic</a>.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <br>
<span style="font-style: italic;"></span><span
 style="font-style: italic;"></span></p>
<div style="text-align: justify;">Finally, C introduces a
substantial modification of the DGA and query
procedure, discussed in <a
 href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/index2.html#domain-generation-algorithm">Domain Generation
Algorithm</a>.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
The DGA will be activated on 1 April 2009, and before April 1st
it will enter a loop that sleeps 24 hours and then rechecks the date
via
<span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">getlocaltime</span>.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Prior to entering the April 1st date check, C will
sleep for an initial random interval between 30 and 90 minutes.&nbsp;
More
specifically, this sleep interval is between 30 and 90 minutes if the
local
hour is after 11 a.m. and before 7 a.m. If the&nbsp; local time is
after 8 a.m.
and before 11 a.m., the sleep period will be between 2.5&nbsp; and
3.5 hours.&nbsp; It will then check for Internet connectivity, and if
connected will enter the domain generation logic.&nbsp;&nbsp; The next
section describes this logic in greater detail.<br>
</div>
<br>
<br>
<h2><a name="domain-generation-algorithm"></a>Domain Generation
Algorithm</h2>
<p style="text-align: justify;">The domain generation algorithm
and query procedure (<a href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/dga-code">Figure 2</a>) have been
significantly modified from previous versions of Conficker.&nbsp; Among
the potential motivations for these changes may be to address the
recent actions of the Conficker Cabal, as it has moved to block future
registrations of Conficker A and B domains.&nbsp; Among the key
changes,
Conficker C increases the number of daily domain names
generated, from 250 to 50,000 potential Internet rendezvous points.
&nbsp;&nbsp; Of these 50,000 domains, only 500 are queried, and unlike
previous versions, they are queried only once per day.&nbsp; <br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Furthermore, C
provides significantly more filtering of the IP addresses produced by
the DNS
queries.&nbsp; The IP address is rejected if<br>
</p>
<ol>
  <li>it was associated with a query that returned more than one
IP address <br>
  </li>
  <li>it is <span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">127.0.0.1</span>
(localhost) or other trivial address<br>
  </li>
  <li>it
matches an address with an internal blacklist (see <a
 href="index.html#Appendix_2_Domain_Generator_Filtered">Appendix 2</a>
for the full
blacklist) </li>
  <li style="text-align: justify;">another
DNS query had previously returned the identical IP.&nbsp;&nbsp; Note,
if an organization chooses to register and resolve multiple
Conficker C domains to a single IP address, C-infected machines will
not contact that IP more than one time<br>
  </li>
</ol>
<p style="text-align: justify;">If none of the domains are alive and
ready to serve a digitally
signed payload,&nbsp; C will sleep for 24 hours, and then will generate
a
new list of 50,000 domains. &nbsp;&nbsp; The algorithm produces a
domain name set
that is independent of Conficker A and B, and will overlap these
other domain sets only in a rare coincidence. &nbsp;&nbsp; The
name of each generated domain is 4 to 10 characters, to which
a randomly selected&nbsp; TLD is appended from the following list of
116
suffix (mapping to 110 TLDs):<br>
</p>

<div style="text-align: justify;">To resolve the set of domain names to

Windows <span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">gethostbyname</span>
API.&nbsp; If the queried domain produces an IP address that passes C's

port 80/TCP.&nbsp; Here it uses a single call to the <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">HttpQueryInfo</span>
API (i.e., an empty HTTP Get request).&nbsp; If it succeeds in
connecting
to an
authentic Conficker rendezvous point, the server will immediately send
a digitally signed Win32 executable for the client to
execute.&nbsp;&nbsp; This variant does <span
 style="font-style: italic;">not&nbsp; </span>produce the well-known
Conficker <span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">search

 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">q=</span> or <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">aq=</span>).&nbsp;
Rather, the empty
HTTP request may prove more difficult for IDS and network
forensic&nbsp; signatures to identify.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
<br>
<br>
Variant C cycles through its domain query loop once per 24 hour period,
whereas A cycled through its domain list every 3 hours, and B
cycled every 2 hours.&nbsp; In runtime testing of the domain generation
algorithm, infected clients
may take over 4 hours to complete the full 500-set of daily domain
queries.&nbsp; <br>
<br>
Another noticeable difference between variants A/B/B++ and the new C
variant
is that the new variant incorporates a 300-second timeout interval on
the downloaded session. &nbsp;&nbsp; If the binary take more time than
this to
download, the session is terminated.&nbsp; There is also a
file size limit imposed by C of 512 Kbs. &nbsp; If this download size
is
reached before completion, then the routine is exited.&nbsp; However,
even when
the binary download is stopped prematurely, the digital
signature is still checked. &nbsp; If&nbsp; C succeeds in downloading a
valid Win32 executable, the&nbsp; domain generation algorithm's main
thread sleeps for 4 days, and then resume generating and
contacting domains.<br>
<br>
Once the file has been downloaded, C validates the digital
signature of the binary, and spawns this executable via<span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;"> ShellExecute</span>,
as
discussed for Conficker A and B (<a
 href="http://mtc.sri.com/Conficker/#A_Static_Analysis_of_Conficker">Binary
Download and Validation</a>).<br>

<h2><a name="peer-to-peer"></a>Peer-to-Peer Logic<br>
</h2>
<div style="text-align: justify;">
<p>Conficker C introduces yet another mechanism to coordinate infected
hosts.&nbsp; This new coordination strategy employs a P2P protocol, and
the Conficker authors have taken some care to hinder its analysis
through code obfuscation.&nbsp; They have also obfuscated the logic
that implements P2P binary download validation, HTTP date checking,
anti-debugger segments, and other logic.&nbsp; In particular, within
the P2P segments, the authors have attempted to impede the
identification of Windows API calls, and have applied other code
obfuscation to&nbsp; hinder analysis.&nbsp; Appendix 5, <a
 href="index.html#appendix5-api-recovery-table">API Recovery Table,</a>&nbsp;
includes the mapping of obfuscated APIs to code offsets, which were
recovered from our analysis.<br>
</p>
<p>Also integrated within the broader P2P program logic are other
obfuscated code segments, for example, those code segments dedicated to
establishing HTTP server communications on a
Conficker-infected host, peer scan logic, and filesharing logic for
both client- and server-side sharing.&nbsp; <br>
<br>
</p>
<h3>P2P Setup Logic</h3>
Upon entry to the P2P main thread, Conficker C dynamically computes an
in-memory import table, which contains the list of obfuscated
APIs.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; C next sets various registry entries and
creates a dedicated working directory for use by the P2P service.&nbsp;
It will then use the standard Microsoft Crypto Library for random
number generation. These steps are the setup for the actual P2P logic. <br>
<br>
C creates a directory in the Windows (OS dependent) standard default
temporary file directory, under the name:&nbsp;&nbsp; <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">C:\...\Temp\{%08X-%04X-%04X-%04X-%08X%04X}</span>.&nbsp;
This directory is used by C's P2P service to store downloaded payload,
and as writing space for storing other information.
Conficker also creates a registry entry that corresponds to C's scratch
directory:
<br>
<br>
<span style="color: rgb(0, 0, 0);">HKLM</span>\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Explorer\{%08X-%04X-%04X-%04X-%08X%04X}
<br>
<br>
The infected node is capable of acting like a server.&nbsp; In this
mode it will interact with the Conficker P2P network and distribute
digitally signed files to other P2P clients.&nbsp;&nbsp; It can operate
in this mode when it has determined that it has a locally stored
binary in its P2P&nbsp; temporary directory, and this file has been
properly digitally signed by the Conficker authors and is
unaltered&nbsp; (i.e., properly hashed and signed).<br>
<br>

DNS lookups of this subset list, and it&nbsp; filters each returned IP
address against the same list of blacklist IP address ranges used by
the domain generation algorithm (see <a

If the IP does not match the blacklist, C connects to the site's
port 80/TCP, and sends an empty URL GET header, for example <br>

<h3>Searching for Peers</h3>
Conficker C peers can act simultaneously as both P2P clients and
servers.&nbsp; To enable this interaction, C opens 2 UDP server
(listen) ports and two TCP server (listen) ports.&nbsp; One or two
additional UDP "client" ports may be employed.&nbsp; File transfers can
occur in both directions,
i.e., clients can "pull" and servers can "receive"&nbsp; files.&nbsp;
The P2P logic of Conficker C is spread through a set of threads to
support its scanning for peers as well as the reception of digitally
signed payloads. The main thread spawns a set of seven additional
threads that are designed to orchestrate Conficker's P2P traffic,
illustrated in <a href="index.html#fig-named-p2p-thread">Figure 3</a>.&nbsp;&nbsp;
<br>
<br>
<hr style="width: 100%; height: 1px;" noshade="noshade"><a
 name="fig-named-p2p-thread"></a><br>
<div style="text-align: center;"><img
 style="width: 445px; height: 362px;" alt="" src="p2p-main-thread.jpg"><br>
</div>

<hr style="width: 100%; height: 1px;" noshade="noshade"><br>
The main P2P thread starts by spawning five threads.&nbsp; The first
thread sets the system time using a call to <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">InternetTimeToSystemTime</span>
based on a connection to one of the popular Internet portals, listed
above.&nbsp; Two additional threads listen on TCP ports and serve as a
mechanism for distributing the payload to other Conficker peers. If a
legitimate Conficker C peer requests the payload, it is delivered by
spawning a separate thread.&nbsp;&nbsp; Finally, two
additional threads coordinate outbound UDP&nbsp; scanning for
Conficker peers.<br>
<br>
Once these threads are started, C enters a wait mode for any incoming
connection. In response to a TCP connection request, it spawns a thread
to receive a digitally signed payload. The signature check and file
validation are performed in a separate thread.&nbsp; Immediately after
spawning the thread and checking the signature, C starts a different
thread, which uses UDP to advertise that it is a Conficker node that
has a digitally signed payload.&nbsp;&nbsp; When scanning, C avoids
certain IP ranges (including certain assigned /8
netblocks).&nbsp;&nbsp; The /8s not scanned by the P2P protocol are 0,
1, 2, 5, 10, 14, 23, 27, 31, 36, 37, 39, 42,
46, 49, 50, 100, 101, 102, 103, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 127, 175,
176, 177, 178, 179, 180, 181, 182, 183, 184, 185, 191, 197, and 223 -
255.<br>
<br>
An incoming connection is first checked to validate whether the source
IP address matches a pattern similar to the IP validations used by the <a
 href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/domain-generation-algorithm"><span style="color: rgb(0, 0, 0);">Domain
Generation Algorithm</span></a>. This includes the check to ensure that
an IP address does not belong to the list of blacklisted ranges (see <a
 href="index.html#Appendix_2_Domain_Generator_Filtered">Appendix 2</a>).&nbsp;
These checks occur every time a random peer address is generated inside
the UDP scan thread. A similar check is used to ensure that the
digitally signed payload is not delivered through the TCP channel when
a remote host requests it. <br>
<br>
There is a unique mapping from IP address to the two TCP and UDP listen
ports in each host. This helps C avoid the need for supernodes or a
peer list (i.e., C requires no embedded hitlist to locate peers).&nbsp;
This is an important departure from previous malware strains, such as
Storm Worm [<a href="http://www.csl.sri.com/users/porras/addendum/ref-4">4</a>].&nbsp;
It can simply scan the Internet looking for other peers that might be
listening, and from this scanning bootstrap itself into Conficker's P2P
network. However, it is possible that there may in fact be an embedded
seedlist as has been suggested by other researchers (neither method
precludes the other).<br>
<br>
<h3>TCP/UDP P2P Protocol</h3>
Our effort to understand and reconstruct the inner workings of the P2P
protocol's messaging scheme is an ongoing activity.&nbsp; Currently, we
have uncovered several interesting aspects of the P2P behavior based on
observing traffic sent to TCP and UDP listen ports on infected
hosts.&nbsp;&nbsp; <br>
<br>
With respect to the TCP-based communication channel, we have&nbsp;
identified certain segments of the TCP payload.&nbsp; In
particular,&nbsp; the first two bytes appear to form a header.&nbsp;
They correspond to the length of the TCP payload - 2 (size of the
header).<br>
<br>
The UDP-based communications channel employs datagram sizes that are
variable.&nbsp; In <a
 href="http://www.csl.sri.com/users/porras/addendum/index.html#fig-udp-datagram-distro">Figure
4</a>, we plot the distribution of the datagram sizes of the UDP PING
packet, and it appears to follow a power-law distribution with a
minimum value of 20 bytes.&nbsp; C will also produce a handful of
4-byte packets.&nbsp; In some cases, the UDP activity appears to be a
precursor (i.e., a setup) to the TCP activity.&nbsp; There could be
several reasons for this, such as the UDP channel providing
negotiations such as key exchange prior to TCP data exchanges.<br>
<br>
&nbsp;</div>

<h3>P2P File Download Logic</h3>
<div style="text-align: justify;">Among its central functions, the P2P
protocol provides the Conficker overlay network with a secure
peer-based&nbsp; file sharing service.&nbsp;&nbsp; As discussed
previously, drones infected with C can operate as both client and
server with the P2P network.&nbsp;&nbsp; A drone may operate as a
server when its TCP server thread detects that its local temporary
storage directory contains a local file that has been digitally signed;
a separate embedded P2P public key is used make this validation.&nbsp;
If a valid digitally signed file is available, the drone may distribute
this file to other peers.<br>
</div>
<br>
<div style="text-align: justify;">Currently, it appears that the TCP
channel is the preferred connection through which file sharing is
conducted.&nbsp; A file downloaded by a client is subject to digital
signature validation, and if passed the file will be stored in the
local temporary storage directory, thus enabling the server thread to
propagate the binary during future peer exchanges.&nbsp;&nbsp; &nbsp;
Registry keys are modified to enable autorun of downloaded content.<br>
</div>
<br>
<br>
<h2><a name="sec-pseudo-path"></a>Local Host Patch Logic<br>
</h2>
<p style="text-align: justify;">As part of their initialization
procedure, all Conficker variants will perform in-memory alterations
of&nbsp; certain standard Windows API's.&nbsp;&nbsp; Briefly,&nbsp; all
versions of Conficker (A/B/B++/C) patch the <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">NetpwPatchCanonicalize</span>
API from netapi32.dll,&nbsp; <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">DnsQuery</span>
from dnsapi.dll, <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">SendTo</span> from
dnsrslvr.dll, and <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">NtQueryInformationProcess
</span>(for thread obfuscation) from ntdll.dll.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
The&nbsp; allows <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">NetpwPatchCanonicalize</span>
Conficker to protect its host from other malware that would attempt to
reexploit the MS08-067 buffer overflow, while still allowing
reinfection from other Conficker hosts (see <a
 href="http://mtc.sri.com/Conficker/">Extensions to Conficker's
netapi32.dll
Patch</a> for more details).&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; DnsQuery patching allows
Conficker to suppress connections to security companies (and researcher
websites), that may patch and install tools that would otherwise remove
Conficker from the host (see <a href="index.html#dns-prevention">Domain Lookup
Prevention</a>).&nbsp;
<br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">In B++ NetpwPathCanonicalize has been
patched to allow the buffer
overflow to deliver a URL from which a signed binary can be
pulled.&nbsp; In
C, this path has been abandoned in favor of the P2P mechanism,&nbsp;
and the
patch that C applies is more similar to the one applied to B-infected
hosts.</p>
<p>In C, InternetGetConnectedState has been added to the list of
in-memory
patched APIs. The patch consists of making sure that
InternetGetConnectedState is invoked by Conficker code or a module that
has been loaded by the
calling process. If this is not the case, the main thread exits.<br>
</p>
<p><br>
<br>
</p>
<h2><a name="SecurityProductDisablement"></a>Security Product
Disablement</h2>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Conficker C incorporates a
variety of
strategies to secure and defend its installation on the victim
host.&nbsp; To do this, C employs several measures to cloak its
presence, as well as measures to kill or disable security products
that would otherwise detect its presence.&nbsp;&nbsp; C's assault on
security products begins right away, just after its mutex checks (to
detect new installs from reinfections).&nbsp; At each process
initialization, it
performs an
in-memory patch of the host's DNS resolution services to <a
 href="index.html#dns-prevention">prevent domain lookups</a> to a variety of
security product (and research) sites.&nbsp;&nbsp; C then spawns a
separate thread to <a href="index.html#service-disablement">halt and disable
security and update
services</a>,&nbsp; and then enters an infinite loop.&nbsp; There, it
continually
searches for and&nbsp; <a href="index.html#process-termination">terminates
active security products and patches</a>.&nbsp; &nbsp; These
steps are performed each time C is invoked.&nbsp; <br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Upon first installation,&nbsp; C <a
 href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/install-obfuscate">installs itself and obfuscates its presence</a>
on the victim's host,. These steps allow it to avoid easy diagnosis and
removal
by an attentive user. It deletes all restore
points prior to its infection to thwart rollback, and sets NTFS file
permissions on its stored file image to
prevent write and delete privileges.&nbsp; Most of this logic also
appeared in prior version, but here we find some extensions and updates.<br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">C also incorporates logic to <a
 href="index.html#firewall-disablement">disable Windows' firewall protection</a>
of certain high-order UDP and TCP ports.&nbsp; These firewall
adjustments are not performed at initialization, but rather occur when
C enters its network communication logic.<br>
<br>
</p>
<h3><a name="dns-prevention"></a>Domain Lookup Prevention</h3>
<p style="text-align: justify;">At each process initialization,
Conficker C applies an in-memory patch to dnsapi.dll (Windows XP, 2K)
or dnsrslvr.dll (Vista).&nbsp;&nbsp; It does not patch the DLL files on
the filesystem, only their in-memory instances.&nbsp; These DLLs
contain the standard Windows APIs for domain name resolution and
caching.&nbsp; Conficker modifies Window's DNS lookup and cache
services to prevent successful communications with various security
product vendors and research sites.&nbsp; The list of blocked domain
lookups is shown in <a href="index.html#table-1-patched-DNS">Table 1</a>.<br>

<h3><a name="service-disablement"></a>Windows Security Service
Disablement </h3>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Each time it starts, Conficker C spawns
a thread to disable security services and terminate Conficker removal
software.&nbsp;&nbsp; This thread is responsible for disabling Windows
services that deliver security patches and software updates,
effectively
preventing the victim host from receiving automated software
updates.&nbsp; For example, in addition to disabling Windows Defender
and the Windows error reporting service, this logic disables BITS <span
 style="font-style: italic;">(Background Intelligent Transfer
Service). &nbsp; </span>The BITS service is used to prioritize,
throttle, and control asynchronous file transfers between
machines using idle network bandwidth.&nbsp; It is used by the Windows
Update services and other software updaters to stay current with
the latest patches and security hot fixes.<br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;"><a href="index.html#fig-sec-disablement-code">Figure
6</a> provides a pseudo-code summary of the security disablement
thread.&nbsp; The main program logic is shown in function <font
 face="Monospace"><font color="#000000">disable_security_services_and_terminate_conficker_cleaners</font>().&nbsp;<span
 style="font-family: Helvetica,Arial,sans-serif;"> <span
 style="font-family: Helvetica,Arial,sans-serif;">This function
disables </span></span></font><span
 style="font-family: Helvetica,Arial,sans-serif;">Windows Security
Center Service
(wscsvc),&nbsp;
Windows Defender Service (WinDefend), Windows Automatic Update
Service (wuauserv), BITS (Background Intelligent Transfer
Service), Windows Error Reporting Service
(ERSvc), and the Windows Error Reporting
Service (WerSvc).&nbsp; It further deletes Windows Defender from the
Run Registry Key, deactivates security center notifications (</span><font
 style="font-family: Helvetica,Arial,sans-serif; color: rgb(0, 0, 0);"
 face="Monospace">FD6905CE-952F-41F1-9A6F-135D9C6622CC)</font><span
 style="font-family: Helvetica,Arial,sans-serif;">, and deletes the
safeboot security key.&nbsp; It then spawns the
monitor_and_terminate_conficker_cleaners thread, discussed in <a
 href="index.html#process-termination">Security Product Terminator Thread</a>.&nbsp;&nbsp;
<br>
</span></p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">The disable_security_service
pseudo-code is also show in <a href="index.html#fig-sec-disablement-code">Figure
6</a>.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
This function Illustrates the actual logic used to disable the five
security services.
First, C opens the security manager with all access privileges.&nbsp;
It then loops through the set of resident services, ignoring all
services
reported as kernel devices.&nbsp; If it finds a matching device name,
it first shuts down the service, sleeps for 4 seconds, and then sets
the
service
configuration to permanently disable the service.<br>
</p>

<h3><a name="install-obfuscate"></a>Obfuscating C's Installation and
Its Presence<br>
</h3>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Like variants A and B, variant C begins
obfuscating its presence at the moment its (bootstrapping) DLL is
initialized on the
victim host.&nbsp;&nbsp; Upon initialization, the DLL creates
a protected memory segment, and then spawns this segment as a remote
thread to the <span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">netsvcs
</span>or <span style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">explorer
</span>process, depending on the OS.&nbsp;
It sets the SVC display name to nil, does not return from the loadlib
initialization function, and effectively prevents standard Windows
service utilities from listing its DLL as loaded and active.&nbsp; Once
the process is activated, it stores its DLL under a randomly generated
filename (with DLL extension), and sets the date of the DLL to that of
kernel32.dll.&nbsp; The file is then stored on disk using the following
directory selection logic:&nbsp; <br>
</p>
<ol>
  <li>It attempts to
place the DLL in the <span
 style="font-family: Courier New,Courier,monospace;">System32</span>
directory</li>
  <li>It attempts to place the DLL inside the Program
Files directory.&nbsp; Here it attempts to select one of the
following subdirectories:&nbsp;
\\Movie Maker, \\Internet Explorer, \\Windows Media Player,
\\Windows NT</li>
  <li>It places the DLL in the user temp directory<br>
  </li>
</ol>
<p style="text-align: justify;">C must also alter the registry to
ensure that its DLL is reloaded at next boot.&nbsp;&nbsp; To cloak
its registry key settings,&nbsp; C randomly selects and sets various
registry keys to obfuscate&nbsp; the modifications it made to the
svchosts or netsvcs registry segments.<br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">The following strings are
added to the registry to obfuscate svchost's registry configuration
changes:&nbsp; <span style="font-style: italic;">App, Audio, DM, ER,
Event, help, Ias, Ir, Lanman, Net, Ntms, Ras, Remote, Sec, SR, Tapi,
Trk, W32, win, Wmdm, Wmi, wsc, wuau, xml, access, agent, auto, logon,
man, mgmt, mon, prov, serv, Server,&nbsp; Service, Srv, srv, Svc, svc,
System, Time</span>.</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">The following are strings that are
added to the registry to obfuscate netsvcs's registry configuration
changes: <span style="font-style: italic;">Boot, Center, Config,
Driver, Helper, Image, Installer, Manager, Microsoft, Monitor, Network,
Security, Server, Shell, Support, System, Task, Time, Universal,
Update, Windows, Hardware, Control, Audit, Event, Notify, Backup,
Trusted, Component, Framework, Management, Browser, Machine, Logon,
Power, Storage, Discovery, Policy</span>.<br>
<br>
</p>
<h3><a name="firewall-disablement"></a>Firewall Disablement</h3>
<div style="text-align: justify;">To interact with external
clients during P2P communications, C disables the blocking of
several
high-order TCP and UDP
application ports.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is done through HKLM modifications,
where the opened ports are listed in the GloballyOpenPorts registry
key.&nbsp; These ports are fixed per Conficker installation<span
 style="color: rgb(255, 0, 0);"></span>.
&nbsp; The following is an example set of firewall modifications made
during a Conficker C run:<br>
</div>
<p><small>SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\SharedAccess\Parameters\FirewallPolicy\StandardProfile\GloballyOpenPorts\List,
Value Name: 11930:TCP, New Value: 11930:TCP:*:Enabled:PackagesOffice
MSDownloaded&nbsp;</small><br>
</p>
<p><small>SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\SharedAccess\Parameters\FirewallPolicy\StandardProfile\GloballyOpenPorts\List,
Value Name: 45436:TCP, New Value: 45436:TCP:*:Enabled:PackagesOffice
SpeechGames </small><br>
</p>
<p><small>
SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\SharedAccess\Parameters\FirewallPolicy\StandardProfile\GloballyOpenPorts\List,
Value Name: 48481:UDP, New Value: 48481:UDP:*:Enabled:PackagesOffice
PagesPages </small><br>
</p>
<p><small>
SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\SharedAccess\Parameters\FirewallPolicy\StandardProfile\GloballyOpenPorts\List,
Value Name: 57338:UDP, New Value: 57338:UDP:*:Enabled:PackagesOffice
MediaDistribution</small><br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Listed with these firewall port
disablement changes are apparent
product package names, such as MSDownloaded, SpeechGames,
MediaDistribution, and PagesPages.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; These package
names are bogus, and appear to associate these security changes to
software packages that appear benign.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <br>
</p>

<h2><a name="sec-global-impact"></a>Global Network Impact<br>
</h2>
<div style="text-align: justify;"><br>
From nearly the moment of its initial outbreak on 5 March 2009 (UTC),
Conficker C has produced a visible effect on Internet-wide scan
patterns.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Following connection patterns from a series
of ports used across Conficker variants, we are able to track the
spread of C infections from within our honeynet.&nbsp; By
following volume drops in Conficker A/B/B++ TCP/445 scan sources, with
the rise in Conficker
C's TCP and UDP P2P scan activity, we can accurately estimate the
time of release and relative size of Conficker C.&nbsp; <br>
<br>
We believe the primary delivery mechanism used to distribute variant C
has been Internet rendezvous points, which had reportedly been blocked [<a
 href="http://public.mtc.sri.com/Conficker/addendumC/ref-15">15</a>] . &nbsp;&nbsp; Numerous examples of Conficker
C's Win32 <span style="font-style: italic;">dropper </span>executables
have reportedly been delivered into pre-infected Conficker B hosts (we
do not yet have confirmed reports of Conficker A upgrades).&nbsp; When
received by a B host, the <span style="font-style: italic;">dropper </span>application
performs the following actions:<br>
<ol>
  <li>it conducts a self-expiration check and exits if stale (we have
observed 72 hour expirations)</li>
  <li>checks the machine for C-specific mutexes, which would indicate
this machine has already been upgraded. &nbsp; If found, the dropper
exits </li>
  <li>drops a local DLL file into the user's temp directory using a
temporary file name</li>
  <li>invokes the dropped DLL using rundll32.exe, which spawns
Conficker C<br>
  </li>
  <li>deletes itself (the dropper application) from the system<br>
  </li>
</ol>
In <a href="index.html#fig-445tcp-scan-drops">Figure 8</a>, we present
a network traffic analysis that illustrates the impact of Conficker C
upgrades.&nbsp; As shown in this figure, there is a significant drop in
TCP/445 scanning activity, which coincides with&nbsp; the rise in UDP
and TCP P2P
activity from Conficker C.&nbsp; The drop in
TCP/445 activity was corroborated with similar drops in CAIDA's&nbsp;
telescope [<a href="index.html#ref-caida-report">17</a>]. <br>
<br>
&nbsp;<br>
</div>
<hr style="width: 100%; height: 1px;" noshade="noshade">
<div style="text-align: left;"><a name="fig-445tcp-scan-drops"></a><br>
</div>
<p style="text-align: center;"><img style="width: 612px; height: 468px;"

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: bold;">Figure

<span style="font-weight: bold;">dropoffs observed&nbsp; beginning 5

<div style="text-align: justify;">Based on our measurements, the
first&nbsp; wave of Conficker C upgrades began at roughly 6 p.m. PST on
4
March 2009 (2 a.m. GMT on 5 March 2009).&nbsp; As illustrated in <a
 href="index.html#fig-445tcp-scan-drops">Figure 8</a>,

approximately 20% of Conficker hosts.&nbsp; These upgrades occurred in
close proximity to the date change of 5 March 2009 UTC, implying that
when active B machines visited their new set of rendezvous points on
this new day, the Conficker authors had likely established a server
ready to deliver the C dropper application.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <a
 href="index.html#fig-445tcp-scan-drops">Figure 8</a> also illustrates that a
second
upgrade occurred on&nbsp; 17 March 2009&nbsp; (UTC), claiming as much
as another 50%
of the remaining B population. <br>

<h2>In-Situ Analysis - Sandbox Operations<br>
</h2>
<p style="text-align: justify;">We used dynamic sandbox monitoring
techniques to evaluate the interactions of Conficker C when operating
live on the Internet.&nbsp; The release used for this analysis was
monitored and filtered such that it would not cause&nbsp; harm to other
external hosts while these experiments were being conducted. <br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">We describe the network profile of a
Conficker C infected host during a 30-minute sandbox execution.&nbsp;
Since our current experiments were conducted in early March 2009, we
did not
see the HTTP rendezvous point
lookups.&nbsp;
We
expect this activity profile to change on 1 April.&nbsp;&nbsp;
During our pre 1 April sandbox run, we observed the following network
effects, which are illustrated in <a
 href="index.html#fig-net-traffic-profile">Figure

10</a> summarizies a long-running

activity.&nbsp; The DNS activity includes two components: attempts to
contact the 500
rendezvous points, and attempts to contact Internet portals for finding
the date.&nbsp; The trace from this live experimental run corroborates
our static analysis results: each C host contacts 500
rendezvous points each day over 116 TLDs with a flat entropy (random
domain name space).&nbsp; We see a dropoff in overall levels of DNS
activity&nbsp; after
hour 3 when it has looked up the IP addresses of all 500 domains.&nbsp;
In our case, all
these were failed (NXDOMAIN) attempts, as these domains have not yet
been
registered.&nbsp;

The UDP and TCP&nbsp; P2P activity also drops off in the first 2-hours
before settling on a
steady scanning rate. The HTTP date check activity remains a relatively
steady six to nine hosts
contacted per hour. The key implication from the in-situ analysis is
that it should be
fairly easy to fingerprint Conficker C&nbsp; based upon its unique TCP
and UDP
scanning patterns.&nbsp; We
should also be able to identify C hosts (starting 1 April 2009)
based on the volume of
NXDOMAIN responses these hosts would receive for failed DNS lookups.<br>

<h2>Conclusion<br>
</h2>
<p style="text-align: justify;">We present an analysis of Conficker
Variant C, which emerged on the Internet at roughly 6 p.m. (PST) on 4
March 2009.&nbsp; This variant incorporates significant new
functionality,
including a new domain generation algorithm and a new peer-to-peer file
sharing service.&nbsp;&nbsp; Absent from our discussion has been any
reference to the well-known attack propagation vectors (RCP buffer
overflow, USB, and NetBios Scans) that have allowed C's predecessors to
saturate so much of the Internet.&nbsp; Although not present in C,
these attack propagation services are but one peer upload away from any
C infected host, and may appear at any time.&nbsp;&nbsp; C is, in fact,
a robust and secure distribution utility for distributing malicious
content and binaries to millions of computers across the Internet.
&nbsp; This utility incorporates a potent arsenal of methods to defend
itself from security products, updates, and diagnosis tools.&nbsp; It
further demonstrates the rapid development pace at which Conficker's
authors are maintaining their current foothold on a large number of
Internet-connected hosts.&nbsp; Further, if organized into a
coordinated offensive weapon, this multimillion-node botnet poses a
serious and dire threat to the Internet. &nbsp; <br>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">Our report represents one of many
Conficker analysis studies going on throughout the whitehat community,
and we are in direct contact with numerous groups that will produce
additional details, and will help clarify errors that exist in this
report.&nbsp; This report is a living document, and we will update it
regularly, as our understanding of variant C continues to grow.&nbsp;
</p>
