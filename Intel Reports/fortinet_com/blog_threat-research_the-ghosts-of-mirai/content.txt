<div class="cmp cmp-text aem-GridColumn aem-GridColumn--default--12">
  <p><b><a href="https://www.fortinet.com/fortiguard/labs.html?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_medium&#61;campaign&amp;utm_campaign&#61;FortiGuardLabs">FortiGuard Labs</a> Threat Research Report</b></p>
<p><b>Affected Platforms:</b> Linux<br />
<b>Impacted Users:</b>      Any organization<br />
<b>Impact:</b>                     Remote attackers gain control of the vulnerable systems<br />
<b>Severity Level:</b>         Critical</p>
<p>It has been almost five years since the source code of the notorious MIRAI <a href="https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/iot-security?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_campaign&#61;iot-security">IoT</a> <a href="https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/malware?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_campaign&#61;malware">malware</a> was <a href="https://krebsonsecurity.com/2016/10/source-code-for-iot-botnet-mirai-released/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">released</a> to the public by its author in late 2016. This event led to the emergence of numerous copycats, creating their own flavors of IoT botnet armies. Although improvements have been constantly added since then by various threat actors, the structure and goal of the campaigns have remained the same.</p>
<p>IoT malware scans the Internet for IoT devices that use default or weak usernames and passwords. They also seek to exploit known—and sometimes even <a href="https://www.fortinet.com/blog/threat-research/rise-of-one-more-mirai-worm-variant?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_campaign&#61;rise-of-one-more-mirai-worm-variant">zero-day</a>—vulnerabilities to increase their chances of gaining access. And once they do, malicious binaries are downloaded and executed that make the device part of a zombie network that could then be instructed to participate in a Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDOS) attack that could cause a service outage to an unfortunate target. Some threat actors even sell these curated botnets as a service.</p>
<p>We have been closely monitoring the current state of the IoT botnet threat landscape through the perspective of an IoT device with the help of a honeypot system. This article describes our observations over the last few weeks.</p>
<h2>Where are These Attacks Coming From?</h2>
<p>To simulate what it would be like for a new IoT device to be connected to the internet for the first time, we set up a fresh honeypot system to capture what kinds of attacks it would receive. This honeypot was designed to be vulnerable to telnet credential brute force attacks. The statistics in this article were taken from a three-week period.</p>
<p>On average, this honeypot system received around 200 attacks per day, ultimately recording nearly 4700 telnet connections in just three weeks. We were then able to identify nearly 4000 of those attacks and connect them to a Mirai-related malware family. </p>

  <h2>The Enigmatic “Hajime”</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.fortinet.com/blog/industry-trends/fighting-the-evolution-of-malware?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_campaign&#61;fighting-the-evolution-of-malware">Hajime</a> was dubbed as the successor to the first generation of Mirai. Built on the same principle and goals as of its predecessor, it tries to propagate to IOT devices by means of brute-forcing credentials using a password list of common default device passwords. However, unlike Mirai, Hajime utilizes a decentralized peer-to-peer network to issue commands to its bots. This makes it much harder to locate the Command-and-Control (C2) server for a takedown.</p>
<p>Aside from its sophisticated bot network communication, it is also one of the most mysterious variants due to its vague intentions. Commands sent to Hajime bots are in the form of structured messages that are passed along in the peer-to-peer network. One of these commands instruct bots to download and execute binaries, internally called &#34;modules&#34;. Only the spreading module has been observed being served in the wild. No attack or disruptive modules have been observed, and Hajime has never been associated with any disruption attacks. Furthermore, part of its behavior is to block access to ports that are commonly targeted by other IoT malware, thereby inadvertently (or not) somewhat protecting the infected device from further infections.</p>
<p>Lastly, it delivers the following message to the device’s terminal:</p>
<p><b><i>Just a <a title="white hat" href="https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/whitehat-security?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_medium&#61;blog&amp;utm_campaign&#61;blog-whitehat-security">white hat</a>, securing some systems.</i></b></p>
<p><b><i>Important messages will be signed like this!</i></b></p>
<p><b><i>Hajime Author.</i></b></p>
<p><b><i>Contact CLOSED Stay sharp!</i></b></p>
<p>It was only a matter of time before some speculated that Hajime might be the work of a real vigilante.</p>
<h2>SYLVEON Coming Out of Retirement?</h2>
<p>What surprised us more was the appearance of the SYLVEON variant on the table. In mid-2019 there was a 14-year old European IoT malware author that went by the name of “Light The Sylveon” and “Light The Leafeon”. </p>
<p>When we took quick look at the decrypted strings of one of the binaries we captured, the word “Leafeon” was found, creating speculation that this might be the author’s comeback.</p>

  <h2>SORA - The Surviving Member of the Wicked Family</h2>
<p>It is also interesting to see Mirai variants that were authored by the threat actor known as <i>Wicked</i> that we <a href="https://www.fortinet.com/blog/threat-research/a-wicked-family-of-bots?utm_source&#61;blog&amp;utm_campaign&#61;a-wicked-family-of-bots">covered</a> three years ago. These variants include Owari, Omni, Wicked, and SORA. Based on an interview at that time, the author stated he was going to focus on Owari and Omni while abandoning the other two variants, including SORA. Based on our observastions, it seems that SORA has more successfully survived than its siblings.</p>

  <h2>Conclusion </h2>
<p>As the number of installed IoT devices continues to explode, especially given the current lack of security standards available to protect them, IoT will be a hotbed for malware operations for the foreseeable future, as we have demonstrated in this article. And interestingly, Mirai variants are still very active in terms of attack and development.</p>
<p><b>Solutions</b></p>
<p>Every artifact collected from our honeypot systems and other sources are automatically processed to ensure that our customers are protected from these attacks. That said, the following precautions are highly recommended:</p>
<ul><li>As credential brute-forcing is still the primary way malwares get into IoT devices, setting usernames and passwords that are difficult to guess can go a long way towards securing them. </li><li>In addition, to protect against known vulnerabilities, always keep device software up to date.</li></ul>
<p>Fortinet customers are protected by the following:</p>
<ul><li>Related samples are detected by FortiGuard Antivirus</li><li>Downloaded URLs and identified C2s are blocked by FortiGuard Web Filtering Service.</li><li>Mentioned exploit attacks are detected using the following IPS signatures:<ul><li>CVE-2021-22986 - <a href="https://www.fortiguard.com/encyclopedia/ips/50002" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">F5.iControl.REST.Interface.Remote.Command.Execution</a></li><li>CVE-2009-4490<b> </b><span>- </span><a href="https://www.fortiguard.com/encyclopedia/ips/37660" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Acme.thttpd.and.minihttpd.Command.Injection.Vulnerability</a></li><li>CVE-2018-10088 - <a href="https://www.fortiguard.com/encyclopedia/ips/46264" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">XiongMai.uc-httpd.Buffer.Overflow</a></li><li>CVE-2020-28188<b> </b><span>- </span><a href="https://www.fortiguard.com/encyclopedia/ips/49716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TerraMaster.TOS.Makecvs.PHP.Unauthenticated.Command.Execution</a> </li><li>CVE-2020-25506 - <a href="https://www.fortiguard.com/encyclopedia/ips/31320" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">D-Link.ShareCenter.Products.CGI.Code.Execution</a> </li><li>CVE-2020-29957 -
