class="oj ok gu bf ol om qc hz oo op qd ic or os qe ou ov ow qf oy oz pa qg pc pd pe bk">What is Stealthy, Anyway?</h1><p id="9f66" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">When it comes to network traffic, the best approach we can take to remain “stealthy” is to try to blend in with existing traffic. By far, HTTP(S) is the most popular callback method for beaconing malware for three reasons:</p><ol class=""><li id="bcd0" class="nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi qh qi qj bk"><strong class="np he"><em class="qk">It’s easy to implement</em></strong> — Simple, text-based, flexible protocol with many available libraries</li><li id="f757" class="nn no gu np b hx ql nr ns ia qm nu nv nw qn ny nz oa qo oc od oe qp og oh oi qh qi qj bk"><strong class="np he"><em class="qk">It’s likely to be open outbound </em></strong>— Browser-based workflows necessitate HTTP(S) outbound</li><li id="4815" class="nn no gu np b hx ql nr ns ia qm nu nv nw qn ny nz oa qo oc od oe qp og oh oi qh qi qj bk"><strong class="np he"><em class="qk">There is a ton of existing traffic </em></strong>— Our traffic will be a needle in a haystack</li></ol><p id="e189" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">As long as the contents of our HTTP(S) messages look similar to real web traffic (a.k.a., our “C2 profile”) then we should be able to blend in and remain “stealthy”. But, there is a catch:</p><p id="9061" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk"><em class="qk">“Everything is stealthy until someone is looking for it.”</em> <a class="af qq" href="https://twitter.com/tifkin_" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">— @tifkin_</a></p><p id="09e5" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">However you chose to structure your messages, there may be telltales that could give away your particular brand of malware in the future. If you don’t write your own C2 profile and just use an open-source one, then it is likely that some security products have already fingerprinted your malware’s communication style.</p><p id="fcad" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">In addition to fingerprinting C2 profiles, network defenders have a few more ways to block a lot of unknown malicious traffic, and cause a headache for red teams:</p><ol class=""><li id="7d20" class="nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi qh qi qj bk">Blocking traffic to new or uncategorized domains</li><li id="d997" class="nn no gu np b hx ql nr ns ia qm nu nv nw qn ny nz oa qo oc od oe qp og oh oi qh qi qj bk">Blocking traffic to any domain that is new to the target network (first visits)</li><li id="95e4" class="nn no gu np b hx ql nr ns ia qm nu nv nw qn ny nz oa qo oc od oe qp og oh oi qh qi qj bk">Only allowing traffic to a narrow set of websites required to do business (allow listing)</li></ol><p id="d05f" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">Bypassing block number one is easy. Just host some benign content on your domain until it is a few months old and categorized. Blocks two and three are a bit more challenging to bypass. Let’s look at a few options that will likely still work even in the case of domain allow listing and that are also somewhat “stealthy”.</p></div></div></div><div class="ab cb pu pv pw px" role="separator"><span class="py by bm pz qa qb"></span><span class="py by bm pz qa qb"></span><span class="py by bm pz qa"></span></div><div class="gn go gp gq gr"><div class="ab cb"><div class="ci bh fz ga gb gc"><h1 id="c760" class="oj ok gu bf ol om qc hz oo op qd ic or os qe ou ov ow qf oy oz pa qg pc pd pe bk">The Classic: DNS</h1><p id="0e32" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">DNS has long been the preferred choice for red teams as a long-haul C2 callback. Like HTTP(S), it is extremely likely that DNS traffic is allowed outbound. There is also a ton of existing traffic to blend in. It only comes with two caveats:</p><ol class=""><li id="5aeb" class="nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi qh qi qj bk"><strong class="np he"><em class="qk">Size limits</em></strong> — We only get <a class="af qq" href="https://devblogs.microsoft.com/oldnewthing/20120412-00/?p=7873" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">253 characters max</a> per request to work with</li><li id="e5ef" class="nn no gu np b hx ql nr ns ia qm nu nv nw qn ny nz oa qo oc od oe qp og oh oi qh qi qj bk"><strong class="np he"><em class="qk">Case insensitive </em></strong>— We will need to add additional bytes to <a class="af qq" href="https://gist.github.com/dtmsecurity/16728513bb92fe7bdec532be426ef17a" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">encode</a> data that isn’t lowercase alphanumeric</li></ol><p id="130c" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">DNS responses can be up to 512 bytes, but that’s still a major limitation. What this means is that most DNS C2 profiles break up the call and response data into small chunks and generate a very high volume of DNS requests over short periods. In addition, most DNS C2 profiles are designed to be “efficient” and use all of the available bytes for each request, which generates requests that don’t look normal at all compared to regular DNS lookups. Keeping this in mind, here’s some tips on how to use DNS effectively, while staying stealthy:</p><p id="c0e3" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">First, I would recommend only using DNS for check-ins, or other communications that require very small data transfers. In general, try to stay well under the 253 character limit.</p><p id="088a" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">Second, design post-exploitation commands that require very little output. For instance, one command might simply retrieve a host name, and let you know if the implant can load a webpage on that host. Another command might then instruct the implant to call back to that host over HTTPS.</p><p id="b9b8" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">Finally, remember that DNS caching is a thing. That’s because normal ECHO Request (PING) and ECHO Reply messages always have a data payload of ‘abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwabcdefghi’. Why? I have no idea. Just know that fake messages are easy to spot so ICMP tends to be all or nothing when used for C2 traffic.</p><h1 id="093a" class="oj ok gu bf ol om on hz oo op oq ic or os ot ou ov ow ox oy oz pa pb pc pd pe bk">You’ve Got Mail(ware): C2 Over SMTP</h1><p id="d5d7" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">One of my favorite tricks to help my implants blend in is to use <a class="af qq" href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/com/the-component-object-model" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">Microsoft Component Object Model</a>s (COMs) to drive legitimate Windows binaries to do my bidding; for example, creating a hidden instance of <a class="af qq" href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/internet-explorer/ie-developer/platform-apis/aa752084(v=vs.85)" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">Internet Explorer</a> and using it to navigate to my C2 domain to make my HTTPS connections look like they are normal web traffic.</p><p id="3617" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">On networks that restrict outbound access over HTTP(S), we can use this same trick to exfiltrate data over email (SMTP) instead using Outlook’s <a class="af qq" href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/microsoft.office.interop.outlook.application?view=outlook-pia" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">COM interface</a>. If you get fancy with setting some <a class="af qq" href="https://sensepost.com/blog/2016/mapi-over-http-and-mailrule-pwnage/" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">custom email rules</a> for the compromised user, you could write an implant that uses only SMTP for C2 communications.</p><p id="d43a" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">One major advantage to this approach would be that you aren’t using HTTP(S) traffic at all. No matter how locked down the corporate web proxy is, if the compromised user can send and receive emails, then your implant will likely be able to call home.</p><p id="92ff" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">In addition, you wouldn’t need the implant to Beacon at all. Your implant could just sit and wait for special emails to come in with its next instructions.</p><p id="d977" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">The last major upside to this C2 method is that data is exchanged between email addresses and not C2 domains, making it much easier to make changes on the fly. For instance, instructions could come from multiple different email addresses. Outputs could be sent to multiple destination addresses using email CC or BCC just in case one email address gets blocked. If you used disposable addresses, like Gmail accounts, you wouldn’t even need to purchase a C2 domain at all.</p><h1 id="007b" class="oj ok gu bf ol om on hz oo op oq ic or os ot ou ov ow ox oy oz pa pb pc pd pe bk">Old Dog, New Transport: DNS Over TLS and HTTPS (DoT and DoH)</h1><p id="72e2" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">The DNS protocol does not support encryption, which means that all of your DNS requests, “<a class="af qq" href="https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/dns/dns-over-tls/" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">can be read by networks, ISPs, or anybody able to monitor transmissions</a>”. To address this issue, DNS over TLS (DoT), and DNS over HTTPS (DoH) are now commonplace alternatives for traditional DNS. Most web browsers these days use DoH as an added security feature (or to enact exclusive access to <a class="af qq" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m1mhaC5NH7s&amp;t=1465s" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">monitor you</a>). That means that most networks will have a decent volume of legitimate DoH traffic for us to blend in. DoT is an okay option, but requires additional lift to implement in an implant. DoT also uses port 853 instead of the traditional 53, which might be blocked outbound on restrictive networks. DoH, on the other hand, just uses port 443/TCP. It is DNS exposed as a web API. Here’s a couple of examples:</p><p id="06ab" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk"><a class="af qq" href="https://dns.google/resolve?name=example.com" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">https://dns.google/resolve?name=example.com</a></p><pre class="pn po pp pq pr qs qt qu bp qv bb bk"><span id="9c6c" class="qw ok gu qt b bg qx qy l qz ra">curl -H &quot;accept: application/dns-json&quot; &quot;https://cloudflare-dns.com/dns-query?name=example.com&amp;type=A&quot;</span></pre><p id="4660" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">Even if our target network is utilizing domain allow listing, what are the odds that “dns.google”, or “cloudflare-dns.com”, or both are on the list? I’d say pretty high. We still have DNS size limitations to deal with, but get some major advantages over a traditional DNS callback. First, our requests are encrypted. We can safely go back to generating extremely anomalous 253 byte DNS queries. Second, our implant is no longer sending raw DNS requests. HTTPS is going to look a lot more normal comparatively. Finally, we can implement a DNS callback without needing a DNS library. The DNS protocol is complex, but these DoH APIs are extremely simple.</p><h1 id="7cb8" class="oj ok gu bf ol om on hz oo op oq ic or os ot ou ov ow ox oy oz pa pb pc pd pe bk">LOTS of Options: Living Off Trusted Sites</h1><p id="d963" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">Speaking of sites that are likely to be on a corporate allow list, there are plenty of “trusted sites” we might be able to leverage to broker messages between our implants and our C2 server. This technique is known as living off trusted sites, or LOTS for short. The idea is that there are some sites that are used so commonly by businesses, that blocking them would be prohibitive for most organizations. Many of those same sites allow users to post or store data in some capacity, where it can subsequently be retrieved. You can therefore leverage the reputation of these sites to get data in and out of your target network. Here’s a great list curated by <a class="af qq" href="https://twitter.com/mrd0x" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">@mrd0x</a>:</p><p id="61cf" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk"><a class="af qq" href="https://lots-project.com/" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">https://lots-project.com/</a></p><p id="49c8" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">You can use the “C&amp;C” filter to find sites that are particularly suited for use as C2 message brokers. You might also find a few good places to host a static phishing site or deliver a payload!</p><h1 id="d03d" class="oj ok gu bf ol om on hz oo op oq ic or os ot ou ov ow ox oy oz pa pb pc pd pe bk">Swimming Upstream: STUN and TURN</h1><p id="a54b" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">If you’ve never heard of Session Traversal Utilities for NAT (<a class="af qq" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STUN" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">STUN</a>) or Traversal Using Relays around NAT (<a class="af qq" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traversal_Using_Relays_around_NAT" rel="noopener ugc nofollow" target="_blank">TURN</a>), then you are not alone. Not many red teamers need to interact with these protocols or even know what they are. These protocols are the backbone for most web based audio and video streaming services. Think Discord, GoToMeeting, Facebook Messenger, and Google Hangouts to name a few. All these services have one challenge in common: How do we connect two (or more) devices so they can stream data between each other when they are both behind firewalls? That’s where STUN and TURN come into play. Note that they both have “traversal” and “NAT” (A.K.A firewall) in the name. These protocols allow devices to set up peer connections even when they are behind a firewall. It’s kind of like poking a little hole in the firewall so we can talk efficiently with someone else who also poked a hole in theirs.</p><figure class="pn po pp pq pr ps pk pl paragraph-image"><div All of these factors make them a potential blind spot for many organizations.</p><h1 id="dccd" class="oj ok gu bf ol om on hz oo op oq ic or os ot ou ov ow ox oy oz pa pb pc pd pe bk">Flow Like Water: Mixed Callbacks</h1><p id="c5c8" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx pf nr ns ia pg nu nv nw ph ny nz oa pi oc od oe pj og oh oi gn bk">I like to think about C2 communications as sort of like sharing notes back in grade school. There may be many paths a note might take from A to B; maybe between a mutual friend or mutual friends. Maybe you use an agreed upon drop box location (maybe multiple), or implement your own special code. In any case, the path the note (data) traverses doesn’t matter as long as it gets from A to B and back again. So when I apply this viewpoint to C2 communications, I prefer to break up the problem into two questions:</p><ol class=""><li id="874b" class="nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi qh qi qj bk">How can I get data into the organization from the outside?</li><li id="5821" class="nn no gu np b hx ql nr ns ia qm nu nv nw qn ny nz oa qo oc od oe qp og oh oi qh qi qj bk">How can I send data from inside the organization to the outside?</li></ol><p id="da8c" class="pw-post-body-paragraph nn no gu np b hx nq nr ns ia nt nu nv nw nx ny nz oa ob oc od oe of og oh oi gn bk">The answer to each question doesn’t need to match. They just have to be individually valid answers. You could receive instructions from a shared Google Doc, and post the output to a private GitHub repository. You could perform an initial check-in over SMTP, send regular follow-up check-ins over DNS, pull instructions over DoH, and exfiltrate output using a “video feed” over STUN. You could even use a mix of methods for any one step, either in parallel, or in round-robin fashion.
