<h1 class="blog-banner__heading">
       Fork in the Ice: The New Era of IcedID
  </h1>

    </div>
  </div>

      <span class="blog-content__date">  <time datetime="2023-03-27T10:00:00Z">March 27, 2023</time>

</span>
        <span class="blog-content__author">
                Pim Trouerbach, Kelsey Merriman and Joe Wise
  
        </span>
    </div>
    <div class="node-full__body blog-content__body">  <p class="MsoNormal">​<strong>Key Findings</strong></p>

<ul>
	<li>Proofpoint is tracking new variants of IcedID used by at least three threat actors.</li>
	<li>Initial analysis suggests this is a forked version with potentially a separate panel for managing the malware.</li>
	<li>While much of the code base is the same, there are several key differences.</li>
	<li>One key difference is the removal of banking functionality such as web injects and backconnect.</li>
	<li>Proofpoint researchers hypothesize the original operators behind Emotet are using an IcedID variant with different functionality.</li>
</ul>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Overview</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint researchers have observed and documented, for the first time, three distinct variants of the malware known as IcedID. Proofpoint calls the two new variants recently identified “Forked” and “Lite” IcedID. This report details the following variants of IcedID:</p>

<ul>
	<li><strong>Standard IcedID Variant</strong> – The variant most commonly observed in the threat landscape and used by a variety of threat actors.</li>
	<li><strong>Lite IcedID Variant</strong> – New variant observed as a follow-on payload in November Emotet infections that does not exfiltrate host data in the loader checkin and a bot with minimal functionality.</li>
	<li><strong>Forked IcedID Variant</strong> – New variant observed by Proofpoint researchers in February 2023 used by a small number of threat actors which also delivers the bot with minimal functionality.</li>
</ul>

<p class="MsoNormal">IcedID is a malware originally classified as a banking malware and was first observed in 2017. It also acts as a loader for other malware, including ransomware.&nbsp;&nbsp;As previously <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">published</a>, historically there has been just one version of IcedID that has remained constant since 2017. The well-known IcedID version consists of an initial loader which contacts a Loader C2 server, downloads the standard DLL Loader, which then delivers the standard IcedID Bot.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">In November 2022, Proofpoint researchers observed the first new variant of IcedID Proofpoint dubbed “IcedID Lite” distributed as a follow-on payload in a <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">TA542</a> Emotet campaign. It was dropped by the Emotet malware soon after the actor returned to the e-crime landscape after a nearly four-month break.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The IcedID Lite Loader observed in November 2022 contains a static URL to download a “Bot Pack” file with a static name (botpack.dat) which results in the IcedID Lite DLL Loader, and then delivers the Forked version of IcedID Bot, leaving out the webinjects and backconnect functionality that would typically be used for banking fraud.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Starting in February 2023, Proofpoint observed the new Forked variant of IcedID. To date, Proofpoint has uncovered seven campaigns using the Forked IcedID variant. This variant was distributed by TA581 and one unattributed threat activity cluster which acted as initial access facilitators. The campaigns used a variety of email attachments such as Microsoft OneNote attachments and somewhat rare to see .URL attachments, which led to the Forked variant of IcedID.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The IcedID Forked Loader, first observed in February 2023, is more <a name="_Int_Q117qi4y">similar to</a> the Standard IcedID Loader in that it contacts a Loader C2 server to retrieve the DLL loader and bot. That DLL loader has similar artifacts to the Lite Loader, <a name="_Int_2dLqcDo6">and also</a> loads the Forked IcedID Bot.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The following picture shows the high-level overview of the various IcedID variants Proofpoint researchers have identified.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329165543-1" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="a35e7f1d-5c43-4b21-8afb-53e0cec0f0da" height="348" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329165543-1.png" width="936" loading="lazy"><em>Figure&nbsp; 1: Overview of the three IcedID variants.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Threat Actor Details</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint has identified hundreds of IcedID campaigns from 2022 through 2023, and at least five threat actors were observed directly distributing this malware in campaigns since 2022. Nearly all threat actors and unattributed threat activity clusters use the Standard IcedID variant. Proofpoint considers most of these threat actors to be initial access brokers that <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/first-step-initial-access-leads-ransomware">facilitate infections leading to ransomware</a>.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint continues to see all variants of the IcedID malware in campaign data, so researchers assess with high confidence that the changes detailed below are not direct upgrades to the Standard IcedID codebase. It is likely a cluster of threat actors is using modified variants to pivot the malware away from typical banking trojan and banking fraud activity to focus on payload delivery, which likely includes prioritizing ransomware delivery. Additionally, based on artifacts observed in the codebase, timing and association with Emotet infections, Proofpoint researchers suspect the initial developers of Emotet have partnered with IcedID operators to expand their activities including using the new Lite variant of IcedID that has different, unique functionality and likely testing it via existing Emotet infections.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Lite IcedID variant has <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">only been observed</a> following TA542 Emotet infections, but Proofpoint cannot definitively attribute the Lite variant to TA542 as follow-on infections are typically outside of researchers’ visibility. The following are threat actors frequently associated with IcedID.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>TA578 </strong>– Proofpoint has observed TA578 deliver IcedID in campaigns since June 2020. Typically, this actor uses email themes such as “stolen images” or “copyright violation” to deliver malware. In addition to IcedID, TA578 also frequently conducts campaigns delivering Bumblebee malware. TA578 uses the Standard IcedID variant.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>TA551 </strong>– Proofpoint has observed TA551 deliver IcedID in campaigns since November 2018. This actor usually uses thread hijacking to typically deliver attached files including Word documents, PDFs, and recently, OneNote documents. TA551 has used multiple malware types, with recent payloads including IcedID, SVCReady, and Ursnif. TA551 uses the Standard IcedID variant.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>TA577 </strong>– Proofpoint has observed TA577 use IcedID in limited campaigns since February 2021. This actor typically uses thread hijacking to deliver malware, with Qbot being TA577’s preferred payload. However, Proofpoint has observed IcedID delivered by TA577 in six campaigns since 2022. TA577 uses the Standard IcedID variant.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong><a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/security-briefs/ta544-targets-italian-organizations-ursnif-malware">TA544</a></strong> – Proofpoint observed TA544 use IcedID in limited campaigns throughout 2022. This actor typically targets organizations in Italy and Japan, and typically delivers Ursnif malware. TA544 uses the Standard IcedID variant.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>TA581 </strong>– TA581 is a newly classified threat actor Proofpoint has tracked as an unattributed activity cluster since mid-2022. This actor typically uses business-relevant themes such as payroll, customer information, invoice, and order receipts to deliver a variety of filetypes or URLs. TA581 typically delivers IcedID, but has been observed using Bumblebee malware and telephone-oriented attack delivery (<a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/caught-beneath-landline-411-telephone-oriented-attack-delivery">TOAD</a>) payloads. TA581 uses the Forked IcedID variant.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Campaign Details</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint has only observed the IcedID Lite Loader variant delivered as a second-stage payload following Emotet infections associated with <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">November 2022 campaigns</a>. Below are examples of the Standard and Forked IcedID variants observed as first-stage payloads.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Example 1: IcedID Standard Campaign</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint observed a campaign with over 2,800 messages on 10 March 2023. This campaign began with thread hijacked emails which contained HTML attachments. The HTML attachments used HTML Smuggling to drop a password protected, zipped Windows Script File (WSF). The password “747” was displayed in the HTML file. The WSF ran a VBScript which initiated a PowerShell command to download and execute an intermediate script which then downloaded and executed the Standard IcedID Loader using a non-standard export “init”.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Example 2: IcedID Forked Campaign</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint observed a campaign with over 13,000 messages on 3 February 2023. This campaign began with invoice-themed email lures requesting confirmation from the recipient to manage a contract. The emails were personalized to the recipient by using the recipient’s name in the greeting of the email. The observed emails contained the subject "How can i contact you?" with an attachment&nbsp;name (regex): "unpaid_[0-9]{4}-February-03\.one".</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">These messages contained Microsoft OneNote attachments (.one). When opened, the OneNote document instructed the recipient to "open" the document by double-clicking the button displayed in the OneNote document. An HTML Application (HTA) file was concealed beneath the "open" text which, if clicked, executed the HTA file. The HTA file initiated a PowerShell command used to download and execute an IcedID loader. The IcedID loader was executed with&nbsp;rundll32 using a non-standard export: "PluginInit". The PowerShell command also downloaded and opened a decoy PDF.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Example 3: IcedID Forked Campaign</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint observed a campaign with over 200 messages conducted from 20 February to 23 February 2023. This campaign included two different email lures: 1) a recall notice purporting to be from the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act; and 2) a violation purporting to be from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The emails contained .URL attachments.&nbsp;A URL file is&nbsp;a shortcut that points to a specific Uniform Resource Locator.&nbsp;If the recipient clicked to open the .URL file, the recipient's default web browser would access the URL contained in the file. If the .URL file was opened&nbsp;it would initiate the download of a batch (.bat) file. The batch file would download and execute an IcedID loader with&nbsp;rundll32 using a non-standard export: "PluginInit".</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Malware analysis</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Before comparing the Standard Loader to the Forked Loader, it is worth covering the highlights of the IcedID Lite Loader as there is code overlap and clear similarities when compared with the Forked Loader. For an in-depth analysis of the Lite Loader, check out Proofpoint’s previous report <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">here</a>. The Lite Loader’s purpose is to download the next stage of the malware from a hardcoded domain and URI path. The domain is decrypted from the configuration and the URI path is decrypted within the function that makes the HTTP request. Unlike the Standard IcedID Loader, there is no host information being exfiltrated within the request. When the Lite Loader was dropped on Emotet infections, that fact made sense, since this version of IcedID was specifically being deployed on already infected machines, and there was no need to check the host information.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">As far as behavior is concerned, the Forked Loader functions the same as the Standard Loader. The goal is to send host info to the loader C2, then to gate the bot download. This gating mechanism is to ensure that only truly infected machines get the bot binary vs researchers or malware sandboxes. If the checks are passed, the C2 will return the encrypted bot and DLL loader which is where the real capabilities of the botnet emerge. The differences come within the binary itself by how the code is/was structured and how they obfuscate the sample. Both variants of the loader initiate their malicious code by creating a thread for the malware main. Before this happens though, the Forked Loader decrypts and copies strings into global variables where they will be later used to resolve required functions. This pattern of decrypting strings for future use will come up later in the analysis of the DLL loader.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171007-7" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="c2c006aa-ed07-4ade-a781-b4f4c7f1f251" height="694" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171007-7.png" width="540" loading="lazy"></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><em>Figure 17: String decryption of the DLL names used within the Forked Loader.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">With the DLL strings decrypted, the malware then decrypts the loader configuration by taking the first 64 bytes and XORing it against the next 64 bytes. The first four bytes of the decrypted buffer will contain the project identifier (ID) (a campaign identifier of sorts) and then a singular domain which is used to gate the download of the bot.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171023-8" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="ccef3599-358a-43ad-8b75-34c76dee39a0" height="728" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171023-8.png" width="814" loading="lazy"><br>
<em>Figure 18: Decryption of the config buffer in the Forked Loader.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For whatever reason, there is an extraneous “1.bin” that is appended to a string which isn’t used. As far as Proofpoint researchers can tell, this string is not used and serves no purpose. With the config decrypted, the malware creates the cookies that contain the host information and sends an HTTP request that will contain the encrypted bot response.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171038-9" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="39bf4582-42b5-4d98-82f1-e287a42d37ea" height="280" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171038-9.png" width="624" loading="lazy"></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><em>Figure 19:&nbsp;Raw response from the loader C2 containing the encrypted bot and DLL loader.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The response gets decrypted with the IcedID decryption routine, then split into the encrypted bot (being “license.dat”) and the custom DLL loader which is generally some randomly generated filename ending in .tmp.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Next, a function is called that decrypts strings that are not used at any point within the binary itself. The function starts by creating a structure that is going to be returned at the end of the function. This structure contains two domains and various URIs that could potentially be used to get a separate version of the bot.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171231-14" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="f44363d8-4bff-4fb8-9d62-b3f8aa8383db" height="714" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171231-14.png" width="728" loading="lazy"><br>
<em>Figure 24: Decryption of “Lite Loader”" domains.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For all the Forked DLL Loader variants we have seen, there are two domains that are decrypted: “tourdeworldsport[.]com” and “handsinworld[.]com”. Neither of these domains are used within the file, and at the time of this report have no <a name="_Int_PhkEGq0a">relations</a> on VirusTotal. Looking into the “handsinworld” domain, passive DNS shows that the domain started resolving to its current IP of “193[.]37[.]69[.]107” on 12 Nov 2022. This is also around the time that Emotet dropped the IcedID Lite Loader onto the Epoch 4 and Epoch 5 botnet. More information on the Lite Loader and Emotet can be found in our previous report <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">here</a>. The other domain “tourdeworldsport”, also started resolving to the IP “5[.]61[.]34[.]46” on 18 Nov 2022.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">With the domain names decrypted, the DLL Loader decrypts 10 strings that should be URIs to be appended to the domains.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171245-15" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="f8d068d6-c9c9-41dd-b228-c88550b25f39" height="656" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171245-15.png" width="632" loading="lazy"><br>
<em>Figure 25: Decryption of “Lite Loader” filenames.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Within this list though, they have typos for botpackn3dat. Most likely there should be a period before .dat. This is the same URI structure (/botpack.dat) that the Lite Loader used to download the bot and DLL loader from the C2 in November 2022 when it was dropped via Emotet infections.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">After the strings are decrypted, the structure referencing them is never used again. This is most likely code that has been copy/pasted from the lite loader. If implemented correctly, these strings should appear in the actual loader of IcedID and not within the DLL Loader where it currently resides. These commonalities between the Lite Loader and this DLL Loader make it seem as if the same group that dropped IcedID via Emotet is behind these campaigns as well.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Looking at the Forked IcedID Bot variant and the Standard Bot variant in BinDiff, researchers observed that the Standard IcedID bot contains more functionality than the Forked variant.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329165812-9" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="b9984ab9-b2e2-43b1-965d-02f5434b9baa" height="392" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329165812-9.png" width="936" loading="lazy"><em>Figure 26: Output of BinDiff showing the Standard Bot vs the Forked Bot.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Combining Hexray’s Lumina and BinDiff shows that the Standard Bot contains functionality relating to web injects, adversary in the middle (AiTM) and backconnect capabilities that do not exist within the Forked variant. This could be because banking fraud has become increasingly more difficult over the last couple of years.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329165741-8" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="d308a344-2503-48a9-bc11-870a7aed505c" height="868" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329165741-8.png" width="520" loading="lazy"></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><em>Figure 27: Functions that have been removed within the Forked Bot.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Within the communications of the bot, there is an authentication header which contains the bot’s project ID, some other details and the version of the bot.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Authorization: Basic OTk4MDc1MzAwOjA6MTE5OjY1OjM1</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; 998075300:0:119:65:35</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Base64 decoding this value gives up the version as the third component of the list. For the Standard IcedID Bot, this value is set to 119 as seen above, but for the Forked variant, we get the following base64 decoded header;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; 998075300:0:111:67:1</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This value contains version 111, which could indicate the fork happened when the Standard Bot was using that version.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Finally, there seems to be a bug within the Forked variant of the bot where the URIs of specific requests are not constructed properly which causes 404s to occur.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Lite Loader Anomaly</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">After analysis of the separate variants was finished, Proofpoint identified a file called “botpackn1.dat” on <a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/7c8b3b8cf2b721568b96f58e5994b8ddb8990cd05001be08631ade7902ae6262">VirusTotal</a> that seemed related to our Lite Loader.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171441-18" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="a1ded546-6edf-4939-9383-4889819d8b80" height="708" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171441-18.png" width="936" loading="lazy"><br>
<em>Figure 29: VirusTotal page showing the botpack used in the Lite Loader.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That filename is embedded within the custom Forked DLL Loader mentioned previously. This relationship was enough to prompt further analysis. In the <a href="https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/comprehensive-look-emotets-fall-2022-return">article</a> where Proofpoint described the IcedID Lite Loader being dropped via Emotet infections, researchers documented the structure of the botpack format and how to decrypt it as well. Taking that same script and applying it to this file leads to a valid configuration where researchers can analyze the configuration.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><br>
<img alt="image-20230329171418-17" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="9936c072-08e8-4f2d-8cd2-2e3a9ff03513" height="194" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171418-17.png" width="796" loading="lazy"><em>Figure 30: Commandline output showing the decrypted botpack structure.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This botpackn1.dat contains the later stages of the infection chain, so with some pivoting on the VirusTotal relationships, researchers land on the distribution URL (<a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/url/e6426ab8889d3284160de0c785342bda6c8b47835cc16ec085e7e625adf71d21">VT Link</a>) “http[:]//lepriconloots[.]com/botpackn1.dat”. Pivoting again to find files that reach to the URL, we come across the Lite Loader <a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/4871d83c32ce40c24171ec40c4548dd320fe183a58d3866aa88c0b12d2d7b3ae">sample</a> itself. Looking at the build artifacts of this sample, it seems like the threat actors have removed the PDB path, but the Lite Loader still contains the build name “Loader.dll”. Loader.dll was initially used within IcedID to refer to the Lite Loader back when it was dropped via Emotet infections in November, but that same build name is now being used within the Forked DLL Loader. This could mean the codebase is similar enough where the threat actors can interchange the loader and the DLL Loader, or that these actors are copy/pasting extraneous code.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><img alt="image-20230329171357-16" data-entity-type="file" data-entity-uuid="4cccf708-5b05-4f1c-8462-fe1996071a56" height="432" src="/sites/default/files/inline-images/image-20230329171357-16.png" width="574" loading="lazy"><br>
<em>Figure 31: Embedded build name of the DLL Loader.</em></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Finally, pivoting on where this “c2.dll” (IcedID Lite loader) came from, the distribution URL “http[:]//104[.]156[.]149[.]6/webdav/c2.dll” is observed. Similarly, this IP address hosted an IcedID campaign from TA581 that occurred on 21 February 2023. The TA581 campaign ended up loading the DLL “host.dll” from that same directory and led to one of the first campaigns of Proofpoint observing the Forked variant. At the time when this distribution URL was live the IP was hosting an open directory on /webdav/ that contained various bat files, a forked IcedID loader as well as this lite loader.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><strong>Conclusion</strong></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">IcedID is a popular malware typically used by more advanced cyber criminal threat actors, and its use across the threat landscape has remained relatively consistent until recently. Ultimately, there seems to be considerable effort going into the future of IcedID and the malware’s codebase, including the addition of two new variants described in this report. While historically IcedID’s main function was a banking trojan, the removal of banking functionality aligns with the overall landscape shift away from banking malware and an increasing focus on being a loader for follow-on infections, including ransomware.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Proofpoint anticipates that while many threat actors will continue to use the Standard variant, it is likely the new variants will continue to be used to facilitate additional malware attacks.</p>
