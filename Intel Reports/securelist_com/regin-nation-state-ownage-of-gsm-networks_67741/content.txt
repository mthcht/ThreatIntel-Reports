																							<h2>&quot;Beware of Regin, the master! His heart is poisoned. He would be thy bane...&quot;</h2>											
											<div class="js-reading-content">
												<div class="c-wysiwyg">
													<address>Motto: &#8220;<em>Beware of Regin, the master! His heart is poisoned. He would be thy bane&#8230;</em>&#8220;</address>
<address>&#8220;The Story of Siegfried&#8221; by James Baldwin</address>
<address> </address>
<h2 id="introduction-history">Introduction, history</h2>
<p><a title="Regin: Nation-state ownage of GSM networks" href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2018/03/08070305/Kaspersky_Lab_whitepaper_Regin_platform_eng.pdf">Download our full Regin paper (PDF)</a>.</p>
<p>In the spring of 2012, following a Kaspersky Lab presentation on the unusual facts surrounding the <a href="https://www.kaspersky.com/about/press/major_malware_outbreaks/duqu">Duqu</a> malware, a security researcher contacted us and mentioned that Duqu reminded him of another high-end malware incident. Although he couldn&#8217;t share a sample, the third-party researcher mentioned the &#8220;Regin&#8221; name, a malware attack that is now dreaded by many security administrators in governmental agencies around the world.</p>
<p>For the past two years, we&#8217;ve been tracking this most elusive malware across the world. From time to time, samples would appear on various multi-scanner services, but they were all unrelated to each other, cryptic in functionality and lacking context.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s unknown exactly when the first samples of Regin were created. Some of them have timestamps dating back to 2003.</p>
<p>The victims of Regin fall into the following categories:</p>
<ul>
<li>Telecom operators</li>
<li>Government institutions</li>
<li>Multi-national political bodies</li>
<li>Financial institutions</li>
<li>Research institutions</li>
<li>Individuals involved in advanced mathematical/cryptographical research</li>
</ul>
<p>So far, we&#8217;ve observed two main objectives from the attackers:</p>
<ul>
<li>Intelligence gathering</li>
<li>Facilitating other types of attacks</li>
</ul>
<p>While in most cases, the attackers were focused on extracting sensitive information, such as e-mails and documents, we have observed cases where the attackers compromised telecom operators to enable the launch of additional sophisticated attacks. More about this in the <b>GSM Targeting </b>section below.</p>
<p>Perhaps one of the most publicly known victims of Regin is <strong>Jean Jacques Quisquater</strong> (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Quisquater">https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Quisquater</a>), a well-known Belgian cryptographer. In February 2014, Quisquater announced he was the victim of a sophisticated cyber intrusion incident. We were able to obtain samples from the Quisquater case and confirm they belong to the Regin platform.</p>
<p>Another interesting victim of Regin is a computer we are calling &#8220;<strong>The Magnet of Threats</strong>&#8220;. This computer belongs to a research institution and has been attacked by <strong>Turla</strong>, <strong>Mask/Careto</strong>, <strong>Regin</strong>, <strong>Itaduke</strong>, <strong>Animal Farm</strong> and some other advanced threats that do not have a public name, all co-existing happily on the same computer at some point.</p>
<h2 id="initial-compromise-and-lateral-movement">Initial compromise and lateral movement</h2>
<p>The exact method of the initial compromise remains a mystery, although several theories exist, which include man-in-the-middle attacks with browser zero-day exploits. For some of the victims, we observed tools and modules designed for lateral movement. So far, we have not encountered any exploits. The replication modules are copied to remote computers by using Windows administrative shares and then executed. Obviously, this technique requires administrative privileges inside the victim&#8217;s network. In several cases, the infected machines were also Windows domain controllers. Targeting of system administrators via web-based exploits is one simple way of achieving immediate administrative access to the entire network.</p>
<h2 id="the-regin-platform">The Regin platform</h2>
<p>In short, Regin is a cyber-attack platform which the attackers deploy in the victim networks for ultimate remote control <i>at all possible levels</i>.</p>
<p>The platform is extremely modular in nature and has multiple stages.</p>
<p><a href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three.png" class="magnificImage"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1671" height="858" class="aligncenter wp-image-67743 size-full" src="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three.png" alt="Regin-graph-three" srcset="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three.png 1671w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three-300x154.png 300w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three-1024x526.png 1024w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three-768x394.png 768w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070347/Regin-graph-three-1536x789.png 1536w" sizes="(max-width: 1671px) 100vw, 1671px" /></a></p>
<p style="text-align: center;font-weight: bold"><em>Regin platform diagram</em></p>
<p>The first stage (&#8220;stage 1&#8221;) is generally the only executable file that will appear in victim&#8217; systems. Further stages are stored either directly on the hard drive (for 64 bit systems), as NTFS Extended Attributes or registry entries. We&#8217;ve observed many different stage 1 modules, which sometimes have been merged with public sources to achieve a type of polymorphism, complicating the detection process.</p>
<p>The second stage has multiple purposes and can remove the Regin infection from the system if instructed so by the 3rd stage.</p>
<p>The second stage also creates a marker file that can be used to identify the infected machine. Known filenames for this marker are:</p>
<ul>
<li><b>%SYSTEMROOT%\system32\nsreg1.dat</b></li>
<li><b>%SYSTEMROOT%\system32\bssec3.dat</b></li>
<li><b>%SYSTEMROOT%\system32\msrdc64.dat</b></li>
</ul>
<p>Stage 3 exists only on 32 bit systems &#8211; on 64 bit systems, stage 2 loads the dispatcher directly, skipping the third stage.</p>
<p>Stage 4, the dispatcher, is perhaps the most complex single module of the entire platform. The dispatcher is the user-mode core of the framework. It is loaded directly as the third stage of the 64-bit bootstrap process or extracted and loaded from the VFS as module <b>50221 </b>as the fourth stage on 32-bit systems.</p>
<p>The dispatcher takes care of the most complicated tasks of the Regin platform, such as providing an API to access virtual file systems, basic communications and storage functions as well as network transport sub-routines. <i>In essence, the dispatcher is the brain that runs the entire platform.</i></p>
<p>A thorough description of all malware stages can be found in our full <a href="//media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2018/03/08070305/Kaspersky_Lab_whitepaper_Regin_platform_eng.pdf">technical paper</a>.</p>
<h3 id="virtual-file-systems-32-64-bit">Virtual File Systems (32/64-bit)</h3>
<p>The most interesting code from the Regin platform is stored in encrypted file storages, known as Virtual File Systems (VFSes).</p>
<p>During our analysis we were able to obtain 24 VFSes, from multiple victims around the world. Generally, these have random names and can be located in several places in the infected system. For a full list, including format of the Regin VFSes, see our <a href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2018/03/08070305/Kaspersky_Lab_whitepaper_Regin_platform_eng.pdf">technical paper</a>.</p>
<h2 id="unusual-modules-and-artifacts">Unusual modules and artifacts</h2>
<p>With high-end APT groups such as the one behind Regin, mistakes are very rare. Nevertheless, they do happen. Some of the VFSes we analyzed contain words which appear to be the respective codenames of the modules deployed on the victim:</p>
<ul>
<li><b><b>legspinv2.6 and LEGSPINv2.6</b></b></li>
<li><b>WILLISCHECKv2.0</b></li>
<li><b>HOPSCOTCH</b></li>
</ul>
<p>Another module we found, which is a plugin type <b>55001.0</b> references another codename, which is <b>U_STARBUCKS</b>:</p>
<p><a href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070343/regin_starbucks.png" class="magnificImage"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="792" height="238" class="aligncenter wp-image-67747 size-full" src="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070343/regin_starbucks.png" alt="regin_starbucks" srcset="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070343/regin_starbucks.png 792w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070343/regin_starbucks-300x90.png 300w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070343/regin_starbucks-768x231.png 768w" sizes="(max-width: 792px) 100vw, 792px" /></a></p>
<h3 id="gsm-targeting">GSM Targeting</h3>
<p>The most interesting aspect we found so far about Regin is related to an infection of a large GSM operator. One VFS encrypted entry we located had internal id <b>50049.2</b> and appears to be an activity log on a GSM Base Station Controller.</p>
<p><a href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070338/868px-Gsm_structures.svg_.png" class="magnificImage"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="868" height="529" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-67750" src="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070338/868px-Gsm_structures.svg_.png" alt="868px-Gsm_structures.svg" srcset="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070338/868px-Gsm_structures.svg_.png 868w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070338/868px-Gsm_structures.svg_-300x183.png 300w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070338/868px-Gsm_structures.svg_-768x468.png 768w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070338/868px-Gsm_structures.svg_-330x200.png 330w" sizes="(max-width: 868px) 100vw, 868px" /></a></p>
<p style="text-align: center;font-weight: bold"><em>From <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_station_subsystem">https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_station_subsystem</a></em></p>
<p>According to the GSM documentation (<a href="http://www.telecomabc.com/b/bsc.html">http://www.telecomabc.com/b/bsc.html</a>): &#8220;<i>The Base Station Controller (BSC) is in control of and supervises a number of Base Transceiver Stations (</i><a href="http://www.telecomabc.com/b/bts.html"><i>BTS</i></a><i>). The BSC is responsible for the allocation of radio resources to a mobile call and for the handovers that are made between base stations under his control. Other handovers are under control of the </i><a href="http://www.telecomabc.com/m/msc.html"><i>MSC</i></a><i>.</i>&#8221;</p>
<p>Here&#8217;s a look at the decoded Regin GSM activity log:</p>
<p><a href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070333/regin_gsmlog.png" class="magnificImage"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="685" height="232" class="size-full wp-image-67752 aligncenter" src="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070333/regin_gsmlog.png" alt="regin_gsmlog" srcset="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070333/regin_gsmlog.png 685w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070333/regin_gsmlog-300x102.png 300w" sizes="(max-width: 685px) 100vw, 685px" /></a></p>
<p>This log is about 70KB in size and contains hundreds of entries like the ones above. It also includes timestamps which indicate exactly when the command was executed.</p>

<h2 id="communication-and-cc">Communication and C&amp;C</h2>
<p>The C&amp;C mechanism implemented in Regin is extremely sophisticated and relies on communication drones deployed by the attackers throughout the victim networks. Most victims communicate with another machine in their own internal network, through various protocols, as specified in the config file. These include HTTP and Windows network pipes. The purpose of such a complex infrastructure is to achieve two goals: give attackers access deep into the network, potentially bypassing air gaps and restrict as much as possible the traffic to the C&amp;C.</p>

<h2 id="attribution">Attribution</h2>
<p>Considering the complexity and cost of Regin development, it is likely that this operation is supported by a nation-state. While attribution remains a very difficult problem when it comes to professional attackers such as those behind Regin, certain metadata extracted from the samples might still be relevant.</p>
<p><a href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070312/regin_timestamps.png" class="magnificImage"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="size-full wp-image-67780 aligncenter" src="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070312/regin_timestamps.png" alt="regin_timestamps" width="583" height="348" srcset="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070312/regin_timestamps.png 583w, https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2014/11/08070312/regin_timestamps-300x179.png 300w" sizes="(max-width: 583px) 100vw, 583px" /></a></p>
<p>As this information could be easily altered by the developers, it&#8217;s up to the reader to attempt to interpret this: as an intentional false flag or a non-critical indicator left by the developers.</p>
<p>More information about Regin is available to Kaspersky Intelligent Services&#8217; clients. Contact: <a href="mailto:intelreports@kaspersky.com">intelreports@kaspersky.com</a></p>
<h2 id="conclusions">Conclusions</h2>
<p>For more than a decade, a sophisticated group known as Regin has targeted high-profile entities around the world with an advanced malware platform. As far as we can tell, the operation is still active, although the malware may have been upgraded to more sophisticated versions. The most recent sample we&#8217;ve seen was from a 64-bit infection. This infection was still active in the spring of 2014.</p>
<p>The name Regin is apparently a reversed &#8220;In Reg&#8221;, short for &#8220;In Registry&#8221;, as the malware can store its modules in the registry. This name and detections first appeared in anti-malware products around March 2011.</p>
<p>From some points of view, the platform reminds us of another sophisticated malware: <a href="https://securelist.com/the-epic-turla-operation/65545/">Turla</a>. Some similarities include the use of virtual file systems and the deployment of communication drones to bridge networks together. Yet through their implementation, coding methods, plugins, hiding techniques and flexibility, <i>Regin surpasses Turla as one of the most sophisticated attack platforms</i> we have ever analysed.</p>
<p>The ability of this group to penetrate and monitor GSM networks is perhaps the most unusual and interesting aspect of these operations. In today&#8217;s world, we have become too dependent on mobile phone networks which rely on ancient communication protocols with little or no security available for the end user. Although all GSM networks have mechanisms embedded which allow entities such as law enforcement to track suspects, there are other parties which can gain this ability and further abuse them to launch other types of attacks against mobile users.</p>

<p>Kaspersky products detect modules from the Regin platform as: <strong>Trojan.Win32.Regin.gen</strong> and <strong>Rootkit.Win32.Regin</strong>.</p>
