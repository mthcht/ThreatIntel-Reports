                            <h1 class="title-post" itemprop="name">API In, Adapters In: The Silent Takeover of AI Models</h1>
                    <div class="date">
            <span itemprop="datePublished"><a href="https://threat.boutique/archive/2026-10" title="Show all posts made on this month">04 October 2026</a></span>
            - Posted in            <span itemprop="articleSection"><a href="https://threat.boutique/category/other">Other</a></span> by            <span itemprop="author"><a href="https://threat.boutique/author/b3vSclAuunBMsdt">Jok3r</a></span> -

        </div>
                    <div class="featured-image">
                <a href="https://threat.boutique/2026/10/api-in-adapters-in-the-silent-takeover-of-ai-models"><img src="https://threat.boutique/content/images/20261004001247-image.jpg" alt="API In, Adapters In: The Silent Takeover of AI Models"/></a>
            </div>
                                        <div class="post-body post-1791058331" itemprop="articleBody">
            <h4>Background:</h4>

<p>One of the stages in model training is the fine-tuning of an LLM. This is a highly important and useful process for shaping the LLM’s final representation. However, this stage can pose a risk if an attacker gains access to it. As we know, modern infrastructures are often remote and typically include APIs or other types of connections that engineers use to manage these stages.</p>

<h4>Attacker advantages to intercept in LLM tuning stage:</h4>

<p>From an evasion perspective, an attacker does not have much time to remain in the victim’s environment. Retraining the full model chain is also unsuitable for a covert operation due to the high resource consumption involved. For this reason, the attacker can instead train over an adapter. This allows them to inject their own code/instruction into only a small adapter module, which can later be integrated into the model during the fine-tuning process established by the victim. Tampering with the entire LLM is very different from training a small adapter that becomes part of the model.</p>

<h4>Attack simulation</h4>

<p>In this example, let’s imagine that the victim has exposed a weak point in the assets where fine-tuning is taking place. Using classic methods (brute force, credential dumping/stealing, and so on), the attacker is already inside. The first step is to import their instructions with the help of already established tools. In our example, the victim is using InstructLab as a complementary method to assist with LLM fine-tuning. Through a series of steps, the attacker creates a new adapter. As a result, the behavior of the LLM model changes immediately. In this example, we have instructed the LLM model that ACME CORP’s mascot is Ziggy, instead of the general information the model originally contained. This type of attack can lead to the output of false information and can even result in malicious behavior.
<img src="https://threat.boutique/content/images/20261004002957-234956.png" alt="enter image description here" /></p>

<h4>Prevention mechanisms</h4>

<ul>
<li>Always monitor and control the environment where model fine-tuning is
performed, with special attention to newly created files.</li>
<li>Ensure that LLM fine-tuning runs through an automated pipeline and
that no one has write access except the designated service account.</li>
<li>Always maintain hashes of the generated files and the model itself,
and correlate them with the production versions.</li>
</ul>

<h4>Conclusion</h4>

<p>Keeping the keys to your kingdom is one of the most challenging tasks in the current cybersecurity industry. Always keep in mind that threat actors constantly search for the shortest path to achieve their goals, and time is very limited in their operations. Therefore, use that to your advantage and prioritize preventing the weak points that can allow an attacker to reach their objective quickly. Those points should be addressed first.</p>
