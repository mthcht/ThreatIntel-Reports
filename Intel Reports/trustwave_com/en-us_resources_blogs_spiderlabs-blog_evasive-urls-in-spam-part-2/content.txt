          <h1>Evasive URLs in Spam: Part 2</h1>
          
        </div>
        <div class="blog-details-header-right">
          
            
            <a href="#" class="theme-changer">Change theme to light</a>
            
            
            

            
            <div class="hero-spider" role="img" aria-label="Evasive URLs in Spam: Part 2"></div>
            

            
          
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="toc-content content-wrapper">
        
        <div class="wrapper-main_content">
          
          <div class="blog-details-metadata ">
            <span class="date-time">
              <time datetime="2020-10-01 05:00:00">October 01, 2020</time>
            </span>
            <span class="minread">2 Minute Read</span>
            
              <span class="blog-author">by Diana Lopera</span>
            
          </div> 
          
          
          <div class="wrapper-blog-content">
            <span id="hs_cos_wrapper_post_body" class="hs_cos_wrapper hs_cos_wrapper_meta_field hs_cos_wrapper_type_rich_text" style="" data-hs-cos-general-type="meta_field" data-hs-cos-type="rich_text"><p style="text-align: left;">A URL can be completely valid, yet still misleading. In this blog, we will present another technique with URLs that we observed in a recent malicious spam campaign. This is the continuation of an earlier <a href="evasive-urls-in-spam/">blog</a> that discussed how valid URL formats can be used in evading detection.</p> 
<!--more--> 
<p>The spams in this campaign have a PowerPoint Add-in attachment which contains a malicious macro. When the PowerPoint file is closed, it accesses a URL via the Windows binary mshta.exe, and this leads to different malware being installed into the system. This routine is not unusual for macro downloaders. However, we find the obfuscation used on the URL interesting and worthy of further investigation.</p> 
<div> 
 <div id="photo-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026be4142bca200d">
  <img loading="lazy" src="https://www.levelblue.com/hubfs/Imported_Blog_Media/6a0133f264aa62970b026be4142bca200d-640wi" alt="Email_sample" title="Email_sample"> 
  <div id="caption-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026be4142bca200d" style="font-size: 14px; text-align: center;">
   <em>Figure 1: The spam containing a PowerPoint Add-in and the PowerPoint’s process tree</em>
  </div> 
  <div>
   &nbsp;
  </div> 
 </div> 
 <div id="photo-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b0263e96807d6200b">
  <img loading="lazy" src="https://www.levelblue.com/hubfs/Imported_Blog_Media/6a0133f264aa62970b0263e96807d6200b-600wi" alt="Ppt_hiew" title="Ppt_hiew"> 
  <div id="caption-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b0263e96807d6200b" style="font-size: 14px; text-align: center;">
   <em>Figure 2: The PowerPoint attachment and its macro code where the initial malicious URL is formulated</em>
  </div> 
 </div> 
</div> 
<p>&nbsp;</p> 
<p>The domains associated with this campaign are already known to host malicious files and obfuscated malicious data. To trick the email recipient, and avoid being flagged by email and AV scanners, the cybercriminals behind this campaign employed a<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://tools.ietf.org/html/rfc3986#section-7.6">semantic attack on these URLs</a>.</p> 
<p>A URI may have an<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://tools.ietf.org/html/rfc3986#section-3.2">Authority</a><span>&nbsp;</span>component and below is its structure. If the Userinfo subcomponent is present, it is preceded by two slashes and followed by an “@” character.<a href="evasive-urls-in-spam-part-2/#_msoanchor_1"></a></p> 
<p>authority = [<strong>userinfo</strong>@]host[:port]</p> 
<p>Userinfo is rarely used, and as such, can be used to try and fool a casual observer. In this campaign, dummy userinfo is incorporated on the URLs. The bad guys are attempting to make the domains unnoticeable yet still conforming with the<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://tools.ietf.org/html/rfc3986#section-3">generic URI syntax</a>.</p> 
<div> 
 <div id="photo-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026bde9562d1200c">
  <img loading="lazy" src="https://www.levelblue.com/hubfs/Imported_Blog_Media/6a0133f264aa62970b026bde9562d1200c-900wi" alt="Url_flow" title="Url_flow"> 
  <div id="caption-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026bde9562d1200c" style="font-size: 14px; text-align: center;">
   <em>Figure 3: The URL flow</em>
  </div> 
 </div> 
</div> 
<p>&nbsp;</p> 
<p>The initial URL shown in the image above has the domain<span>&nbsp;</span><em>j[.]mp –<span>&nbsp;</span></em>a URL shortening service offered by Bit.ly, a URL shortener too. To avoid being characterized as a short URL and eventually evading detection signatures, the string “<em>%909123id”<span>&nbsp;</span></em>is repeatedly used in the userinfo. Since the URL<span>&nbsp;</span><em>j[.]mp/kassaasdskdd</em><span>&nbsp;</span><em>(shortened from Figure 3) </em>does not require a userinfo to gain access to any resources, the userinfo data will be ignored when the URL is accessed. The first URL, accessed by the PowerPoint attachment, redirects to an obfuscated VBScript hosted on Pastebin.</p> 
<div> 
 <div id="photo-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026bde9562f2200c">
  <img loading="lazy" src="https://www.levelblue.com/hubfs/Imported_Blog_Media/6a0133f264aa62970b026bde9562f2200c-900wi" alt="Vbscript" title="Vbscript"> 
  <div id="caption-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026bde9562f2200c" style="font-size: 14px; text-align: center;">
   <em>Figure 4: The obfuscated script on Pastebin and its de-obfuscated data</em>
  </div> 
  <div>
   &nbsp;
  </div> 
 </div> 
 <div id="photo-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026bde95632a200c">
  <img loading="lazy" src="https://www.levelblue.com/hubfs/Imported_Blog_Media/6a0133f264aa62970b026bde95632a200c-700wi" alt="RegistryJPG" title="RegistryJPG"> 
  <div id="caption-xid-6a0133f264aa62970b026bde95632a200c" style="font-size: 14px; text-align: center;">
   <em>Figure 5: The registry entry created by the VBScript on Figure 4</em>
  </div> 
 </div> 
</div> 
<p>&nbsp;</p> 
<p>The VBScript, contained in the 2<sup>nd</sup><span>&nbsp;</span>URL in Figure 3, is a dropper. It writes a PowerShell downloader into the registry and sets its persistence. The PowerShell downloads and processes the raw data on two more Pastebin URLs, and then executes the output binaries.</p> 
<p>The third and the fourth URLs are Pastebin URLs too. Both contain dummy userinfo as well which will be ignored by the Pastebin URLs. The content at the third URL <em>pastebin[.]com/raw/uhMtv3Bk</em><span>&nbsp;</span><em>(shortened from Figure 3)<span>&nbsp;</span></em>contains an<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/358a36a890383fc6aeaa89261e8a47b6217dd9bd1a31b63590b38bffe092a4f5/detection">obfuscated PowerShell code</a>. The PowerShell executes 2 DotNet compiled DLLs – the<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/e4d14ba73670184066a00cf5d3361580f6c4fbc5d0862a90278d82e95426faa5/detection">first DLL </a>bypasses the Anti-Malware Scan Interface (AMSI) and then loads<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/8ed29945294e0ba0ae9d5c94c3871dfb00eb9c32b2c7a7704005b31642977a02/detection">a DLL injector</a><span>&nbsp;</span>into the memory. The fourth URL<span>&nbsp;</span><em>pastebin[.]com/raw/Nz1mPUdT (shortened from Figure 3)</em><span>&nbsp;</span>contains an<span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/db27da48b6e129333ddd2de1661ab8069ef964f1f72848b2101d99a16c7af1bc/detection">obfuscated malware</a><span>&nbsp;</span><a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/19f9e641335afc56669566ad979c572f924e2fbb3ab32ecbb9ccfd155c75ef2c/detection">Lokibot</a><span>&nbsp;</span>sample. This will be injected to a legit process notedpad.exe by the DLL injector mentioned earlier.</p> 
<p style="text-align: left;"><span style="color: #262626; font-size: 1.5rem; letter-spacing: 0px;">Summary</span></p> 
<p style="text-align: left;">We found it interesting that the attackers were using URIs in this way, which essentially is an attack on the user’s preconceived notion of what a URI should look like. It may also defeat security solutions, which may be expecting URIs in a certain format.</p> 
<p style="text-align: left;"><a href="https://www.trustwave.com/en-us/services/email-security/" rel="noopener">Trustwave Secure Email Gateway</a> has added protection for this threat for our customers. As advised by my colleague in the <a href="evasive-urls-in-spam/">blog</a>, be cautious with URLs received from external emails – investigate links before clicking.</p>
