    <h1 class="page-headline">Okrum: Ke3chang group targets diplomatic missions</h1>    <p class="sub-title">Tracking the malicious activities of the elusive Ke3chang APT group, ESET researchers have discovered new versions of malware families linked to the group, and a previously unreported backdoor</p>

    <div class="article-authors d-flex flex-wrap">  <div class="article-author d-flex">  <a href="/blog/en/our-experts/zuzana-hromcova/" title="Zuzana Hromcová">   <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/2023/07/zuzana-hromcova-2.jpeg" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <img class="author-image me-3"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/2023/07/zuzana-hromcova-2.jpeg" alt="Zuzana Hromcová"  /></picture>  </a>  <div class="author-text"><p><a href="/blog/en/our-experts/zuzana-hromcova/" title="Zuzana Hromcová"><b>Zuzana Hromcová</b></a></p></div></div> </div>

                    <div class="article-body">
    <p>In this blogpost, we will sum up the findings published in full in our white paper &ldquo;<a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/ESET_Okrum_and_Ketrican.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Okrum and Ketrican: An overview of recent Ke3chang group activity</a>&rdquo;.</p>
<p>The Ke3chang group, also known as APT15, is a threat group <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/groups/G0004/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">believed to be operating out of China</a>. Its activities were traced back to 2010 in FireEye&rsquo;s 2013 report on <a href="https://www.fireeye.com/content/dam/fireeye-www/global/en/current-threats/pdfs/wp-operation-ke3chang.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">operation Ke3chang</a> &ndash; a cyberespionage campaign directed at diplomatic organizations in Europe.</p>
<p>We have been tracking the malicious activities related to this threat actor and discovered a&nbsp;previously undocumented malware family with strong links to the Ke3chang group &ndash; a backdoor we named Okrum. According to ESET telemetry, Okrum was first detected in December 2016, and targeted diplomatic missions in Slovakia, Belgium, Chile, Guatemala and Brazil throughout 2017.</p>
<p>Furthermore, from 2015 to 2019, we detected new versions of known malware families attributed to the Ke3chang group &ndash; BS2005 backdoors from operation Ke3chang and the <a href="https://www.nccgroup.trust/uk/about-us/newsroom-and-events/blogs/2018/march/apt15-is-alive-and-strong-an-analysis-of-royalcli-and-royaldns/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">RoyalDNS malware</a>, reported by NCC Group in 2018.</p>
<p><em>Note: New versions of operation Ke3chang malware from 2015-2019 are detected by ESET systems as Win32/Ketrican and collectively referred to as Ketrican backdoors/samples, marked with the relevant year, across our white paper and this blogpost.</em></p>

<h2>Investigation timeline</h2>
<h3>2015: Ketrican</h3>
<p>In 2015, we identified new suspicious activities in European countries. The group behind the attacks seemed to have a particular interest in Slovakia, where a big portion of the discovered malware samples was detected; Croatia, the Czech Republic and other countries were also affected.</p>
<p>Our technical analysis of the malware used in these attacks showed close ties to BS2005 backdoors from <a href="https://www.fireeye.com/content/dam/fireeye-www/global/en/current-threats/pdfs/wp-operation-ke3chang.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">operation Ke3chang</a>, and to a related <a href="https://researchcenter.paloaltonetworks.com/2016/05/operation-ke3chang-resurfaces-with-new-tidepool-malware/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">TidePool malware</a> family discovered by Palo Alto Networks in 2016 that targeted Indian embassies across the globe.</p>
<h3>2016-2017: Okrum</h3>
<p>The story continued in late 2016, when we discovered a new, previously unknown backdoor that we named Okrum. The malicious actors behind the Okrum malware were focused on the same targets in Slovakia that were previously targeted by Ketrican 2015 backdoors.</p>
<h3>2017: Ketrican and RoyalDNS</h3>
<p>We started connecting the dots when we discovered that the Okrum backdoor was used to drop a Ketrican backdoor, freshly compiled in 2017.</p>
<p>In 2017, the same entities that were affected by the Okrum malware (and by the 2015 Ketrican backdoors) again became targets of the malicious actors. This time, the attackers used new versions of the RoyalDNS malware and a Ketrican 2017 backdoor.</p>
<h3>2018: Ketrican</h3>
<p>In 2018, we discovered a new version of the Ketrican backdoor that featured some code improvements.</p>
<h3>2019: Ketrican</h3>
<p>The group continues to be active in 2019 &ndash; in March 2019, we detected a new Ketrican sample that has evolved from the 2018 Ketrican backdoor. It attacked the same targets as the backdoor from 2018.</p>
<p>This timeline of events shows that the attackers were focused on the same type of targets but were using different malicious toolsets to compromise them. In the process, they exposed Okrum, a formerly unknown project. Figure 1 shows ESET detections related to our investigation in the context of previously documented Ke3chang activity.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure1-WM.png"><img class="wp-image-127778 size-large" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure1-WM.png" alt="" width="1024" height="549" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 1. Timeline of previously documented Ke3chang group activity and ESET detections related to our investigation</em></p>

<h2>Links to Ke3chang group</h2>
<p>Our research has shown that the Ketrican, Okrum, and RoyalDNS backdoors detected by ESET after 2015 are linked to previously documented Ke3chang group activity, and to each other, in a&nbsp;number of ways. These are the most important connections:<em>&nbsp;</em></p>
<ul>
<li>Ketrican backdoors from 2015, 2017, 2018 and 2019 have all evolved from malware used in Operation Ke3chang</li>
<li>The RoyalDNS backdoor detected by ESET in 2017 is similar to the RoyalDNS backdoor used in previously reported attacks</li>
<li>Okrum is linked to Ketrican backdoors in that it was used to drop a Ketrican backdoor compiled in 2017</li>
<li>Okrum, Ketrican and RoyalDNS target the same type of organizations; some of the entities affected by Okrum were also targeted with one or more of Ketrican/RoyalDNS backdoors</li>
<li>Okrum has a&nbsp;similar modus operandi as previously documented Ke3chang malware &ndash; it is equipped with a&nbsp;basic set of backdoor commands and relies on manually typing shell commands and executing external tools for most of its malicious activity</li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>Okrum</strong></h2>
<h3>Distribution and targets</h3>
<p>According to our telemetry, Okrum was used to target diplomatic missions in Slovakia, Belgium, Chile, Guatemala, and Brazil, with the attackers showing a&nbsp;particular interest in Slovakia.</p>
<p>The operators of the malware tried to hide malicious traffic with its C&amp;C server within regular network traffic by registering seemingly legitimate domain names. For example, the samples used against Slovak targets communicated with a domain name mimicking a Slovak map portal (support.slovakmaps[.]com). A similar masquerade was used in a sample detected in a Spanish speaking country in South America &ndash; the operators used a domain name that translates as &ldquo;missions support&rdquo; in Spanish (misiones.soportesisco[.]com).</p>
<p>How the Okrum malware was distributed to the targeted machines is a question that remains to be answered.</p>
<h3>Technical details</h3>
<p>The Okrum backdoor is a dynamic-link library that is installed and loaded by two earlier-stage components. During our investigation, the implementation of these two components was being changed frequently. Every few months, the authors actively changed implementation of the Okrum loader and installer components to avoid detection. By the time of publication, ESET systems have detected seven different versions of the loader component and two versions of the installer, although the functionality remained the same.</p>
<p>The payload of Okrum is hidden in a&nbsp;PNG file. When the file is viewed in an image viewer, a familiar image is displayed, as seen in Figure 2, but the Okrum loaders are able to locate an extra encrypted file that the user cannot see. This steganography technique is an attempt by the malicious actors to stay unnoticed and evade detection.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure2-WM.png"><img class="wp-image-127779 size-large" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure2-WM.png" alt="" width="1024" height="336" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 2. An innocuous-looking PNG image with an encrypted malicious DLL embedded inside</em></p>
</div>
<p>As for functionality, Okrum is only equipped with basic backdoor commands, such as downloading and uploading files, executing files and shell commands. Most of the malicious activity has to be performed by typing shell commands manually, or by executing other tools and software. This is a common practice of the Ke3chang group, which had also been pointed out previously in the <a href="https://www.intezer.com/miragefox-apt15-resurfaces-with-new-tools-based-on-old-ones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Intezer</a> and <a href="https://www.nccgroup.trust/uk/about-us/newsroom-and-events/blogs/2018/march/apt15-is-alive-and-strong-an-analysis-of-royalcli-and-royaldns/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NCC Group</a> reports monitoring Ke3chang group activity.</p>
<p>Indeed, we have detected various external tools being abused by Okrum, such as a keylogger, tools for dumping passwords, or enumerating network sessions. The Ketrican backdoors we detected from 2015 to 2019 used similar utilities. We can only guess why the Ke3chang actor uses this technique &ndash; maybe the combination of a simple backdoor and external tools fully accommodates their needs, while being easier to develop; but it may also be an attempt to evade behavioral detection.</p>
<p>The detection evasion techniques we observed in the Okrum malware include embedding the malicious payload within a&nbsp;legitimate PNG image, employing several anti-emulation and anti-sandbox tricks, as well as making frequent changes in implementation.</p>
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Our analysis of the links between previously documented Ke3chang malware and the newly discovered Okrum backdoor lets us claim with high confidence that Okrum is operated by the Ke3chang group. Having documented Ke3chang group activity from 2015 to 2019, we conclude that the group continues to be active and works on improving its code over time.</p>
<p>ESET detection names and other Indicators of Compromise for these campaigns can be found in the full white paper: &ldquo;<a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/ESET_Okrum_and_Ketrican.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Okrum and Ketrican: An overview of recent Ke3chang group activity</a>&rdquo;.</p>
