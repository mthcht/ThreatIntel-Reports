    <h1 class="page-headline">Android ransomware is back</h1>    <p class="sub-title">ESET researchers discover a new Android ransomware family that attempts to spread to victims’ contacts and deploys some unusual tricks</p>

    <div class="article-authors d-flex flex-wrap">  <div class="article-author d-flex">  <a href="/blog/en/our-experts/lukas-stefanko/" title="Lukas Stefanko">   <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/2023/07/lukas-stefanko.jpeg" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <img class="author-image me-3"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/2023/07/lukas-stefanko.jpeg" alt="Lukas Stefanko"  /></picture>  </a>  <div class="author-text"><p><a href="/blog/en/our-experts/lukas-stefanko/" title="Lukas Stefanko"><b>Lukas Stefanko</b></a></p></div></div> </div>

                    <div class="article-body">
    <p><em>UPDATE (July 30th, 2019): Due to rushing with the publication of this research &ndash; in order to warn about this threat as soon as possible &ndash; we erroneously stated that &ldquo;because of the hardcoded key value that is used to encrypt the private key, it would be possible to decrypt files without paying the ransom by changing the encryption algorithm to a decryption algorithm</em><em>&rdquo;. However, this </em><em>&ldquo;hardcoded key</em><em>&rdquo; is an RSA-1024 public key, which can&rsquo;t be easily broken, hence creating a&nbsp;decryptor for this particular ransomware is close to impossible. Hat tip goes to </em><em>Alexey Vishnyakov from Positive Technologies who drew our attention to this inaccuracy.</em></p>
<p>After two years of decline in Android ransomware, a new family has emerged. We have seen the ransomware, detected by ESET Mobile Security as Android/Filecoder.C, distributed via various online forums. Using victims&rsquo; contact lists, it spreads further via SMS with malicious links. Due to narrow targeting and flaws in execution of the campaign, the impact of this new ransomware is limited. However, if the operators start targeting broader groups of users, the Android/Filecoder.C ransomware could become a serious threat.</p>
<p>Android/Filecoder.C has been active since at least July 12<sup>th</sup>, 2019. Within the campaign we discovered, Android/Filecoder.C has been distributed via malicious posts on Reddit and the &ldquo;XDA Developers&rdquo; forum, a forum for Android developers. We reported the malicious activity to XDA Developers and Reddit. The posts on the XDA Developers forum were removed swiftly; the malicious Reddit profile was still up at the time of publication.</p>
<p>Android/Filecoder.C spreads further via SMS with malicious links, which are sent to all contacts in the victim&rsquo;s contact list.</p>
<p>After the ransomware sends out this batch of malicious SMSes, it encrypts most user files on the device and requests a ransom.</p>
<p>Users with ESET Mobile Security receive a warning about the malicious link; should they ignore the warning and download the app, the security solution will block it.</p>
<h2>Distribution</h2>
<p>The campaign we discovered is based on two domains (see the IoCs section below), controlled by the attackers, that contain malicious Android files for download. The attackers lure potential victims to these domains via posting or commenting on Reddit (Figure 1) or XDA Developers (Figure 2).</p>
<p>Mostly, the topics of the posts were porn-related; alternatively, we&rsquo;ve seen also technical topics used as a lure. In all comments or posts, the attackers included links or QR codes pointing to the malicious apps.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-1-WM-631x1024-2.png"><img class="wp-image-128407 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-1-WM-631x1024-2.png" alt="" width="631" height="1024" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 1. The attacker&rsquo;s Reddit profile with malicious posts and comments</em></p>
</div>
<p><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-2a-1.png"><img class="aligncenter wp-image-128408 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-2a-1.png" alt="" width="1034" height="287" /></a></p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-2b.png"><img class="wp-image-128301 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-2b.png" alt="" width="1040" height="295" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 2. Some of the attackers&rsquo; malicious posts on the XDA Developers forum</em></p>
</div>
<p>In one link that was shared on Reddit, the attackers used the URL shortener bit.ly. This bit.ly URL was created on Jun 11, 2019 and as seen in Figure 3 its statistics show that, at the time of writing, it had reached 59 clicks from different sources and countries.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-3-1024x783-1.png"><img class="wp-image-128409 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-3-1024x783-1.png" alt="" width="1024" height="783" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 3. Statistics for the bit.ly link shared on Reddit during the ransomware campaign</em></p>
</div>
<h2>Spreading</h2>
<p>As previously mentioned, the Android/Filecoder.C ransomware spreads links to itself via SMS messages to all the entries in the victim&rsquo;s contact list.</p>
<p>These messages include links to the ransomware; to increase the potential victims&rsquo; interest, the link is presented as a link to an app that supposedly uses the potential victim&rsquo;s photos, as seen in Figure 4.</p>
<p>To maximize its reach, the ransomware has the 42 language versions of the message template seen in Figure 5. Before sending the messages, it chooses the version that fits the victim device&rsquo;s language setting. To personalize these messages, the malware prepends the contact&rsquo;s name to them.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-4-1-1.png"><img class="wp-image-128412 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-4-1-1.png" alt="" width="824" height="244" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 4. An SMS with a link to the ransomware; this language variant is sent if the sending device has the language set to English</em></p>
</div>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-5.png"><img class="wp-image-128304 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-5.png" alt="" width="695" height="879" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 5. A total of 42 language versions that are hardcoded in the ransomware</em></p>
</div>
<h2>Functionality</h2>
<p>Once potential victims receive an SMS message with the link to the malicious application, they need to install it manually. After the app is launched, it displays whatever is promised in the posts distributing it &ndash; most often, it&rsquo;s a sex simulator online game. However, its main purposes are C&amp;C communication, spreading malicious messages and implementing the encryption/decryption mechanism.</p>
<p>As for C&amp;C communication, the malware contains hardcoded C&amp;C and Bitcoin addresses in its source code. However, it can also dynamically retrieve them: they can be changed any time by the attacker, using the free Pastebin service.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-6-1.png"><img class="wp-image-128413 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-6-1.png" alt="" width="572" height="153" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 6. An example of a set of addresses for the ransomware to retrieve C&amp;C addresses</em></p>
</div>
<p>The ransomware has the ability to send text messages, due to having access to the user&rsquo;s contact list. Before it encrypts files, it sends a message to each of the victim&rsquo;s contacts using the technique described in the &ldquo;Spreading&rdquo; section above.</p>
<p>Next, the ransomware goes through files on accessible storage &ndash; meaning all the device&rsquo;s storage except where system files reside - and encrypts most of them (see the &ldquo;File encryption mechanism&rdquo; section below). After the files are encrypted, the ransomware displays its ransom note (in English) as seen in Figure 7.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-7.png"><img class="wp-image-128306" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-7.png" alt="" width="572" height="1017" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 7. A ransom note displayed by Android/Filecoder.C</em></p>
</div>
<p>It is true that if the victim removes the app, the ransomware will not be able to decrypt the files, as stated in the ransom note. Also, according to our analysis, there is nothing in the ransomware&rsquo;s code to support the claim that the affected data will be lost after 72 hours.</p>
<p>As seen in Figure 8, the requested ransom is partially dynamic. The first part of what will be the amount of bitcoins to be requested is hardcoded &ndash; the value is 0.01 &ndash; while the remaining six digits are the user ID generated by the malware.</p>
<p>This unique practice may serve the purpose of identifying the incoming payments. (In Android ransomware, this is typically achieved by generating a separate Bitcoin wallet for each encrypted device.) Based on the recent exchange rate of approximately US$9,400 per bitcoin, the derived ransoms will fall in the range US$94-188 (assuming that the unique ID is generated randomly).</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-8.png"><img class="wp-image-128307 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-8.png" alt="" width="488" height="54" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 8. How the malware calculates the ransom </em></p>
</div>
<p>Unlike typical Android ransomware, Android/Filecoder.C doesn&rsquo;t prevent use of the device by locking the screen.</p>
<p>As seen in Figure 9, at the time of writing, the mentioned Bitcoin address, which can be dynamically changed but was the same in all cases we&rsquo;ve seen, has recorded no transactions.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-9.png"><img class="wp-image-128308" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-9.png" alt="" width="488" height="260" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 9. The Bitcoin address used by the attackers</em></p>
</div>
<h2>File encryption mechanism</h2>
<p>The ransomware uses asymmetric and symmetric encryption. First, it generates a public and private key pair. This private key is encrypted using the RSA algorithm with a hardcoded public key stored in the code and sent to the attacker&rsquo;s server. The attacker can decrypt that private key and, after the victim pays the ransom, send that private key to the victim to decrypt their files.</p>
<p>When encrypting files, the ransomware generates a new AES key for each file that will be encrypted. This AES key is then encrypted using the public key and prepended to each encrypted file, resulting in the following pattern: ( (AES)public_key + (File)AES ).seven</p>
<p>The file structure is seen in Figure 10.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-10.png"><img class="wp-image-128309 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-10.png" alt="" width="833" height="338" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 10. Overview of encrypted file structure</em></p>
</div>
<p>The ransomware encrypts the following filetypes, by going through accessible storage directories:</p>
<p>&ldquo;.doc", ".docx", ".xls", ".xlsx", ".ppt", ".pptx", ".pst", ".ost", ".msg", ".eml", ".vsd", ".vsdx", ".txt", ".csv", ".rtf", ".123", ".wks", ".wk1", ".pdf", ".dwg", ".onetoc2", ".snt", ".jpeg", ".jpg", ".docb", ".docm", ".dot", ".dotm", ".dotx", ".xlsm", ".xlsb", ".xlw", ".xlt", ".xlm", ".xlc", ".xltx", ".xltm", ".pptm", ".pot", ".pps", ".ppsm", ".ppsx", ".ppam", ".potx", ".potm", ".edb", ".hwp", ".602", ".sxi", ".sti", ".sldx", ".sldm", ".sldm", ".vdi", ".vmdk", ".vmx", ".gpg", ".aes", ".ARC", ".PAQ", ".bz2", ".tbk", ".bak", ".tar", ".tgz", ".gz", ".7z", ".rar", ".zip", ".backup", ".iso", ".vcd", ".bmp", ".png", ".gif", ".raw", ".cgm", ".tif", ".tiff", ".nef", ".psd", ".ai", ".svg", ".djvu", ".m4u", ".m3u", ".mid", ".wma", ".flv", ".3g2", ".mkv", ".3gp", ".mp4", ".mov", ".avi", ".asf", ".mpeg", ".vob", ".mpg", ".wmv", ".fla", ".swf", ".wav", ".mp3", ".sh", ".class", ".jar", ".java", ".rb", ".asp", ".php", ".jsp", ".brd", ".sch", ".dch", ".dip", ".pl", ".vb", ".vbs", ".ps1", ".bat", ".cmd", ".js", ".asm", ".h", ".pas", ".cpp", ".c", ".cs", ".suo", ".sln", ".ldf", ".mdf", ".ibd", ".myi", ".myd", ".frm", ".odb", ".dbf", ".db", ".mdb", ".accdb", ".sql", ".sqlitedb", ".sqlite3", ".asc", ".lay6", ".lay", ".mml", ".sxm", ".otg", ".odg", ".uop", ".std", ".sxd", ".otp", ".odp", ".wb2", ".slk", ".dif", ".stc", ".sxc", ".ots", ".ods", ".3dm", ".max", ".3ds", ".uot", ".stw", ".sxw", ".ott", ".odt", ".pem", ".p12", ".csr", ".crt", ".key", ".pfx", ".der"</p>
<p>However, it doesn&rsquo;t encrypt files in directories that contain the strings &ldquo;.cache&rdquo;, &ldquo;tmp&rdquo;, or &ldquo;temp&rdquo;.</p>
<p>The ransomware also leaves files unencrypted if the file extension is &ldquo;.zip&rdquo; or &ldquo;.rar&rdquo; and the file size is over 51,200&nbsp;KB/50&nbsp;MB, and &ldquo;.jpeg&rdquo;, &ldquo;.jpg&rdquo; and &ldquo;.png&rdquo; files with a file size less than 150&nbsp;KB.</p>
<p>The list of filetypes contains some entries unrelated to Android and at the same time lacks some typical Android extensions such as .apk, .dex, .so. Apparently, the list has been copied from the notorious WannaCryptor aka WannaCry ransomware.</p>
<p>Once the files are encrypted, the file extension &ldquo;.seven&rdquo; is appended to the original filename, as seen in Figure 11.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-11.png"><img class="wp-image-128310 size-large" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-11.png" alt="" width="580" height="1024" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 11. Encrypted files with the extension &ldquo;.seven&rdquo;</em></p>
</div>
<h2>Decryption mechanism</h2>
<p>Code to decrypt encrypted files is present in the ransomware. If the victim pays the ransom, the ransomware operator can verify that via the website seen in Figure 12 and send the private key to decrypt the files.</p>
<div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-12.png"><img class="wp-image-128311 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2019/07/Figure-12.png" alt="" width="959" height="200" /></a>
<p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 12. Ransom payment verification web page</em></p>
</div>
<h2>How to stay safe</h2>
<ul>
<li>First of all, keep your devices up to date, ideally set them to patch and update automatically, so that you stay protected even if you&rsquo;re not among the most security savvy users.</li>
<li>If possible, stick with Google Play or other reputable app stores. These markets might not be completely free from malicious apps, but you have a fair chance of avoiding them.</li>
<li>Prior to installing any app, check its ratings and reviews. Focus on the negative ones, as they often come from legitimate users, while positive feedback is often crafted by the attackers.</li>
<li>Focus on the permissions requested by the app. If they seem inadequate for the app&rsquo;s functions, avoid downloading the app.</li>
<li>Use a reputable mobile security solution to protect your device.</li>
</ul>
