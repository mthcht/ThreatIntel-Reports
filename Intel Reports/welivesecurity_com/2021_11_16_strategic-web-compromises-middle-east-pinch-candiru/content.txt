    <h1 class="page-headline">Strategic web compromises in the Middle East with a pinch of Candiru</h1>    <p class="sub-title">ESET researchers have discovered strategic web compromise (aka watering hole) attacks against high-profile websites in the Middle East</p>

    <div class="article-authors d-flex flex-wrap">  <div class="article-author d-flex">  <a href="/blog/en/our-experts/matthieu-faou/" title="Matthieu Faou">   <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/mfaou.jpeg" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <img class="author-image me-3"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/mfaou.jpeg" alt="Matthieu Faou"  /></picture>  </a>  <div class="author-text"><p><a href="/blog/en/our-experts/matthieu-faou/" title="Matthieu Faou"><b>Matthieu Faou</b></a></p></div></div> </div>

    <p class="article-info mb-5">
        <span>16 Nov 2021</span>
        <span class="d-none d-lg-inline">&nbsp;&bull;&nbsp;</span>
        <span class="d-inline d-lg-none">, </span>
        <span>17 min. read</span>
    </p>


    <div class="hero-image-container">
         <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x266/blog/2021/11/candiru-eset-research-middle-east-watering-hole-attacks.jpg" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x425/blog/2021/11/candiru-eset-research-middle-east-watering-hole-attacks.jpg" media="(max-width: 1120px)" />  <img class="hero-image"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x700/blog/2021/11/candiru-eset-research-middle-east-watering-hole-attacks.jpg" alt="Strategic web compromises in the Middle East with a pinch of Candiru"  /></picture>    </div>
</div>

                    <div class="article-body">
    <p>Back in 2018, ESET researchers developed a custom in-house system to uncover watering hole attacks (aka strategic web compromises) on high-profile websites. On July 11<sup>th</sup>, 2020 it notified us that the website of the Iranian embassy in Abu Dhabi had been modified and had started injecting JavaScript code from <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://piwiks[.]com/reconnect.js</span>, as shown in Figure 1.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-1.-Script-injection-on-the-website-of-the-Iranian-Embassy-in-Abu-Dhabi.png"><img class="wp-image-156674 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-1.-Script-injection-on-the-website-of-the-Iranian-Embassy-in-Abu-Dhabi.png" alt="" width="738" height="475" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 1. Script injection on the website of the Iranian Embassy in Abu Dhabi</em></p></div></p>
<p>Our curiosity was aroused by the nature of the targeted website and in the following weeks we noticed that other websites with connections to the Middle East started to be targeted. We traced the start of the campaign back to March 2020, when the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">piwiks[.]com</span> domain was re-registered. We believe that the strategic web compromises only started in April 2020 when the website of the Middle East Eye (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">middleeasteye.net</span>), a London-based digital news site covering the region, started to inject code from the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">piwiks[.]com</span> domain.</p>
<p>At the end of July or the beginning of August 2020, all remaining compromised websites were cleaned; it is probable that the attackers themselves removed the malicious scripts from the compromised websites. The threat group went quiet until January 2021, when we observed a new wave of compromises. This second wave lasted until August 2021, when all websites were cleaned again. A few indicators from this second wave were shared on <a href="https://twitter.com/felixaime/status/1417928699513610241" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>Twitter by a fellow researcher</em></a>, which allows us to make a link with what Kaspersky tracks as Karkadann.</p>
<p>We detail the inner working of the compromises in the <em>Technical analysis</em> section, below, but it is worth noting that the final targets are specific visitors of those websites, who are likely to receive a browser exploit. The compromised websites are only used as a hop to reach the final targets.</p>
<p>We also uncovered interesting links with Candiru, detailed in the section <em>Links between the watering holes, spearphishing documents and Candiru</em>. Candiru is a private Israeli spyware firm that was recently <a href="https://www.commerce.gov/news/press-releases/2021/11/commerce-adds-nso-group-and-other-foreign-companies-entity-list" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>added to the Entity List</em></a> (entities subject to licensing restrictions) of the US Department of Commerce. This may prevent any US‑based organization from doing business with Candiru without first obtaining a license from the Department of Commerce.</p>
<p>At the time of writing, it seems that the operators are taking a pause, probably in order to retool and make their campaign stealthier. We expect to see them back in the ensuing months.</p>

<h2>Technical analysis – Strategic web compromises</h2>
<h3>First wave – 2020</h3>
<h4><em><strong>First stage – Injected script</strong></em></h4>
<p>All compromised websites were injecting JavaScript code from the attacker-controlled domains <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">piwiks[.]com</span> and <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">rebrandly[.]site</span>. In the first known case, the injection is as shown in Figure 3.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-1.-Script-injection-on-the-website-of-the-Iranian-Embassy-in-Abu-Dhabi.png"><img class="wp-image-156674 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-1.-Script-injection-on-the-website-of-the-Iranian-Embassy-in-Abu-Dhabi.png" alt="Figure 3. Script injection on the website of the Iranian Embassy in Abu Dhabi" width="738" height="475" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 3. Script injection on the website of the Iranian Embassy in Abu Dhabi</em></p></div></p>
<p>This injection loads a remote JavaScript named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">reconnects.js</span> and a legitimate third-party library, GeoJS, for IP geolocation lookup.</p>
<p>In the cases of <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">rebrandly[.]site</span> injections, the additional scripts are loaded using HTML <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">script</span> tags, as seen in Figure 4.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-4.-Script-injected-into-the-medica-tradefair.co-website-1.png"><img class="wp-image-156744" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-4.-Script-injected-into-the-medica-tradefair.co-website-1.png" alt="" width="800" height="291" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 4. Script injected into the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">medica-tradefair[.]co</span> website</em></p></div></p>
<h4><em><strong>Second stage – Fingerprinting script</strong></em></h4>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">reconnects.js</span> and <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">recon-api.js</span> are almost identical; only the order of some lines or functions are changed. As shown in Figure 5, the malware authors tried to avoid raising suspicions by prepending their script with a copy of the jQuery Browser Plugin header. They were probably hoping that malware analysts would not scroll further.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-5.-Beginning-of-the-fingerprinting-script-used-in-the-first-wave.png"><img class="wp-image-156678" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-5.-Beginning-of-the-fingerprinting-script-used-in-the-first-wave.png" alt="" width="800" height="587" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 5. Beginning of the fingerprinting script used in the first wave</em></p></div></p>
<p>The script first implements a function named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">geoip</span>. It is automatically called by the GeoJS library, previously loaded, as mentioned on <a href="https://www.geojs.io/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>the official GeoJS website</em></a>. The variable <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">json</span> contains the IP geolocation information. The script sends this JSON via an HTTP POST request to the C&amp;C server at the URL <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://rebrandly[.]site/reconnect-api.php</span>. If the server returns an HTTP 200 status code, then the script proceeds to a function named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">main</span>.</p>
<p>First, <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">main</span> gathers information such as the operating system version and the browser version using custom functions shown in Figure 6. They simply parse the browser User-Agent to extract information.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-6.-OS-and-browser-fingerprinting-functions.png"><img class="wp-image-156679" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-6.-OS-and-browser-fingerprinting-functions.png" alt="" width="800" height="615" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 6. OS and browser fingerprinting functions</em></p></div></p>
<p>As shown in Figure 7, the function then checks whether the operating system is either Windows or macOS and only continues if so. This is interesting because it suggests that this operation is intended to compromise computers and not mobile devices such as smartphones. It also checks for a list of common web browsers: Chrome, Firefox, Opera, IE, Safari and Edge.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-7.-The-main-function-of-the-fingerprinting-script-used-in-the-first-wave-1.png"><img class="wp-image-156746" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-7.-The-main-function-of-the-fingerprinting-script-used-in-the-first-wave-1.png" alt="" width="800" height="332" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 7. The </em><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">main</span><em> function of the fingerprinting script used in the first wave</em></p></div></p>
<p>The script also encrypts a hardcoded value, <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">1122</span>, although we don’t know for what purpose. Despite the function being named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">decrypt</span>, it actually encrypts using RSA and the library JSEncrypt. The 1024-bit RSA key is hardcoded and set to:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">-----BEGIN PUBLIC KEY-----</span><br />

<p>Then, the script sends an HTTPS GET request to the C&amp;C server <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">rebrandly[.]site</span>. The id parameter contains the fingerprint data and the last parameter value contains the country provided by the GeoJS library.</p>
<p>If the server returns a reply, it is decrypted using AES from the CryptoJS library, and a hardcoded key <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">flcwsfjWCWEcoweijwf@#$@#$@#499299234@#$!@2</span>. This key stayed the same, even after we tried a few requests.</p>
<p>The decrypted value is supposedly a URL and a new iframe pointing to this URL is created. We were unable to get any valid answer but we believe it leads to a browser remote code execution exploit that allows an attacker to take control of a machine.</p>
<h3>Second wave – 2021</h3>
<p>In January 2021, a new wave of attacks started. The attackers created an entirely new network infrastructure and changed all their JavaScript code.</p>
<h4><em><strong>First stage – Injected script</strong></em></h4>
<p>In order to be a bit stealthier still, in this second wave, they started to modify scripts that were already on the compromised website. So instead of adding code to the main HTML page, they modified libraries such as <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">wp-embed.min.js</span>, as seen in Figure 8. They simply added a few lines at the end of <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://www.smc.gov.ye/wp-includes/js/wp-embed.min.js</span> to load a script from a server they control: <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://visitortrack[.]net/sliders.js</span>.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-8.-The-main-function-of-the-fingerprinting-script-used-in-the-first-wave-2.png"><img class="wp-image-156748 size-full" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-8.-The-main-function-of-the-fingerprinting-script-used-in-the-first-wave-2.png" alt="" width="475" height="162" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 8. Injected script used in the second wave</em></p></div></p>
<p>Another strategy used to limit their exposure is to create a cookie the first time the visitor executes the malicious script, as shown in Figure 9. As the script is conditionally injected depending on whether the cookie already exists, this will prevent further injections. This specific code was found on the website of the Syrian Central Authority for the [sic] Supervision and Inspection (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">casi.gov.sy</span>).</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-9.-Cookie-creation-to-avoid-further-requests.png"><img class="wp-image-156682" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-9.-Cookie-creation-to-avoid-further-requests.png" alt="" width="800" height="248" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 9. Cookie creation to avoid further requests</em></p></div></p>
<h4><em><strong>Second stage</strong></em></h4>
<p>From January to March 2021, for the second-stage script, the operators used a script based on the <a href="https://github.com/flouthoc/minAjax.js" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>minAjax library</em></a>. This is not a fingerprinting script per se as it doesn’t send any information about the browser or the operating system to the C&amp;C server – an example is shown in Figure 10. It should be noted that very similar scripts are used by the <a href="https://www.netskope.com/fr/blog/lnkr-makes-a-comeback-this-ads-for-us" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>LNKR adware</em></a>, so a detection on this might lead to a high volume of false positives.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-10.-Second-stage-script-of-the-second-wave.png"><img class="wp-image-156683" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-10.-Second-stage-script-of-the-second-wave.png" alt="" width="800" height="508" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 10. Second-stage script of the second wave</em></p></div></p>
<p>This script contains the current timestamp, <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">t0</span>, an expiration timestamp, <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">ex</span>, and two hashes <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">juh</span> and <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">cs</span>, whose significance we don’t know at present. These values are sent to the C&amp;C server <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://webfex[.]bz/f/gstats</span>. If the reply is a JSON object and contains the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">fw</span> key, the script issues a redirection to the URL contained in <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">fw</span> using <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">parent.top.window.location.href</span>. As with the first wave, we were not able to get any valid redirect.</p>
<p>In April 2021, this script was changed to <a href="https://fingerprintjs.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>FingerprintJS Pro</em></a>. This is a commercial product whose developers have an official website shown in Figure 11.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-11.-Home-page-of-FingerprintJS.png"><img class="wp-image-156684" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-11.-Home-page-of-FingerprintJS.png" alt="" width="800" height="608" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 11. Home page of FingerprintJS</em></p></div></p>
<p>In comparison to the fingerprinting script used in 2020, this is far more complex because it retrieves the default language, the list of fonts supported by the browser, the time zone, the list of browser plugins, the local IP addresses using <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">RTCPeerConnection</span>, and so on. Network communications with the C&amp;C server are encrypted with an AES session key. As shown in Figure 12, the server can return JavaScript code that will be executed in the context of the current web page.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-12.-FingerprintJS-Pro-adds-JavaScript-code-to-the-current-page.png"><img class="wp-image-156685" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2021/11/Figure-12.-FingerprintJS-Pro-adds-JavaScript-code-to-the-current-page.png" alt="" width="800" height="205" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 12. FingerprintJS Pro adds JavaScript code to the current page</em></p></div></p>
<p>As with the previous cases, we never got a valid redirect. We still believe it leads to a browser exploit and it shows that this campaign is highly targeted.</p>
<h3>Spearphishing documents and links with Candiru</h3>
<h4><strong><em>Reminder of the Citizen Lab publication</em></strong></h4>
<p>In the <a href="https://citizenlab.ca/2021/07/hooking-candiru-another-mercenary-spyware-vendor-comes-into-focus/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>Citizen Lab Candiru blogpost</em></a>, there is a section called <em>A Saudi-Linked Cluster?</em>. It mentions <a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/91b3f4836cdf956292c806b5e4d1a9475eb7553083ea61b0a768c9a86b9fec2f" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>a spearphishing document that was uploaded to VirusTotal</em></a>.</p>
<p>The C&amp;C server used by this document is <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://cuturl[.]space/lty7uw</span> and VirusTotal captured a redirection from this URL to <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">https://useproof[.]cc/1tUAE7A2Jn8WMmq/api</span>. The domain <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">useproof[.]cc</span> was resolving to <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">109.70.236[.]107</span> and, according to the Citizen Lab, this server matched their so-called CF3 fingerprint for Candiru C&amp;C servers. This domain was registered via Porkbun, as are most Candiru-owned domains.</p>
<p>Two domains resolving to the same IP address caught our attention:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">webfx[.]cc</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">engagebay[.]cc</span></li>
</ul>

<p>What is interesting to note is that the watering holes are limited to a quite narrow victimology. We also noted that domains known to be operated by Candiru (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">webfx[.]cc</span> for example) are very similar to domains used for the watering holes (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">webfx[.]bz</span>). However, they were not registered in the same fashion and their servers are configured very differently.</p>
<p>In July 2021, Google published <a href="https://blog.google/threat-analysis-group/how-we-protect-users-0-day-attacks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>a blogpost</em></a> providing details on exploits used by Candiru. It includes CVE‑2021-21166 and CVE-2021-30551 for Chrome and CVE-2021-33742 for Internet Explorer. They are full remote code execution exploits that allow an attacker to take control of a machine by making the victim visit a specific URL that then delivers the exploit. This shows Candiru has the capabilities to exploit browsers in a watering hole attack.</p>
<p>Hence, we believe that the watering holes behave similarly to the documents. The first C&amp;C server, injected in the compromised websites, would redirect to another C&amp;C server, owned by a spyware firm such as Candiru and delivering a browser exploit.</p>
<p>Based on this information, we assess:</p>
<ul>
<li>with <strong>low</strong> confidence that the creators of the documents and the operators of the watering holes are the same.</li>
<li>with <strong>medium</strong> confidence that the operators of the watering holes are customers of Candiru.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>This report describes two strategic web compromise campaigns targeting high-profile organizations in the Middle East, with a strong focus on Yemen. We also revealed links to Candiru, a spyware firm, that sells state‑of‑the‑art offensive software tools and related services to government agencies.</p>
<p>We were unable to get an exploit and the final payload. This shows that the operators choose to narrow the focus of their operations and that they don’t want to burn their zero-day exploits.</p>
<p>We stopped seeing activity from this operation at the end of July 2021, shortly after the release of blogposts by the Citizen Lab, Google and Microsoft detailing the activities of Candiru.</p>
