    <h1 class="page-headline">IsaacWiper and HermeticWizard: New wiper and worm targeting Ukraine</h1>    <p class="sub-title">ESET researchers uncover a new wiper that attacks Ukrainian organizations and a worm component that spreads HermeticWiper in local networks</p>

    <div class="article-authors d-flex flex-wrap">  <div class="article-author d-flex">  <a href="/blog/en/our-experts/eset-research/" title="ESET Research">   <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/authors/eset-research.png" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <img class="author-image me-3"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x45/blog/authors/eset-research.png" alt="ESET Research"  /></picture>  </a>  <div class="author-text"><p><a href="/blog/en/our-experts/eset-research/" title="ESET Research"><b>ESET Research</b></a></p></div></div> </div>

        <span>01 Mar 2022</span>
        <span class="d-none d-lg-inline">&nbsp;&bull;&nbsp;</span>
        <span class="d-inline d-lg-none">, </span>
        <span>19 min. read</span>
    </p>


    <div class="hero-image-container">
         <picture>  <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x266/blog/2022/03/isaacwiper-hermeticwizard-wiper-worm-Ukraine-cyberattacks-malware.jpg" media="(max-width: 768px)" />   <source srcset="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x425/blog/2022/03/isaacwiper-hermeticwizard-wiper-worm-Ukraine-cyberattacks-malware.jpg" media="(max-width: 1120px)" />  <img class="hero-image"  src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/tn/-x700/blog/2022/03/isaacwiper-hermeticwizard-wiper-worm-Ukraine-cyberattacks-malware.jpg" alt="IsaacWiper and HermeticWizard: New wiper and worm targeting Ukraine"  /></picture>    </div>

                    <div class="article-body">
    <span class="update-block"><p> <em>Update (March 4<sup>th</sup>, 2022): We fixed an error in the analysis of IsaacWiper. It uses the Mersenne Twister PRNG and not the ISAAC</em><em> PRNG as initially written.</em></p></span><p>As the recent hostilities started between Russia and Ukraine, ESET researchers discovered several malware families targeting Ukrainian organizations.</p>
<ul>
<li>On February 23<sup>rd</sup>, 2022, a destructive campaign using HermeticWiper targeted multiple Ukrainian organizations.</li>
<li>This cyberattack preceded, by a few hours, the start of the invasion of Ukraine by Russian Federation forces</li>
<li>Initial access vectors varied from one organization to another. We confirmed one case of the wiper being dropped by GPO, and uncovered a worm used to spread the wiper in another compromised network.</li>
<li>Malware artifacts suggest that the attacks had been planned for several months.</li>
<li>On February 24<sup>th</sup>, 2022, a second destructive attack against a Ukrainian governmental network started, using a wiper we have named IsaacWiper.</li>
<li>ESET Research has not yet been able to attribute these attacks to a known threat actor.</li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>Destructive attacks in Ukraine</strong></h2>
<p>As stated in this ESETResearch <a href="https://twitter.com/ESETresearch/status/1496581903205511181"><em>tweet</em></a> and <a href="https://www.welivesecurity.com/2022/02/24/hermeticwiper-new-data-wiping-malware-hits-ukraine/"><em>WLS blogpost</em></a>, we uncovered a destructive attack against computers in Ukraine that started around 14:52 on February 23<sup>rd</sup>, 2022 UTC. This followed distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)<a href="https://www.politico.eu/article/minister-ukraine-websites-down-in-another-massive-online-attack/"><em> attacks against major Ukrainian websites</em></a> and preceded the Russian military invasion by a few hours.</p>
<p>These destructive attacks leveraged at least three components:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>HermeticWiper</strong>: makes a system inoperable by corrupting its data</li>
<li><strong>HermeticWizard</strong>: spreads HermeticWiper across a local network via WMI and SMB</li>
<li><strong>HermeticRansom</strong>: ransomware written in Go</li>
</ul>
<p>HermeticWiper was observed on hundreds of systems in at least five Ukrainian organizations.</p>
<p>On February 24<sup>th</sup>, 2022, we detected yet another new wiper in a Ukrainian governmental network. We named it IsaacWiper and we are currently assessing its links, if any, with HermeticWiper. It is important to note that it was seen in an organization that was <em>not</em> affected by HermeticWiper.</p>
<h3><strong>Attribution</strong></h3>
<p>At this point, we have not found any tangible connection with a known threat actor. HermeticWiper, HermeticWizard, and HermeticRansom do not share any significant code similarity with other samples in the ESET malware collection. IsaacWiper is still unattributed as well.</p>
<h3><strong>Timeline</strong></h3>
<p>HermeticWiper and HermeticWizard are signed by a code-signing certificate (shown in Figure 1) assigned to <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Hermetica Digital Ltd</span> issued on April 13<sup>th</sup>, 2021. We requested the issuing CA (DigiCert) to revoke the certificate, which it did on February 24<sup>th</sup>, 2022.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2022/03/Figure-1.-Code-signing-certificate-assigned-to-Hermetic-Digital-Ltd.png"><img class="wp-image-159777" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2022/03/Figure-1.-Code-signing-certificate-assigned-to-Hermetic-Digital-Ltd.png" alt="" width="800" height="474" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 1. Code-signing certificate assigned to Hermetic Digital Ltd</em></p></div></p>
<p>According to a <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/cyprus-games-writer-denies-links-malware-found-before-russian-invasion-2022-02-24/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>report by Reuters</em></a>, it seems that this certificate was not stolen from Hermetica Digital. It is likely that instead the attackers impersonated the Cypriot company in order to get this certificate from DigiCert.</p>
<p>ESET researchers assess with high confidence that the affected organizations were compromised well in advance of the wiper’s deployment. This is based on several facts:</p>
<ul>
<li>HermeticWiper PE compilation timestamps, the oldest being December 28<sup>th</sup>, 2021</li>
<li>The code-signing certificate issue date of April 13<sup>th</sup>, 2021</li>
<li>Deployment of HermeticWiper through GPO in at least one instance suggests the attackers had prior access to one of that victim’s Active Directory servers</li>
</ul>
<p>The events are summarized in the timeline in Figure 2.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2022/03/Figure-2.-Timeline-of-important-events.png"><img class="wp-image-159778" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2022/03/Figure-2.-Timeline-of-important-events.png" alt="" width="800" height="474" /></a><p class="caption-text"><em>Figure 2. Timeline of important events</em></p></div></p>
<h3><strong>Initial access</strong></h3>
<h3>HermeticWiper</h3>
<p>The initial access vector is currently unknown but we have observed artifacts of lateral movement inside the targeted organizations. In one entity, the wiper was deployed through the default domain policy (GPO), as shown by its path on the system:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\system32\GroupPolicy\DataStore\0\sysvol\&lt;redacted&gt;\Policies\{31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9}\Machine\cc.exe</span></p>
<p>This indicates that attackers likely took control of the Active Directory server.</p>
<p>In other instances, it is possible that <a href="https://github.com/SecureAuthCorp/impacket" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>Impacket</em></a> was used to deploy HermeticWiper. A Symantec <a href="https://symantec-enterprise-blogs.security.com/blogs/threat-intelligence/ukraine-wiper-malware-russia" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>blogpost</em></a> states that the wiper was deployed using the following command line:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cmd.exe /Q /c move CSIDL_SYSTEM_DRIVE\temp\sys.tmp1 CSIDL_WINDOWS\policydefinitions\postgresql.exe 1&gt; \\127.0.0.1\ADMIN$\__1636727589.6007507 2&gt;&amp;1</span></p>
<p>The last part is the same as the default behavior in Impacket’s <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">wmiexec.py</span>, found on <a href="https://github.com/SecureAuthCorp/impacket/blob/cd4fe47cfcb72d7d35237a99e3df95cedf96e94f/examples/wmiexec.py#L295"><em>GitHub</em></a>.</p>
<p>Finally, a custom worm that we have named HermeticWizard was used to spread HermeticWiper across the compromised networks via SMB and WMI.</p>
<h3>IsaacWiper</h3>
<p>The initial access vector is also currently unknown. It is likely that attackers used tools such as Impacket to move laterally. On a few machines, we have also observed <a href="https://github.com/kavika13/RemCom" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>RemCom</em></a>, a remote access tool, being deployed at the same time as IsaacWiper.</p>
<h2><strong>Technical analysis</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>HermeticWiper</strong></h3>
<p>HermeticWiper is a Windows executable with four drivers embedded in its resources. They are legitimate drivers from the EaseUS Partition Master software signed by <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CHENGDU YIWO Tech Development Co.</span>, and they implement low-level disk operations. The following files were observed:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">0E84AFF18D42FC691CB1104018F44403C325AD21</span>: x64 driver</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">379FF9236F0F72963920232F4A0782911A6BD7F7</span>: x86 driver</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">87BD9404A68035F8D70804A5159A37D1EB0A3568</span>: x64 XP driver</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">B33DD3EE12F9E6C150C964EA21147BF6B7F7AFA9</span>: x86 XP driver</li>
</ul>
<p>Depending on the operating system version, one of those four drivers is chosen and dropped in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\System32\drivers\&lt;4 random letters&gt;.sys</span>. It is then loaded by creating a service.</p>
<p>HermeticWiper then proceeds by disabling the Volume Shadow Copy Service (VSS) and wipes itself from disk by overwriting its own file with random bytes. This anti-forensic measure is likely intended to prevent the analysis of the wiper in a post-incident analysis.</p>
<p>It is interesting to note that most of the file operations are performed at a low level using <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">DeviceIoControl</span> calls.</p>
<p>The following locations are overwritten with random bytes generated by the Windows API function <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CryptGenRandom</span>:</p>
<ul>
<li>The master boot record (MBR)</li>
<li>The master file table (MFT)</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">$Bitmap</span> and <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">$LogFile</span> on all drives</li>
<li>The files containing the registry keys (<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">NTUSER</span>*)</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\System32\winevt\Logs</span></li>
</ul>
<p>In addition, it also recursively wipes folders and files in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Windows</span>, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Program Files</span>, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Program Files(x86)</span>, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">PerfLogs</span>, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Boot</span>, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">System Volume Information</span>, and <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">AppData</span> folders, using a <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">FSCTL_MOVE_FILE</span> operation. This technique appears to be quite unusual and very similar to what is implemented in the <a href="https://github.com/bleachbit/windows-wipe/blob/master/filewipe.py" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>Windows Wipe project on GitHub</em></a> (see the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">wipe_extent_by_defrag</span> function). It also wipes symbolic links and big files in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">My Documents</span> and <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Desktop</span> folders by overwriting them with random bytes.</p>
<p>Finally, the machine is restarted. However, it will fail to boot, because the MBR, the MFT, and most files were wiped. We believe it is not possible to recover the impacted machines.</p>
<h3><strong>HermeticWizard</strong></h3>
<p>Looking for other samples signed by the same code-signing certificate (<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Hermetica Digital Ltd</span>), we found a new malware family that we named HermeticWizard.</p>
<p>It is a worm that was deployed on a system in Ukraine at 14:52:49 on February 23<sup>rd</sup>, 2022 UTC. It is a DLL file developed in C++ that exports the functions <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">DllInstall</span>, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">DllRegisterServer</span>, and <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">DllUnregisterServer</span>. Its export DLL name is <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Wizard.dll</span>. It contains three resources, which are encrypted PE files:</p>
<ul>
<li>A sample of HermeticWiper (<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">912342F1C840A42F6B74132F8A7C4FFE7D40FB77</span>)</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">exec_32.dll,</span> responsible for spreading to other local computers via WMI (<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">6B5958BFABFE7C731193ADB96880B225C8505B73</span>)</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">romance.dll</span>, responsible for spreading to other local computers via SMB (<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">AC5B6F16FC5115F0E2327A589246BA00B41439C2</span>)</li>
</ul>
<p>The resources are encrypted with a reverse XOR loop. Each block of four bytes is XORed with the previous block. Finally, the first block is XORed with a hardcoded value, <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">0x4A29B1A3</span>.</p>
<p>HermeticWizard is started using the command line <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">regsvr32.exe /s /i &lt;path&gt;</span>.</p>
<p>First, HermeticWizard tries to find other machines on the local network. It gathers known local IP addresses using the following Windows functions:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">DNSGetCacheDataTable</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">GetIpNetTable</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">WNetOpenEnumW(RESOURCE_GLOBALNET, RESOURCETYPE_ANY)</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">NetServerEnum</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">GetTcpTable</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">GetAdaptersAddresses</span></li>
</ul>
<p>It then tries to connect to those IP addresses (and only if they are local IP addresses) to see if they are still reachable. In case the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-s</span> argument was provided when HermeticWizard was started (<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">regsvr32.exe /s /i:-s &lt;path&gt;</span>), it also scans the full /24 range. So, if<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> 192.168.1.5</span> was found in, for example, the DNS cache, it incrementally scans from <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">192.168.1.1</span> to <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">192.168.1.254</span>. For each IP address, it tries to open a TCP connection on the following ports:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">20: ftp</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">21: ftp</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">22: ssh</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">80: http</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">135: rpc</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">137: netbios</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">139: smb</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">443: https</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">445: smb</span></li>
</ul>
<p>The ports are scanned in a random order so it’s not possible to fingerprint HermeticWizard traffic that way.</p>
<p>When it has found a reachable machine, it drops the WMI spreader (detailed below) on disk and creates a new process with the command line <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">rundll32 &lt;current folder&gt;\&lt;6 random letters&gt;.ocx #1 -s &lt;path to HermeticWizard&gt; - i &lt;target IP&gt;</span>.</p>
<p>It does the same with the SMB spreader (detailed below) that is also dropped in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">&lt;current folder&gt;\&lt;6 random letters&gt;.ocx</span>, but with different random letters.</p>
<p>Finally, it drops HermeticWiper in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">&lt;current folder&gt;\&lt;6 random letters&gt;.ocx</span> and executes it.</p>
<h3><strong>WMI spreader</strong></h3>
<p>The WMI spreader, named by its developers<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> exec_32.dll</span>, takes two arguments:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-i</span>: The target IP address</li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-s</span>: The file to copy and execute on the target machine</li>
</ul>
<p>First, it creates a connection to the remote <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">ADMIN$</span> share of the target using <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">WNetAddConnection2W</span>. The file provided in the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-s</span> argument is then copied using <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CopyFileW</span>. The remote file has a random name generated with <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CoCreateGUID</span> (e.g., <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cB9F06408D8D2.dll</span>) and the string format <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">c%02X%02X%02X%02X%02X%02X</span>.</p>
<p>Second, it tries to execute the copied file, HermeticWizard, on the remote machine using DCOM. It calls <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CoCreateInstance</span> with <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CLSID_WbemLocator</span> as argument. It then uses WMI <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Win32_Process</span> to create a new process on the remote machine, with the command line <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\windows\system32\cmd.exe /c start C:\windows\system32\\regsvr32.exe /s /i C:\windows\&lt;filename&gt;.dll</span>.</p>
<p>Note that the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-s</span> argument is not passed to HermeticWizard, meaning that it won’t scan the local network again from this newly compromised machine.</p>
<p>If the WMI technique fails, it tries to create a service using <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">OpenRemoteServiceManager</span> with the same command as above.</p>
<p>If it succeeds in executing the remote DLL in any way, it sleeps until it can delete the remote file.</p>
<h3><strong>SMB spreader</strong></h3>
<p>The SMB spreader, named by its developers<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> romance.dll</span>, takes the same two arguments as the WMI spreader. Its internal name is likely a reference to the EternalRomance exploit, even if it does not use any exploit.</p>
<p>First it attempts to connect to the following pipes on the remote SMB share (on port 445):</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">samr</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">browser</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">netlogon</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">lsarpc</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">ntsvcs</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">svcctl</span></li>
</ul>
<p>These are pipes known to be used in lateral movement. The spreader has a list of hardcoded credentials that are used in attempts to authenticate via NTLMSSP to the SMB shares:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-- usernames --</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">guest</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">test</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">admin</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">user</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">root</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">administrator</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">manager</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">operator</span></p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-- passwords --</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">123</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Qaz123</span><br />
<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Qwerty123</span></p>
<p>This list of credentials is surprisingly short and is unlikely to work in even the most poorly protected networks.</p>
<p>If the connection is successful, it attempts to drop, to the target <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">ADMIN$</span> share, the file referenced by the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">-s</span> argument. As for the WMI spreader, the remote filename is generated by a call to <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">CoCreateInstance</span>.</p>
<p>It then executes, via SMB, the command line <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cmd /c start regsvr32 /s /i ..\\&lt;filename&gt;  &amp; start cmd /c \"ping localhost -n 7 &amp; wevtutil cl System\"</span>.</p>
<h3><strong>HermeticRansom</strong></h3>
<p>ESET researchers also observed HermeticRansom – ransomware written in Go – being used in Ukraine at the same time as the HermeticWiper campaign. HermeticRansom was first reported in the early hours of February 24<sup>th</sup>, 2022 UTC, in a <a href="https://twitter.com/AvastThreatLabs/status/1496663206634344449" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>tweet</em></a> from AVAST. Our telemetry shows a much smaller deployment compared to HermeticWiper. This ransomware was deployed at the same time as HermeticWiper, potentially in order to hide the wiper’s actions. On one machine, the following timeline was observed:</p>
<ul>
<li>2022-02-23 17:49:55 UTC: HermeticWiper in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Temp\cc.exe</span> deployed</li>
<li>2022-02-23 18:06:57 UTC: HermeticRansom in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Temp\cc2.exe</span> deployed by the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">netsvcs</span> service</li>
<li>2022-02-23 18:26:07 UTC: Second HermeticWiper in<span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;"> C:\Users\com.exe</span> deployed</li>
</ul>
<p>On one occasion, we observed HermeticRansom being deployed through GPO, just like HermeticWiper:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\WINDOWS\system32\GroupPolicy\DataStore\0\sysvol\&lt;redacted&gt;\Policies\{31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9}\Machine\cpin.exe</span></p>
<p>A few strings were left in the binary by the attackers; they reference US President Biden and the White House:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">_/C_/projects/403forBiden/wHiteHousE.baggageGatherings</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">_/C_/projects/403forBiden/wHiteHousE.lookUp</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">_/C_/projects/403forBiden/wHiteHousE.primaryElectionProcess</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">_/C_/projects/403forBiden/wHiteHousE.GoodOffice1</span></li>
</ul>
<p>Once files are encrypted, the message in Figure 3 is displayed to the victim.</p>
<p><div class="caption center"><a href="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2022/03/Figure-3.-HermeticRansom’s-ransom-note.png"><img class="wp-image-159779" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/blog/2022/03/Figure-3.-HermeticRansom’s-ransom-note.png" alt="" width="800" height="368" /></a><p class="caption-text">Figure 3. HermeticRansom’s ransom note</p></div></p>
<h3><strong>IsaacWiper</strong></h3>
<p>IsaacWiper is found in either a Windows DLL or EXE with no Authenticode signature; it appeared in our telemetry on February 24<sup>th</sup>, 2022. As mentioned earlier, the oldest PE compilation timestamp we have found is October 19<sup>th</sup>, 2021, meaning that if its PE compilation timestamp was not tampered with, IsaacWiper might have been used in previous operations months earlier.</p>
<p>For DLL samples, the name in the PE export directory is <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">Cleaner.dll</span> and it has a single export <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">_Start@4</span>.</p>
<p>We have observed IsaacWiper in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">%programdata%</span> and <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\System32</span> under the following filenames:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">clean.exe</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cl.exe</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cl64.dll</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cld.dll</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">cll.dll</span></li>
</ul>
<p>It has no code similarity with HermeticWiper and is way less sophisticated. Given the timeline, it is possible that both are related but we haven’t found any strong connection yet.</p>
<p>IsaacWiper starts by enumerating the physical drives and calls <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">DeviceIoControl</span> with the IOCTL <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">IOCTL_STORAGE_GET_DEVICE_NUMBER</span> to get their device numbers. It then wipes the first 0x10000 bytes of each disk using the Mersenne Twister pseudorandom generator. The generator is seeded using the <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">GetTickCount</span> value.</p>
<p>It then enumerates the logical drives and recursively wipes every file of each disk with random bytes also generated by the Mersenne Twister PRNG. It is interesting to note that it recursively wipes the files in a single thread, meaning that it would take a long time to wipe a large disk.</p>
<p>On February 25<sup>th</sup>, 2022, attackers dropped a new version of IsaacWiper with debug logs. This may indicate that the attackers were unable to wipe some of the targeted machines and added log messages to understand what was happening. The logs are stored in <span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">C:\ProgramData\log.txt</span> and some of the log messages are:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">getting drives...</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">start erasing physical drives...</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">–– start erasing logical drive</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">start erasing system physical drive...</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">system physical drive –– FAILED</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: 'courier new', courier, monospace;">start erasing system logical drive</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>Conclusion</strong></h2>
<p>This report details a destructive cyberattack that impacted Ukrainian organizations on February 23<sup>rd</sup>, 2022, and a second attack that affected a different Ukrainian organization from February 24<sup>th</sup> through 26<sup>th</sup>, 2022. At this point, we have no indication that other countries were targeted.</p>
<p>However, due to the current crisis in Ukraine, there is still a risk that the same threat actors will launch further campaigns against countries that back the Ukrainian government or that sanction Russian entities.</p>
<p>A list of IoCs can also be found in <a href="https://github.com/eset/malware-ioc/tree/master/ua_wipers" target="_blank" rel="noopener">our GitHub repository</a>.</p>
