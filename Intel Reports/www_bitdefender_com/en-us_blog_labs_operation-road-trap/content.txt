e-Transfer credentials</strong></li></ul><p>This means victims may first attempt to pay a fine, then unknowingly provide <strong>online banking details</strong>, leading to a much broader financial compromise.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>"Parking Notice: Past due violation on file. Handle the matter before we initiate collection proceedings, please visit the link below. https://myparking-help[.]com</blockquote><h3 id="united-kingdom-%E2%80%98air-pay%E2%80%99-road-payment-texts">United Kingdom: ‘Air-Pay’ road payment texts</h3><p>One of the most recent identified waves of scam messages is targeting users in the <strong>United Kingdom</strong>, using a simple but effective lure related to road or journey payments.</p><p>Researchers spotted:</p><ul><li>A <strong>single active campaign</strong> using this theme</li><li>Use of <strong>minimal text and direct links</strong> to prompt quick action</li></ul><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>“Pay for your journey by card, click https://im.air-pay.uk/…”</blockquote><p>Unlike other regions that rely on threats or penalties, this campaign uses a <strong>low-friction approach</strong>:</p><ul><li>No mention of fines or legal consequences</li><li>No urgency or escalation language</li><li>A simple request to “pay for your journey”</li></ul><h3 id="ireland-eflow-toll-scam">Ireland: eFlow toll scam</h3><p>In Ireland, scammers are impersonating <strong>eFlow</strong>, the electronic toll system used on the M50 motorway.</p><p>Researchers identified:</p><ul><li> Most targeted regions include<strong> </strong>Leinster and Connacht</li><li>Use of <strong>short code 7726</strong>, also seen in campaigns targeting the United States</li></ul><p>The messages claim a toll is unpaid and direct users to a payment link.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>“eflow: You have an unpaid toll on the motorway. Last reminder — outstanding toll fees may escalate to recovery action and result in rego restrictions. Pay now: https://cutt.ly/…”</blockquote><blockquote>“Last reminder - outstanding toll fees could escalate to recovery action and result in rego restrictions. Pay now to avoid impact. https://paylinkt[.]vip/au”</blockquote><h3 id="new-zealand-police-and-justice-system-fine-scam">New Zealand: Police and justice system fine scam</h3><p>In New Zealand, scammers are impersonating <strong>NZ Police and the Ministry of Justice</strong>, targeting users with messages about overdue traffic fines.</p><p>What stands out:</p><ul><li>Scammers’ use of <strong>government-themed domains</strong> to look legitimate</li><li>Most-targeted regions include: Auckland, Wellington Region and Canterbury</li><li>Inclusion of <strong>mobile-specific instructions</strong> to increase interaction</li></ul><p>The messages claim a traffic fine is overdue and warn of escalating consequences if not paid.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>“NZ Police: Your traffic fine is overdue over 10 days. Merci de régulariser la situation…”</blockquote><h3 id="luxembourg-government-portal-parking-fine-scam">Luxembourg: Government portal parking fine scam</h3><p>In Luxembourg, scammers are impersonating <strong>Guichet.lu</strong>, the official government services portal, using formal messages related to parking violations.</p><p>What stands out:</p><ul><li>One of the smallest smishing campaigns we’ve tracked</li><li>Includes a case reference number, making it feel official</li><li>Mentions specific dates and violations</li></ul><p>The messages claim a parking infraction has been recorded and must be resolved to avoid additional penalties.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>“Guichet – Grand-Duché de Luxembourg. Référence dossier : 2026-458721. Motif : Infraction de stationnement relevée le 21/03/2026. Régularisez via https://lu-retrofacturation[.]com afin d’éviter une majoration.”</blockquote><h3 id="colombia-toll-and-traffic-fine-texts-at-scale">Colombia: Toll and traffic fine texts at scale</h3><p>The second-largest wave of scam messages has been targeting users in Colombia, particularly in <strong>Bogotá, Antioquia, and Valle del Cauca</strong>.</p><p>The messages impersonate <strong>toll operators and traffic authorities</strong>, using multiple sender numbers to distribute the scam at scale.</p><p>We’ve identified:</p><ul><li><strong>11,000+ scam messages</strong> linked to this fraudulent activity</li><li><strong>Multiple rotating short codes</strong> (including 893181–893183 and 893175)</li><li>Use of <strong>repeated domain patterns</strong>, mainly through bibtly.cc and bitcutt.cc</li></ul><p>All links ultimately redirect users to phishing pages designed to collect payment details.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote><em>Sr(a) INFRACTOR! Iniciamos proceso juridico por sanciones viales. Salda HOY con DCTO del 50% en nuestro portal. Consulta: .b2026.biltly[.]cc/ Aplican TyC</em></blockquote><h3 id="spain-dgt-traffic-fine-scam">Spain: DGT traffic fine scam</h3><p>In Spain, scammers are impersonating the <strong>DGT (Dirección General de Tráfico)</strong>, the official traffic authority.</p><p>Researchers identified:</p><ul><li>The most-targeted regions include Madrid, Catalonia and Andalusia</li><li>Use of <strong>spoofed sender names</strong>, including “DGT” and “miMultas”</li></ul><p>The messages are written in Spanish and warn users about an unpaid traffic fine.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>“[DGT]: Último recordatorio antes del aumento de su multa pendiente de pago. Consulte su expediente en el siguiente enlace: https://seguridad-ahora[.]im/dgt.php”</blockquote><blockquote>“Ultimo recordatorio antes del aumento de su multa pendiente de pago. Consulta tú expediente: https://mimultas-es[.]wasmer STOP/INFO: https://mcsms[.]io/aua1bG”</blockquote><h3 id="brazil-traffic-fine-and-tax-obligation-scam">Brazil: Traffic fine and tax obligation scam</h3><p>In Brazil, scammers are combining <strong>traffic-related themes with tax-related pressure</strong>, targeting users with messages about obligations tied to their <strong>CPF (Brazilian tax ID)</strong>.</p><p>Researchers identified:</p><ul><li>Messages written in <strong>Portuguese</strong>, tailored to local users</li><li>Most messages were concentrated in <strong>São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais</strong></li><li>Use of <strong>short code 70101</strong> for delivery</li></ul><p>The messages warn users about an alleged open obligation and urge them to act quickly to avoid restrictions.</p><p><strong>Example message:</strong></p><blockquote>“🚨Comunicado importante: Obrigação tributária em aberto. Tome as devidas providências, evitando restrições em seu CPF. Confira: https://confira[.]online/app3”</blockquote><h3 id="india-traffic-fine-scam-delivering-malware">India: Traffic fine scam delivering malware</h3><p>In India, the campaign takes a more direct approach by delivering <strong>malware disguised as traffic fine notices</strong>.</p><p>The messages impersonate:</p><ul><li>The <strong>Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH)</strong></li><li>Mobile carrier <strong>Airtel</strong></li></ul><p>We’ve identified:</p><ul><li><strong>828 URLs</strong> delivering malware</li><li>Most-targeted regions include Karnataka, Maharashtra and Telangana</li><li>Multiple variations using realistic “e-challan” (traffic fine) references</li></ul><p>Unlike other regions, the goal here is not just to collect payment details but to <strong>install malicious APK on the victim’s device</strong>.</p><p>Example message:</p><blockquote><em>RTO Notice: Your vehicle has an Over Speeding challan [redacted] issued on 22-03-2026. Check &amp; Download now: [malicious link]</em></blockquote><h1 id="how-these-scams-trick-people-into-clicking">How these scams trick people into clicking</h1><p>Even though the messages come from different countries and impersonate different agencies, they share a very similar pattern designed to pressure you into acting fast.</p><p><strong>The “escalation” tactic</strong></p><p>Most messages follow the same script:</p><ul><li>Recipients are told they have an <strong>unpaid toll, fine, or citation</strong></li><li>They’re given a <strong>short deadline</strong> (often 24–72 hours)</li><li>A <strong>penalty fee</strong> is added (commonly around 35%)</li><li>The message warns of consequences, including license suspension, vehicle registration issues, and even legal action or arrest</li><li>Users are pushed to <strong>click a link to resolve the issue immediately</strong></li></ul><h3 id="tricks-designed-specifically-for-mobile-users">Tricks designed specifically for mobile users</h3><p>Bitdefender researchers also observed several techniques meant to bypass phone protections and make messages look more convincing:</p><p><strong>“Reply Y” before clicking</strong></p><p>Some messages ask you to reply first. On iOS devices, this activates links that would otherwise be blocked, making the scam easier to access.</p><p><strong>Messages that look identical to legitimate ones</strong></p><p>Multiple scam campaigns substitute Cyrillic lookalike characters (е, ѕ, м, і, т) for their Latin equivalents, to bypass keyword-based SMS spam filters.</p><p><br><strong>Websites that mimic government pages</strong></p><p>Links often include words like “gov” or “official,” even though they lead to fake domains.</p><p><strong>Sender ID spoofing</strong></p><p>Messages may appear to come from trusted brands or authorities and can even show up in existing message threads. Examples include "Linkt" in Australia and "miMultas" in Spain, which cause messages to appear in the victim's existing legitimate brand thread on some devices.</p><p><strong>Hidden or shortened links</strong></p><p>URL shorteners disguise the final destination, making malicious links look harmless.</p><p><strong>Constantly changing domains</strong></p><p>The scam<strong> </strong>sites are frequently replaced, helping campaign stay active even after some links are blocked.</p><h2 id="hidden-malware-layer"><strong>Hidden Malware Layer</strong></h2><p>While many of these scams aim to steal payment details, some cases go further.</p><p>We’ve spotted hybrid attacks especially targeting drivers in India. Here, the text messages include <strong>direct “Download” calls to action</strong>, pushing users to install apps like <em>Rtochallan.apk</em> hosted on platforms such as GitHub.</p><p>According to <strong>researcher Adina Mateescu</strong>, the app typically loads a <strong>fake payment interface inside a WebView</strong> (a built-in browser component), designed to mimic a government portal and collect personal data.</p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card"><img src="https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/labs/content/images/2026/04/data-src-image-88e31e55-a30a-4f7f-bf4f-b3050d574a68.png" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="300" height="601"></figure><p>&nbsp;</p><p>These apps are not isolated threats. They belong to a broader <strong>malware family of Android trojans</strong> designed to steal financial and personal data while maintaining ongoing access to the device.</p><p>Once installed, the malware immediately establishes persistence by running a <strong>foreground service</strong> (often with a generic name like <em>MyService</em>), ensuring it continues operating even after the app is closed or the device is rebooted.</p><p>From that point, it begins:</p><p><strong>Device profiling</strong><br>&nbsp;Collecting information such as device ID, Android version, IP address, carrier, and battery status</p><p><strong>SIM and phone data enumeration</strong><br>Gathering phone number, SIM details, and network provider information</p><p> <strong>SMS harvesting and monitoring</strong><br>&nbsp;Accessing messages and filtering them for financial keywords related to banking, payments, and transactions</p><p>This activity happens <strong>independently of user interaction</strong>, meaning data collection starts even if the victim never completes the phishing form.</p><h2 id="how-data-is-stolen-and-commands-are-executed">How data is stolen and commands are executed</h2><p>This malware family uses a dual infrastructure for communication and control.</p><h3 id="telegram">Telegram</h3><p>Telegram is used as a primary channel for data exfiltration and operator communication. The malware loads embedded bot tokens and chat IDs from its internal resources (such as res/raw/Loda or assets/index.html) and uses the Telegram Bot API to send messages directly to attacker-controlled channels. Through this mechanism, stolen data is transmitted in real time, allowing operators to receive detailed reports that include device information, intercepted SMS messages, and summaries of victim activity.</p><h3 id="firebase-%E2%80%94-remote-command-and-control">Firebase — remote command and control</h3><p>At the same time, the malware connects to Firebase Realtime Database, which functions as a live command-and-control panel. Through this channel, attackers can remotely interact with the infected device, forwarding incoming SMS messages to their own numbers, sending messages from the victim’s phone, and enabling or disabling call forwarding. They can also push commands and update the malware’s behavior in real time. This setup turns the infected phone into a remotely managed device.</p><h2 id="why-this-is-especially-dangerous">Why this is especially dangerous</h2><p>What sets this malware family apart is how it combines multiple capabilities into a single attack chain:</p><ul><li><strong>WebView-based phishing</strong> to collect personal and identity data</li><li><strong>SMS interception</strong> to capture banking alerts and one-time passwords</li><li><strong>Call and message control</strong> to manipulate communications</li><li><strong>Cloud-based command systems</strong> for real-time attacker control</li></ul><p>In short, it operates as a <strong>hybrid phishing + SMS interception + call forwarding threat</strong>, specifically designed to target <strong>financial data, mobile banking activity, and user identity information</strong>.</p><h3 id="technical-indicators-malware-samples">Technical indicators (malware samples)</h3><p>Bitdefender Labs researcher <strong>Adina Mateescu </strong>also identified multiple Android malware samples associated with this campaign, including both <strong>droppers</strong> (initial installers) and the <strong>payload apps</strong> they deploy.</p><p><strong>Observed MD5 hashes:</strong></p><ul><li>Dropper: 014cda697a053d39f0504f626cb4aa4b<br>&nbsp;Dropped APK: bcc411c84a3ba169e1f989929115834f</li><li> Dropper: a2b03b138ec0a6263b73e17372063345<br>&nbsp;Dropped APK: 71bd06e73ad26ccf19cbc59c02630f14</li><li>Dropper: 25604ed81bc7b603897e390c3c7920e0<br>&nbsp;Dropped APK: 116b2280004a9d313a30acb767b1a85e</li><li>Dropper: 3711004997a5efc09e3eefab8b2fa91f<br>&nbsp;Dropped APK: fdd449fc92ea92b8a5b5478b56c56ecd</li><li>Dropper: 3c45b541cb7f29bb60a83564850e775a<br>&nbsp;Dropped APK: ce83808d52dcfff0c6f96ee5874fd841</li></ul><p>Most of the dropped applications use package names designed to look legitimate, such as <strong>“com.uptodown,”</strong> while others mimic popular apps like <strong>“com.baidu.searchbox.lite”</strong>.</p><h1 id="what-should-you-watch-out-for">What should you watch out for?</h1><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card"><img src="https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/labs/content/images/2026/04/Fake-toll-text-red-flags-1.jpg" class="kg-image" alt="" loading="lazy" width="1080" height="1280" srcset="https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/labs/content/images/size/w600/2026/04/Fake-toll-text-red-flags-1.jpg 600w, https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/labs/content/images/size/w1000/2026/04/Fake-toll-text-red-flags-1.jpg 1000w, https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/labs/content/images/2026/04/Fake-toll-text-red-flags-1.jpg 1080w" sizes="(min-width: 720px) 720px"></figure><p>If you receive a message about a toll or parking fine, take a moment to think about the following:</p><ul><li>Did you expect this message?</li><li>Is there pressure to act immediately?</li><li>Does the link look unusual or unfamiliar?</li><li>Are you being asked to reply before accessing the link?</li><li><strong>Does the message mention a “35% late fee”?</strong><br>This exact phrase has been identified across multiple scam campaigns and serves as a serious red flag.</li></ul><p>Legitimate authorities <strong>rarely request payments through unsolicited text messages with embedded links</strong>.</p><h1 id="these-scams-aren%E2%80%99t-new-but-they%E2%80%99re-getting-worse">These scams aren’t new, but they’re getting worse</h1><p>Scam texts about unpaid tolls or fines have been circulating for years, but their scale is growing rapidly. Authorities across the US have issued multiple warnings, highlighting the extent of the problem. The FCC has received complaints about messages impersonating toll services such as E-ZPass, FasTrak, and I-PASS, while the FBI recorded 59,271 toll scam complaints in 2024 alone. At the same time, the Federal Trade Commission reports that consumers lost $470 million to text-based scams in 2024, a fivefold increase compared to 2020. According to the FTC, these types of messages, including toll-related scams, are now among the most common forms of fraud.</p><h1 id="what-to-do-if-you-receive-one">What to do if you receive one</h1><ul><li>Do not click the link</li><li>Do not reply to the message</li><li>Verify the information directly on the official website</li><li>Delete and report the message</li></ul><p>If you’re unsure, you can safely check suspicious links or messages using tools like<br><a href="https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/consumer/link-checker">Bitdefender Link Checker</a> or <a href="https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/consumer/scamio">Bitdefender Scamio</a>, which can help you assess whether something is a scam without putting your data at risk.</p><h2 id="why-mobile-protection-matters-more-than-ever">Why mobile protection matters more than ever</h2><p>Because these attacks are designed specifically for smartphones, having protection directly on your device is essential.</p><p>A mobile security solution can help reduce the risk by blocking malicious links before they load, detecting scam patterns in messages and websites, and flagging suspicious activity even when links are shortened or disguised. But with campaigns like these constantly evolving, protection isn’t just about reacting to threats — it’s also about recognizing them early.</p><p><a href="https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/consumer/mobile-security-android" rel="noreferrer">Bitdefender Mobile Security</a> includes <a href="https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/scam-radar-bitdefender" rel="noreferrer"><strong>Scam Radar</strong></a>, a feature built into its Scam Protection layer that focuses on early warning. Instead of waiting for a threat to reach your device, Scam Radar monitors emerging scam campaigns and alerts you when they start spreading, including those targeting your region.</p><p>It also provides real examples of scam messages, highlights which brands or authorities are being impersonated, and offers clear guidance on what to watch out for. This kind of context is especially useful for scams involving tolls or parking fines, where familiarity and urgency are key tactics.</p><p>Knowing that a scam is already circulating (and what it looks like) makes it much easier to recognize and avoid before you click.</p></div> <div><!----> <div><p class="tw-py-4 tw-pl-4 tw-font-bold tw-uppercase">tags</p></div> <!----></div> <div class="tw-mb-2.5 tw-flex tw-flex-wrap tw-items-center tw-text-xs tw-font-bold tw-uppercase" style="color:#006DFF;"><a href="/en-us/blog/labs/tag/scam-research" title="Scam Research" class="tw-mr-4 tw-mb-4 tw-border tw-border-[#eff0f1] tw-bg-white tw-p-4 tw-text-xs tw-font-normal tw-lowercase tw-text-[#595959] hover:tw-bg-[#d4d7d9]">Scam Research
